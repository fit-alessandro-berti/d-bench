In Isaiah 55:6–7, repentance is presented as a total surrender—not merely a cessation of bad behavior, but a reorientation of the entire person. The pairing of **“way”** (Hebrew: *derek*) and **“thoughts”** (*mahshavah*) reveals that biblical repentance operates on two levels: the visible path of life and the invisible realm of desire and imagination.

### 1. The “Way” (*derek*): Outward Conduct
The *derek* is the path one habitually walks—the accumulated pattern of choices, actions, and relationships that constitute a life. To “forsake” the way is to stop walking that path. It involves concrete, observable repentance: ceasing the sinful practice, breaking the cycle of behavior, and turning from the outward form of wickedness.

Yet Scripture consistently warns that external reform without internal change is insufficient (Isaiah 1:16–17; Jeremiah 6:19–20). The “way” is the fruit; the “thoughts” are often the root.

### 2. The “Thoughts” (*mahshavah*): Inward Imagination
*Mahshavah* refers not merely to fleeting ideas, but to settled intentions, plans, and the imaginative life—the internal narratives we construct about ourselves, others, and God. In Hebrew anthropology, the “heart” (which encompasses thought and imagination) is the wellspring of action (Proverbs 4:23; Mark 7:21–22). Sin is conceived in the imagination before it is committed in deed.

To forsake one’s *mahshavah* is therefore to renounce the inner posture of rebellion—the prideful, self-exalting, God-rejecting fantasies that generate the wicked way. It is an act of mental and volitional repentance: “I no longer want what I used to want; I no longer plan my life as though God were absent.”

### 3. The Implication: Sin Is Holistic, So Mercy Must Be Comprehensive
This dual call implies that **sin is not merely a list of infractions but a condition of the whole person**—a wrong path sustained by wrong desires. Consequently, mercy cannot be merely transactional (paying a penalty for outward acts); it must be **transformational and abundant**.

When Isaiah promises that God “will abundantly pardon” (literally, *multiply* to forgive, *yarbah le-salleach*), he announces a mercy that reaches both the deed and the desire, the public action and the private imagination. God pardons not only what we have done, but the sinful self that conceived and delighted in it.

### 4. The Possibility of Mercy
This is why the passage insists on urgency: “Seek ye the Lord while he may be found.” Mercy is available precisely because God looks upon the whole person—the contrite heart that abandons both the wicked path and the unrighteous thoughts (Psalm 51:17). The “abundant” pardon is abundant because it forgives the totality of human rebellion, offering not just a clean record but a renewed mind (Romans 12:2) and a new way of walking.

In short, Isaiah presents repentance as the abandonment of a comprehensive self—outer and inner—and mercy as God’s comprehensive answer: a pardon that reaches the imagination where sin was first conceived, and the conduct where it was enacted.
