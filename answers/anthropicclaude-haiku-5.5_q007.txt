Luke 6:37 does not say moral judgments never matter. Several things in the text and its context show this.

**1. The verse presupposes moral assessment.** It pairs "condemn not" with "forgive," and forgiveness only makes sense if a wrong has been recognized as a wrong. You cannot forgive what you have not first judged to be an offense. The verse assumes that people do wrong and that wrongs are real. What it regulates is how we hold that recognition.

**2. The surrounding teaching requires discernment.** Jesus goes on in the same passage: "Can the blind lead the blind?" (6:39), and then, "By their fruits ye shall know them" (6:44). Elsewhere he says, "Judge not according to appearance, but judge righteous judgment" (John 7:24), warns against false prophets (Matthew 7:15), and gives a process for confronting a sinning brother (Matthew 18:15–17). Paul tells the Corinthians to judge those inside the community (1 Corinthians 5:12). A text that forbids all judgment cannot be squared with this. The prohibition must be narrower.

**3. The distinction lies in what is being judged and how.** Several contrasts help:

- **Acts vs. persons.** Discernment asks, "Was this action right?" The condemning spirit moves to "Is this person worthless, beyond repair, one of the bad ones?"
- **Provisional vs. final.** Discernment is a working judgment that stays open to new evidence and to repentance. Condemnation is a sentence, a closed verdict. The verse's language of being "condemned" echoes the final judgment, which belongs to God. The same pattern appears in Romans 14:4: "Who art thou that judgest another man's servant?"
- **Measured by oneself or not.** Verses 41–42 frame the matter as the speck and the beam. Discernment begins with self-examination. The condemning spirit looks outward and is blind to its own faults.
- **Aimed at repair or at distance.** Discernment wants the wrong to stop and the person restored (Galatians 6:1 speaks of restoring a fallen brother "in the spirit of meekness"). Condemnation is satisfied by the separation itself.
- **Standing.** Discernment is proportionate to one's role: a parent, a judge, an elder, a friend who is asked for counsel. Condemnation takes on the role of universal prosecutor.

**4. The "measure" clause explains the stakes.** "With what measure ye mete, it shall be measured to you" (6:38) suggests that the standard you apply to others is the one you will live under. This is a warning about standards, not a ban on them. Someone who applies a rigorous, merciful, self-inclusive standard is in a different position from someone who applies a harsh standard to others and a lenient one to self.

**5. The appetite to condemn is a particular disorder.** Its signs include:

- Pleasure or relief at another's failure
- Verdicts on a person's whole worth based on one act
- Sorting people into permanent categories
- Lack of grief over the wrong, which is replaced by a sense of superiority
- Indifference to one's own sins
- Shutting the door on repentance, so that no path back is left open

Discernment often feels heavy. It grieves, it hopes for change, and it is willing to be wrong about the person while still being clear about the act. The condemning spirit feels light and righteous, which is part of why it is dangerous.

**6. Where the line falls in practice.** Some questions help:

- Am I judging a conduct or a person's standing before God?
- Would I hold myself to the same standard, and do I?
- Am I grieved by this wrong, or gratified?
- Does my judgment have a path toward repair, or is it final?
- Do I have a role that calls for this judgment?

So the verse is not a rejection of moral judgment. It is a correction to the way moral judgment is usually carried out: from a position of self-righteousness, toward persons rather than acts, as a final verdict rather than a provisional one, and without the mercy that the verse itself says we will need. Judgment matters. The question is whether we exercise it as those who have also been forgiven.
