The sharpness of the passage comes from several converging rhetorical forces, and its logic is more careful than a simple "don't judge others."

**What makes the image so sharp**

*The scale of the hyperbole.* A "mote" is a speck of dust or chaff — barely visible. A "beam" is a heavy timber, the kind used in roof construction. The disproportion is deliberately absurd. Jesus doesn't say "your brother has a small flaw and you have a bigger one"; he says your brother has a speck and you have a *plank*. The exaggeration is almost cartoonish, and that's the point — it makes the hypocrisy visible as ridiculous rather than merely wrong.

*The physical impossibility.* The image isn't just disproportionate; it's geometrically absurd. A person with a beam lodged in their eye cannot see another person's eye at all, let alone inspect it closely. The flaw isn't merely embarrassing — it *structurally blocks* the very act the hypocrite is attempting. This gives the metaphor an epistemic layer: sin here isn't just a moral stain, it's a form of blindness. The beam doesn't coexist with bad judgment; it *causes* it.

*The asymmetry of the verbs.* "Beholdest" suggests fixed, scrutinizing attention — the hypocrite studies his brother's speck with a magnifying gaze. "Considerest not" suggests he hasn't even glanced at his own plank. The grammar itself dramatizes selective attention: intense perception directed outward, none directed inward.

*The word "hypocrite."* In Greek, *hypokritēs* is a stage actor. The charge isn't merely "you're a sinner" but "you're performing a role" — playing the part of the morally perceptive helper while the set-piece lodged in your face contradicts the script.

**How it permits correction**

The crucial move is in the final clause, which is often overlooked: "and *then shalt thou see clearly* to cast out the mote out of thy brother's eye."

The mote still gets removed. Jesus doesn't conclude "therefore leave your brother alone" — he concludes "therefore deal with the beam *first*." Three things follow:

1. **Sequence, not prohibition.** Moral correction is legitimate; it's the *order of operations* that's wrong. Self-examination precedes other-examination.

2. **Self-correction is instrumental, not just penitential.** Casting out the beam isn't only about humility — it's what makes you *see clearly*. Only someone who has confronted their own fault has the perceptual accuracy to help another. The passage implies that the hypocrite, if he succeeded in removing the speck, would do it badly — clumsily, harshly, blind — because unexamined sin distorts judgment.

3. **The correction retains its purpose.** The brother's mote is real and presumably still worth removing. The passage never denies that the flaw exists; it denies the critic's standing and competence until he has done the same surgery on himself.

So the passage criticizes hypocrisy not as *the act of judging* but as *judging from a position of pretended purity and impaired sight*. It replaces "don't correct anyone" with something far more demanding: correct yourself first, and then correct your brother — now able to see him as a fellow patient rather than a specimen.
