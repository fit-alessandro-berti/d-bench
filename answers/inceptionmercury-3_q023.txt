Isaiah 55:6–7 gives one of the most complete pictures of repentance in the Old Testament:

> “Let the wicked forsake his way, and the unrighteous man his thoughts: and let him return unto the Lord …”

The pairing of **“way”** and **“thoughts”** is not accidental. It is a deliberate description of the whole person.

## The “way” is outward conduct
In Hebrew, “way” (*derek*) means a road, path, or course of life. It refers to habits, actions, relationships, and practical behavior. The wicked person’s “way” is the visible path of sin: injustice, violence, deceit, idolatry, cruelty, or any life that opposes God’s commands.

So repentance must include a change of **outward conduct**. There must be an actual departure from sinful behavior. Not merely sorrow over it, but a decisive “forsaking” of it.

## The “thoughts” are inward imagination
“Thoughts” (*machashavot*) are deeper than actions. They are the inner world: intentions, plans, desires, schemes, and imagination. In Scripture, the “thoughts of the heart” are the root from which actions grow. A person can appear to change his behavior while still cultivating sinful thoughts: bitterness, lust, pride, greed, self-justification, resentment, or fantasies of evil.

The unrighteous person’s “thoughts” are the hidden source of his “way.” Therefore, repentance must reach **into the mind and heart**. It is not enough to stop doing bad things; one must also stop cherishing them inwardly.

## Why both?
Because human beings are not divided into a body and a mind that can be separately redeemed. Our actions shape our thoughts, and our thoughts shape our actions. Jesus says the heart is the place where evil originates: “out of the heart come evil thoughts, murder, adultery, sexual immorality, theft, false witness, slander” (Matthew 15:19). The “way” is the fruit; the “thoughts” are the root.

So biblical repentance is **a total turning**: outward behavior changes because the inner imagination has changed; inner thoughts change because they are being surrendered to God. If only the outward behavior changes, it is hypocrisy. If only the thoughts change, it is mere sentiment. Both must turn together, because sin has corrupted both.

## What this implies about mercy
God calls the wicked and the unrighteous to return to Him. This command carries a promise: “he will have mercy upon him; and to our God, for he will abundantly pardon.”

The word “abundantly pardon” is striking. In Hebrew, it literally means **“he will multiply to forgive.”** God’s pardon is not stingy or reluctant. It is rich, repeated, and generous. When a person truly turns from both deeds and desires, they meet a God who is eager to show compassion.

But notice the order: mercy is promised **after** turning. It is not a cheap grace that leaves the sinner in his way and his thoughts. The very promise of abundant pardon is the reason repentance is possible: God is near, He can be found, and He is ready to forgive.

Still, the text says “while he may be found” and “while he is near.” This suggests urgency. There is a season of opportunity, a time when God’s grace is genuinely available. We dare not presume that we can delay repentance forever. Yet for any who do turn, the mercy is real and complete.

## Living implication
Isaiah 55 calls us to a repentance that is both **conversion of life** and **conversion of mind**:

- Forsake the way of sin in concrete choices.
- Forsake the thoughts of sin in the inner imagination.
- Return to the Lord—not to an abstract system, but to God Himself.
- Trust that He will pardon abundantly, even when our sin seems overwhelming.

This is the gospel before the gospel: repentance is a turning of the whole person to a merciful God, and that God is always ready to forgive those who come.
