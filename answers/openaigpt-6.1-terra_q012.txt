The passage is **both morally serious and realistic about self-deception**. Jeremiah 17:9 warns that the heart—our inner life of desire, thought, and intention—is not transparent even to ourselves. We can sincerely believe our motives are good while overlooking pride, fear, or self-interest.

But the point is not simply that humans are as bad as possible. Verse 10 answers “Who can know it?” with the Lord’s searching and judging. In the surrounding passage, Jeremiah contrasts trusting in human strength with trusting in God (17:5–8). The concern is therefore not just inaccurate self-perception, but where we place our trust and how that shapes our conduct.

There is also a translation nuance: “desperately wicked” in the KJV is rendered in some translations as “desperately sick” or “beyond cure.” Either way, the image stresses a deep disorder, not merely an occasional mistake.

If the heart is an unreliable witness, we can still talk about motives—but with **humility rather than certainty**:

- Say, “I think I wanted to help, but I may also have wanted approval.”
- Pay attention to patterns and consequences, not just declared intentions.
- Invite honest feedback and, for a believer, ask God to reveal what is hidden.
- Be especially cautious about claiming to know someone else’s inner motives.

The passage calls for neither self-confidence without examination nor endless suspicion of every desire. It encourages honest reflection, accountability, and openness to correction.
