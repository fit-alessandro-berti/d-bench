In Matthew 6:1, the danger is not visibility, but intentionality — not *being* seen, but *wanting* to be seen.

This comes out clearly in the grammar and context:

> "Take heed that ye do not your alms before men, **to be seen of them**..."

The KJV translates the Greek *pros to theathenai autois* — literally "with a view toward being beheld by them." It's a purpose clause. The verse does not say "do not do good where people can see you." It says "do not do good *in order that* people will see you."

There are two related manuscript readings here — some manuscripts have *eleemosyne* (alms/charity), others *dikaiosyne* (righteousness/good deeds in general) — but the principle is the same in either case. Matthew 6 then gives three examples: almsgiving [6:2-4], prayer [6:5-6], and fasting [6:7-18].

### 1. What makes it "morally dangerous"?

According to the logic of the passage, public goodness becomes dangerous when it changes the audience:

1.  **It reorients the reward.** Verse 1 sets up a contrast: "otherwise ye have no reward of your Father which is in heaven." In verses 2, 5, and 16, Jesus repeats the refrain: those who trumpet their goodness "have their reward" — human applause — and therefore forfeit any other.
    The act is not condemned as useless to others. It is described as spiritually empty for the doer because it has already achieved its intended goal: status.

2.  **It instrumentalizes virtue.** The person is no longer giving to help the poor, praying to commune with God, or fasting for discipline. They are using the poor, the prayer, and the fast as props to construct an image. The moral action becomes a means to self-promotion.

3.  **It corrupts character silently.** This is why the verb at the start is "take heed" — *prosechete* — watch, be on guard. The desire for approval can co-opt even genuinely good impulses without the person noticing.

This is a classic theme in virtue ethics, not only Christian: if you do the right thing for the wrong reason — for vanity, reputation, or leverage — you train yourself in vanity, even as you do outward good.

### 2. Is visibility itself the problem?

Not in the context of Matthew.

If all visibility were forbidden, it would contradict:

*   The immediate literary context. Jesus assumes alms, prayer, and fasting will continue — he says "when thou doest alms" and "when thou prayest," not "if."
*   Other teachings attributed to Jesus in the same Gospel: "Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works..." — Matthew 5:16. There, public goodness is *commanded*, provided its purpose is that people glorify God, not the doer.

Interpreters across most Christian traditions have understood this distinction in the same way:

Public goodness is unavoidable — and sometimes required for justice, leadership, or example. What Matthew 6:1 warns against is *performative* goodness, where visibility stops being a circumstance and becomes the motive.

In modern terms, the verse distinguishes between:
*   **Being seen** while doing good — a matter of fact.
*   **Doing good to be seen** — a matter of the heart.

Only the second is called dangerous here.
