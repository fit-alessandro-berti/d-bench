## A short answer first

The text treats bitterness as **none of the four things alone, but as all of them in a particular hierarchy.** It is a *settled disposition* (a posture), *embodied in speech and action* (a habit), *rooted in a grievance you keep alive* (a choice), and — this is the part the passage's neighbours insist on — *a root-like, spreading condition* (contagious). And the forgiveness in v. 32 is not the opposite *feeling*; it is a **habit and posture of releasing** — the practice that keeps the root from taking hold.

Placing them side by side is not a psychological suggestion about matched emotions. It is an **antinomy**: two incompatible ways of being related to God and to other people. You cannot occupy both.

---

## 1. Where the pair comes from, and what shape the sentence has

Ephesians 4:31–32 is a close reproduction of Colossians 3:12–13, reshaped:

- **Col 3:12:** "Put off anger... and put on compassion, kindness, humility, gentleness, patience."
- **Eph 4:32:** "But *be* (γίνεσθε) kind one to another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, even as God in Christ hath forgiven you."

Two things are significant in the Ephesian form.

First, the **"put off / put on" language is baptismal**. Paul has already used it in the same paragraph for the old humanity (4:22: ἀποθέσθαι, "strip off"). So "let all bitterness... be put away from you" is not a mood; it is a *garment* being taken off a body that is being re-dressed into a new identity. Clothes are worn; they are not visited by the soul in passing.

Second, Ephesians replaces "put on" with the **present imperative γίνεσθε — "keep on becoming."** Becoming is not a decision; it is a formation. Paul is describing a shape a community is being moulded into over time, and the participial string "kind... tenderhearted... forgiving" describes the *modes* by which that becoming proceeds.

Then note the two-part architecture of vv. 31–32: **a clearing, then a filling.** All the negatives in v. 31, all the positives in v. 32. Paul is not satisfied with the absence of vice; he names the specific goodness that ought to occupy the vacated space.

---

## 2. Why these particular vices? The list is not arbitrary

Look at the words: *bitterness, wrath, anger, clamour, evil speaking, malice.* This is a recognisable cluster in the biblical wisdom and prophetic literature, and it is consistently **thrown outward**:

- **κραυγή ("clamour")** is the roar of the brawl, the lawsuit in the gate, the public outcry — the noise of a feud in progress (cf. Ps 41:7 LXX, where the enemy is a "Canaanite" who cries out against the psalmist; the φιλαίδες / "Canaanite" idiom).
- **βλασφημία ("evil speaking")** is slander, the false report, the tongue set loose (Ps 57:4 LXX).
- **πονηρία ("malice")** is ill-will as a settled moral condition — and in Ephesians it is the *addition* to the Colossian list, which is telling: Ephesians adds the deep ill-will to the surface behaviours.

So the list moves from an inner state (bitterness) through hot feelings (wrath, anger) to the noisy, public, verbal and legal expressions of a grudge. Two consequences follow:

**(a) The feeling is not the target.** Paul has already said, four verses earlier, "be angry, and sin not; let not the sun go down upon your wrath" (4:26). Anger as a felt heat is permitted and even commanded to be *managed*. What is condemned here is what anger *becomes* when it is allowed to settle into a disposition and then into a pattern of speech and action. The feeling is the smoke; the text is aimed at the fire and the burning building.

**(b) Bitterness is a *state*, not an episode.** The Bible's own idiom for it is botanical and pathological. Hebrews 12:15 — the verse most directly illuminating Ephesians here — warns against allowing "a root of bitterness springing up to trouble you, **whereby many be defiled**" (cf. Deut 29:18). The same metaphor governs Hebrews' view of the heart: "I will be merciful to their iniquities, and **their sins will I remember no more**" (8:10), and "I will put my law within them, and write it in their hearts" (Jer 31:33). In Hebrews' own symbolism the heart is the surface on which the *indictment* was written; forgiveness is the *erasure of that record*; and a "root of bitterness" is a fresh, self-propagating indictment growing where the record used to be, spoiling the whole field.

---

## 3. So: feeling, habit, posture, or contagion? — all four, prioritised

| | Verdict |
|---|---|
| **Feeling** | Partly in view, but not the aim. Wrath and anger are admitted; the sin is their sediment. Paul never commands the absence of a feeling — he commands the absence of a *garment* and the presence of a *practice*. |
| **Habit** | Strongly yes. The clustering with speech ("clamour," "evil speaking"), the habitual imperatives, the "keep on becoming." |
| **Chosen posture** | Yes, and decisively. Bitterness in this Bible is *grounded* — it grows from a specific grievance you continue to hold and feed. It is a standing stance toward a person, and a standing stance can be un-stood. |
| **Contagious** | Yes, and this is the reason Paul writes it this way for a *community*. See below. |

---

## 4. The contagion point: why "one to another"

Heb 12:15 says a root of bitterness defiles **"many."** That is the logic. James makes the chain visible: "where jealousy and strife are, there are disturbances and every evil deed" (3:16), tracing wars and conflicts to desires warring inside (4:1–2); and James uses the very same verb as Ephesians for the remedy — "let all filthiness and superfluity of malice **be put away**" (1:21: ἀποθέσθαι πᾶσαν κηλίδα καὶ περισσείαν κακίας), with the antidote being "the implanted word, able to save your souls" received "with meekness." Add the leaven image (1 Cor 5:6: "a little leaven leaveneth the whole lump") and the conventional wisdom that a "soft answer turneth away wrath" but "grievous words stir up anger" (Prov 15:1), and the case is made: **the human tongue is a shared environment.** What one person says, the whole assembly absorbs.

This is why Paul moves so fast from the individual to the corporate: "**one to another**... **one to another**... **one to another**" (εἰς ἀλλήλους, thrice, in v. 32), and why the letter flows onward into 5:1, "be imitators of God, as beloved children." Forgiveness is not a private interior event; it is a *public practice with a corporate yield*.

Note also the paradox at the heart of it: bitterness is contagious and so is love. If a grudge spreads, so does kindness; the commands in v. 32 are not "add *also* be nice" — they are the *competitive* alternative by which the leaven of the gospel displaces the leaven of resentment.

---

## 5. Why forgiveness is placed *directly* opposite

Because they are the two ends of one beam.

- **Bitterness keeps the account open.** It is the refusal to release a claim; it stores the injury and re-activates it. In the Bible's commercial and juridical idiom, sins are debts, and unforgiveness is the refusal to cancel a debt against someone who has already been forgiven by God.
- **Forgiveness closes the account.** ἄφεσις and ἀφιέναι in Greek are remission language; forgiving is cancelling a debt. And Ephesians 4:26 sets the rhythm: "let not the sun go down upon your wrath" — a daily settling of accounts.

Paul's pair is thus not "don't feel cross / feel nice." It is **"stop prosecuting; start releasing"** — one entire forensic stance replaced by another.

Three further features of the pairing matter.

**(i) It is grounded in what God has already done.** "Even as God in Christ hath forgiven you" (the KJV's "for Christ's sake," κατὰ Χριστοῦ, is an alternative reading to "in Christ"; cf. Eph 1:7; 2:4–5) — the perfect tense, the *prior* act. Human forgiveness here is not produced by technique, willpower, or the offender's merit; it is imitation of a completed divine act. The clause is a *ground*, not a *technique*.

**(ii) It is christological at bottom.** Notice that Ephesians 4:32 has quietly absorbed Colossians 3:13, where the ground is "as Christ also forgave you." Divine forgiveness is *located* in Christ; Christ's forgiveness is the pattern; the community's is the likeness. Against a Christ who was "made obedient unto the death" (Phil 2:8) and who from the cross cried "Father, forgive them" (Luke 23:34), there is no place for the prosecuting stance. The alternative to bitterness is not "niceness"; it is *conformation to Christ* who "forgave you" (Col 3:13).

**(iii) It flows from the Spirit's indwelling.** Ephesians 4 is one continuous movement: Christ dwells in the heart (3:16–17) → the heart is the Spirit's temple, so speech matters, "grieve not the Holy Spirit" (4:30) → therefore bitterness must be stripped off (4:31) → therefore be tender and forgiving (4:32) → "walk in love" (5:2). The passage is *formation*, not a coping strategy. It presumes the Spirit does the work (Phil 2:13) while calling for the human practice that the Spirit works through.

---

## 6. What the verse does and does not ask for

A few honest observations on how to live this text:

- **It commands a stance, not a temperature.** You may feel resentment while you do not feed it, speak it, and keep it. Psalm 42's question — "O my soul, why art thou disquieted within me?" — is answered with an act of trust, not a forced emotion. The command is "slow to anger, quick to the good word" (James 1:19–20; Prov 15:1), and "say not I will recompense evil; wait on the Lord" (Prov 20:24; Rom 12:19).
- **Forgiveness here is release, not denial.** It does not mean the sin did not happen, and the text says nothing about trust, restitution, or restored access. Practical, pastoral counsel commonly distinguishes forgiveness from reconciliation and from consequences — rightly. But the text *does* require the settled disposition, not merely an absence of hostility.
- **The order is significant.** "Let all bitterness be put away" is a clearing operation, and a cleared house needs to be furnished. A community that only manages to stop being nasty has met the command; one that becomes "kind, tenderhearted, forgiving" has met the whole verse.

---

## Bottom line

Paul places bitterness against forgiveness because they are **two incompatible moral orientations, each with a lifestyle attached**: the prosecuting posture that keeps a grievance alive, root-like and leaven-like in its spread; and the releasing posture that cancels the debt, modelled on Christ's own forgiveness and therefore on God's. The feeling of anger is not the target — Paul permits anger and forbids its *sunset*. The garment is the target, and the garment is worn, chosen, seeded in a root, and contagious. Hence the exposure, not the introspection: **the answer to bitterness in a church is not better anger management but a different, deliberately practiced way of being with one another.**
