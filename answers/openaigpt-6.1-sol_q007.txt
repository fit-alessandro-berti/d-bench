Jesus is not saying that moral distinctions disappear. He is challenging the spirit in which we make them—and especially our readiness to turn a judgment about someone’s conduct into a verdict on the whole person.

The difference is between **discerning what is wrong** and **wanting someone to stand condemned**.

### Discernment remains necessary

The surrounding passage assumes that people must distinguish good from evil. Jesus speaks of a tree being known by its fruit (Luke 6:43–45). He also warns about the blind leading the blind and about trying to remove a speck from another person’s eye while ignoring the beam in one’s own.

These images would make little sense if all moral judgment were forbidden. Some fruit is bad; some guidance is dangerous; some conduct needs correction. But the beam-and-speck image changes the order of correction: examine yourself first, so that you can see clearly enough to help another.

Discernment asks:

- What actually happened?
- What harm was done?
- What needs to be protected or repaired?
- What do I not know?
- Am I applying the same standard to myself?

It can lead to firm action: confronting abuse, setting boundaries, refusing dishonesty, or imposing proportionate consequences. Mercy does not require pretending that harm is harmless.

### The appetite to condemn wants more than truth

Condemnation, in the sense challenged here, is not merely recognizing wrongdoing. It is the desire to place another person beneath us—to make their fault the final truth about them.

Its questions are different: *How can I expose them? How can I prove that I am better? How can I ensure they never escape this verdict?*

The outward conclusion may even be accurate: someone really did lie, betray, or injure another. Yet a correct observation can become the instrument of pride, revenge, or pleasure in another’s disgrace. **Being right about the wrong does not necessarily make our response righteous.**

Discernment seeks truth for the sake of protection, correction, and repair. The appetite to condemn seeks a guilty person for the sake of superiority or retaliation.

### Forgiveness is not moral indifference

“Forgive, and ye shall be forgiven” makes this especially clear. Forgiveness presupposes that something genuinely wrong has occurred. If nothing matters morally, there is nothing to forgive.

Nor does forgiveness necessarily mean restored trust, immediate reconciliation, or freedom from consequences. One can relinquish revenge while still requiring accountability. One can refuse to treat an offender as beyond redemption while protecting those they might harm.

The verse sits within Jesus’ call to mercy: “Be ye therefore merciful, as your Father also is merciful” (Luke 6:36). Its aim is not a world without moral seriousness, but moral seriousness without self-righteous cruelty.

A useful test is this: **Do I want the wrong to be put right, or do I want the wrongdoer to remain disgraced?**

Discernment can say, “This was wrong, and it must stop.” Mercy adds, “But this wrong is not mine to turn into the whole and final meaning of your life.”
