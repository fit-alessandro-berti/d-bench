## The Shift in the Verbs

The whole psalm turns on a change of vocabulary at verse 10, and it is worth noticing how long the change takes to arrive.

The first nine verses are all about removal. *Blot out* my transgressions. *Wash* me. *Purge* me with hyssop. *Hide thy face* from my sins. These are the verbs of laundry and erasure — of a stain lifted, a record expunged, a debt cancelled. They assume that the problem is something attached to him which could be detached.

Then at verse 10 the verb changes to *bara'* — "create." It is the word from the first line of Genesis, and in Hebrew scripture it takes only one subject. Men make, form, build, repair; God alone *creates*. David has stopped asking for the removal of something and started asking for the production of something that isn't there. This is not an intensified request for pardon. It is a different request, made after the first one has been exhausted.

The reason for the shift is stated in verse 5: "in sin did my mother conceive me." Whatever we make of that theologically, the psychological observation is exact. He has discovered, by watching himself, that the affair with Bathsheba and the killing of Uriah were not aberrations breaking in from outside. They came out of him. They were *his*, in the way that a man's handwriting is his. And this discovery ruins the logic of pardon-as-solution, because pardon addresses the deed and the deed was only a symptom. A man forgiven for adultery and murder, and otherwise left entirely intact, has been returned to precisely the self that committed adultery and murder. Tomorrow morning he will still be that man, standing on that roof.

## What the Desire for Escape Actually Wants

The wish to be rid of guilt and the wish to be changed feel similar from inside, because both are painful and both are directed at the same episode. But they want different things, and they can be told apart.

The desire for escape from guilt is oriented toward a *feeling*. What it finds intolerable is the sensation of being in the wrong, and so anything that ends the sensation will satisfy it. It can therefore be satisfied by a great many things that have nothing to do with righteousness: by successful minimization ("it was not as bad as it looks"), by comparison ("others have done worse"), by explanation ("I was under enormous pressure"), by the mere passage of time, by ritual correctly performed, by being reassured. It is fungible in its solutions because it has a single aim, which is relief.

Notice the peculiar cruelty this can produce. Someone who urgently needs to stop feeling guilty needs the person he injured to stop being injured. So he presses for forgiveness, hurries the reconciliation, becomes irritated at reminders, and experiences the other's continued pain as a kind of aggression against him. The wound has been converted into a problem about *his* comfort. This is what it looks like when guilt is treated as a debt to be discharged rather than a disease to be cured: once the debt is paid, the creditor has no further standing to complain.

The desire for inner change wants something that cannot be faked, and is therefore much harder to obtain and much less pleasant to pursue. It has to begin by accepting a diagnosis, which means giving up all the consolations listed above. Verse 6 puts it as starkly as it can be put: "thou desirest truth in the inward parts." Not performance, not contrition displayed, but accuracy about oneself in the place where no one is watching. That is a strange thing to ask for, because accuracy about oneself in that place is generally awful.

## The Fear in Verse 11

"Cast me not away from thy presence; and take not thy holy spirit from me."

David is not, I think, afraid of damnation here. He is afraid of something he has watched happen to a specific person. The Spirit departed from Saul, and Saul went on being king — kept the crown, kept the army, kept the court, and became hollow. He continued to function. He simply had no one home.

That is the ruin a person fears once he has understood that his problem is internal rather than forensic: not punishment, but the prospect of continuing on in office, in reputation, in the outward shape of a life, with the animating thing withdrawn. A pardon does nothing whatever about this danger. You can be entirely acquitted and entirely empty.

## Steadiness, Joy, and Freedom

The three things asked for in these verses are worth taking one at a time, because together they describe a whole condition rather than a fix.

*A right spirit* — Hebrew *ruach nakhon*, from the root meaning to establish or make firm. Not so much "correct" as *steady, fixed, reliable*. The failure being addressed was a failure of stability: a man whose settled convictions could be overturned by an accidental glance from a rooftop. He is not asking to be given better opinions. He is asking to be made into something that holds.

*The joy of thy salvation* — note that he asks for the *joy* of it to be restored, not the salvation. The relationship survived; the gladness in it did not. And joy is a revealing thing to request in this situation, because a man who merely wanted his guilt to stop would ask for numbness, and numbness is much easier to arrange. Numbness is what alcohol and distraction and busyness are for. Joy is not the absence of pain; it is a positive relation to something, and you cannot get it by subtraction.

*A free spirit* — *ruach nedivah*, from the root meaning to volunteer, the word behind the freewill offering. Willing, generous, unforced, even princely. This is the destination, and it explains everything that came before. He is not asking to be put under stricter guard. A heart under guard is still a heart that wants the wrong thing and is merely prevented. What he wants is to arrive at a condition in which the good is *desired* rather than merely required — obedience that is a gift rather than a levy. That is a request that pardon structurally cannot fulfill, because pardon operates on the record and this operates on the appetite.

## Two Honest Complications

First: the psalm does not despise pardon. It spends nine verses begging for it. The point is not that asking for forgiveness is a lesser or self-interested move — guilt is a real weight and relief from it is a real mercy. The point is that a request for pardon *alone* discloses what the petitioner thinks the problem is. Verses 1–9 are not a mistake corrected by verse 10; they are the necessary first half of something that would be incomplete if it stopped there.

Second: the desire for inner change has its own corruptions, and they are subtler. It can become perfectionism, or scrupulosity, or a spiritual ambition in which one's own sanctity is the project and God the contractor. "I want to be the sort of person who is good" is still, potentially, a sentence about oneself. The tell is whether the injured party has disappeared from view. Sin as stain-on-me and sin as my-transformation-project can both be ways of keeping the story about me.

Which is why the grammar of these three verses matters as much as the vocabulary. *Create* in me. *Renew* within me. *Cast* me not away. *Take* not from me. *Restore* unto me. *Uphold* me. Every verb has God as its subject and David as its object. He has stopped being the agent of his own repair, and this is not modesty or defeatism — it is a conclusion drawn from evidence. The faculty that would have to carry out the self-reform is the faculty under indictment. Whatever will do this cannot be him.

That is finally why "create" rather than "clean." Cleaning is something you can undertake. Creation you can only receive.
