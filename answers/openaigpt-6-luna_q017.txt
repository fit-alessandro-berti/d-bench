Light is hated because it does more than show what is there: it makes concealment harder. In John’s image, light is truth made visible—God’s presence exposing the difference between what a person is doing and what they may wish to believe about themselves.

Darkness can feel protective. It lets us manage appearances, avoid accountability, and keep painful truths at a distance. If someone’s deeds are “evil,” coming into the light may mean losing that control: being seen accurately, having excuses challenged, or facing consequences. Shame intensifies the impulse to hide. We may fear that if the truth about us is seen, we ourselves will be rejected.

But the passage also distinguishes exposure from rejection. In the surrounding verses, John says the Son is sent to save, not to condemn. The light reveals the need for rescue; it is not simply a spotlight meant to humiliate. The tragedy is that people can prefer hiding even when the light comes as an invitation to life.

To “do the truth” is therefore not to claim moral perfection. It is to live honestly before God—to let one’s actions be seen, including one’s need for change, and to acknowledge that what is good is “wrought in God.” The person who comes to the light trusts that truth can lead not only to conviction, but also to healing and transformation.

So light is hated when being seen seems more threatening than remaining unchanged. The passage asks whether we will protect ourselves from the truth, or trust the light enough to let it tell the truth about us—and then do its work.
