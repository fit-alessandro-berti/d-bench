The image is sharp because it is **physically impossible and grotesquely intimate**.

Jesus does not say, “You have a flaw too.” He says you are trying to perform eye surgery on your brother while a **wooden beam (δοκός, a heavy timber)** is lodged in your own eye, and the flaw you see is only a **tiny splinter of sawdust (κάρφος)** in his. The hyperbole is so extreme it becomes absurd: one cannot even *see* to remove a mote when a plank is blocking the organ of sight. The eye is the seat of perception; if it is blinded by one’s own massive, unacknowledged sin, then judgment is not just wrong—it is self-destructive comedy. The sharpness comes from this **reversal of roles**: the would-be corrector is not a wise physician but a blind fool, and the “brother” is not a villain but a partner in shared vulnerability. The rhetorical question forces the listener to visualize the scene and feel its humiliation.

---

### How it attacks hypocrisy *without* banning correction

The passage is not a prohibition against moral rebuke; it is a **reordering of operations**.

1. **The diagnosis is inconsistency, not correction itself.**  
   The “hypocrite” (ὑποκριτής, a stage actor) plays the part of a righteous judge while concealing a larger, interior corruption. The sin is not that he sees the mote; it is that he **ignores the beam** and uses the mote to deflect attention. Hypocrisy here is *unacknowledged self-deformation*—pretending to see clearly when one is blind.

2. **The text prescribes a sequence, not a silence.**  
   “*First* cast out the beam out of thine own eye; and *then* shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote out of thy brother’s eye.” The word *then* is crucial. Jesus does not say “leave your brother alone”; he says **correct the self first so that correction can be accurate, humble, and safe**. The goal is not to eliminate judgment but to **purify the judge**.

3. **The purpose is restored sight, not domination.**  
   Once the beam is removed, the corrected eye can “see clearly” (διαβλέψεις). That clarity is necessary to help another without crushing him. Moral correction is legitimate only when it is **other-centered and knowledgeable**, not a defense mechanism for one’s own pride. The passage therefore distinguishes between:
   - **Hypocritical judgment**: using another’s small fault to hide one’s massive one.
   - **Genuine correction**: after repentance and self-examination, assisting a brother with clear, unclouded vision.

In short, the beam-and-mote image is so sharp because it makes self-deception visibly ridiculous. And the command is so precise because it **forbids the pride that corrupts correction**, not the correction that love demands. The path is explicit: **heal your own blindness first, then you are actually able to help someone else see.**
