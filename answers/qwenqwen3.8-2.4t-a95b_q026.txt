

Matthew 12:34–35 connects speech and the heart by presenting the heart as a hidden treasury and the mouth as the place where that treasury overflows into public view.

### 1. Speech is connected to hidden moral stockpiles

Jesus says:

> “Out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaketh.”

The “heart” here is not merely emotion; in biblical language it is the center of thought, desire, intention, and moral character. The “abundance” is what fills it, what has accumulated there. So Jesus pictures the heart as a storehouse or treasury.

Then verse 35 makes that explicit:

> “A good man out of the good treasure of the heart bringeth forth good things: and an evil man out of the evil treasure bringeth forth evil things.”

The word “treasure” suggests something stored up over time. A person’s words do not come from nowhere. They arise from a hidden moral reserve: habits, desires, loyalties, attitudes, and dispositions that have been gathered within.

So speech is not just surface noise. It is an index to what a person values and what kind of person they have become.

---

### 2. Words are symptoms

Jesus first presents words as symptoms. A symptom is something visible or audible that reveals an underlying condition.

When Jesus calls them “O generation of vipers,” he is saying, in effect: your venomous speech shows your venomous nature. Their words are not merely isolated mistakes; they are evidence of a diseased moral condition.

This connects with the surrounding context. The Pharisees have just attributed Jesus’ Spirit-empowered works to Satan. Jesus responds that such speech reveals what is inside them. Their words are like a fever that exposes an infection.

In that sense, speech is diagnostic. It shows what the heart is full of.

---

### 3. Words are revelations

Jesus also presents words as revelations. Speech discloses what is hidden.

The heart’s “treasure” may be invisible to other people, but the mouth makes it known. What someone says reveals what they truly treasure: truth or falsehood, mercy or malice, God or self, goodness or evil.

This is why Jesus’ statement is so searching. He is not merely criticizing bad language. He is saying that human speech unveils the moral contents of the inner person. A person may conceal much, but eventually the mouth speaks from the abundance of the heart.

So words are revelatory. They make the hidden stockpile public.

---

### 4. Words are also choices

The passage strongly emphasizes that words flow from the heart, but it does not reduce words to involuntary reflexes. Jesus speaks with moral urgency and indictment:

> “How can ye, being evil, speak good things?”

That is not a gentle psychological observation. It is a rebuke. He holds them accountable. They are not excused because their words came from their hearts; they are condemned because their hearts are evil.

This becomes even clearer a few verses later, where Jesus says that people will give account for every careless word on the day of judgment:

> “For by thy words thou shalt be justified, and by thy words thou shalt be condemned.”  
> — Matthew 12:37

So words are morally responsible acts. They are chosen expressions of the person. The heart produces them, but the heart itself is not morally neutral. It is formed by prior choices, loyalties, and loves.

In other words, people are not merely victims of their inner treasury; they are stewards of it. What they allow to accumulate in the heart shapes what they choose to say.

---

### 5. So are words symptoms, choices, revelations, or all three?

Yes: all three, but in a particular order.

**Words are symptoms** because they reveal the condition of the heart.  
**Words are revelations** because they disclose the hidden treasure within.  
**Words are choices** because the speaker remains morally accountable for them.

The primary emphasis in Matthew 12:34–35 is diagnostic and revelatory: speech shows what is inside. But the context makes clear that Jesus is not describing mere psychological determinism. He is issuing a moral indictment. Their words reveal their heart, and their heart makes them guilty.

So the passage teaches that speech is both an overflow of character and a matter of responsibility. It is not only that people say bad things; it is that their bad words expose a bad treasure. And because that treasure is morally culpable, their words are not merely symptoms to be observed but sins or virtues to be judged.

In short: **words are the audible fruit of the heart’s hidden treasury. They reveal what we store up, display what we truly are, and expose us before God.**
