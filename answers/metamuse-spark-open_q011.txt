The move from "cease to do evil" to "learn to do well" in Isaiah 1:17 is deliberate - and it answers your question directly.

In the Hebrew, it's a shift from *chidlu hara* - stop, leave off evil - to *limdu hetev* - learn, train yourself to do good.

> *chadal* = to cease, to stop doing
> *lamad* = to learn, to practice, to become skilled through discipline

That second verb is key. Goodness is not presented as something instinctive or automatic once evil is removed. It has to be *learned* like an apprenticeship.

The structure of verses 16-17 makes this clear:

**1. Renunciation: wash, be clean, remove, cease.**
This is restraint - putting away evil "from before mine eyes." It ends corrupt worship and injustice that Isaiah has just condemned in 1:10-15, where ritual sacrifice without righteousness is rejected.

**2. Formation: learn to do well.**
Restraint clears the ground, but it doesn't by itself make someone good. The prophet says goodness must be actively cultivated.

**3. Practice: seek judgment, relieve the oppressed, judge the fatherless, plead for the widow.**
*Darshu mishpat* - seek justice.
*Ashru chamotz* - correct oppression / help the oppressed.
*Shifetu yatom* - defend the orphan.
*Rivu almanah* - plead the cause of the widow.

In ancient Israel, the orphan, widow, and oppressed were those with no power to defend themselves in court or society. So "learning to do well" is immediately defined not as private piety or mere politeness, but as practiced love and justice on behalf of the vulnerable.

In Jewish and Christian interpretation alike, this is often read as:

> Mere restraint = "I do no harm"
> Practiced goodness = "I learn to actively do what heals and restores"

Goodness here is not just abstinence from evil, but a skill, a habit, and a responsibility - a form of justice that has to be sought, studied, and done repeatedly until it becomes character.
