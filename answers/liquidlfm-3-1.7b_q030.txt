**Corinthians 13:4‑7 (KJV‑style)**  

> “Charity suffereth long, and is kind; charity envieth not; charity vaunteth not itself, is not puffed up,  
> Doth not behave itself unseemly, seeketh not her own, is not easily provoked, thinketh no evil;  
> Rejoiceth not in iniquity, but rejoiceth in the truth;  
> Beareth all things, believeth all things, hopeth all things, endureth all things.”

---

### 1.  Why does Paul list *so many* negations?

| Negated trait | What it means for love | Why it matters |
|---------------|----------------------|----------------|
| **Envy / covetousness** (“envieth not”) | Love does not desire what another has. | Prevents love from becoming a competition; it keeps the focus on God’s grace rather than personal gain. |
| **Vanity / pride** (“vaunteth not itself, is not puffed up”) | Love does not brag about its own worth. | Avoids the “self‑exaltation” that turns affection into a show‑off. |
| **Irritability / lack of composure** (“behave itself unseemly, not easily provoked”) | Love stays calm under pressure. | Guarantees reliability and peace even when circumstances clash. |
| **Suspicion / self‑interest** (“seeketh not her own”) | Love does not look out for its own safety or comfort. | Shows genuine concern for the other’s welfare. |
| **Sinfulness / judgment** (“rejoiceth not in iniquity”) | Joy is found only in truth, not in wrongdoing. | Keeps love aligned with God’s righteousness. |
| **Superficial boasting** (“believeth all things, hopeth all things”) | Full commitment to reality, not empty optimism. | Roots love in honest faith and perseverance. |

In short, **each negation carves out a space where the opposite impulse would destroy the very essence of love.** By saying “it does *not* …,” Paul defines love by what it *is* rather than what it *avoids*. The result is a picture of love that is **humble, pure, steady, and trustworthy**.

---

### 2.  What vision of strength is being proposed?

The portrait paints love as a **mature, resilient character** whose strength comes from **inner transformation and surrender**, not from external status, power, or personal satisfaction. Key elements of that strength include:

1. **Self‑control & patience** – “Suffereth long” and “endureth all things.” Love endures hardship without breaking.
2. **Humility & modesty** – No boasting, no seeking one’s own glory, no puffed‑up self‑image.
3. **Purity of heart** – Free from envy, suspicion, or jealousy; it “thinketh no evil.”
4. **Reliance on God** – Belief, hope, and endurance flow from trusting God and expecting His favor.
5. **Generosity & service** – Carrying “all things,” giving without demand, and rejoicing in truth rather than in sin.

Together, these traits form a **spiritual fortitude**: the ability to remain faithful and loving amid adversity, to give freely, and to keep one’s eyes fixed on the good rather than on rivals’ successes or one’s own security. This is the **“strength of the Spirit”**—a strength that is rooted in obedience to Christ, grounded in the gospel’s call to love the neighbor as oneself, and expressed through daily choices that reject pride, envy, and suspicion.

---

### 3.  Bottom line

Paul uses a series of **negative assertions** to draw a sharp boundary between healthy love and destructive self‑centeredness. By **declaring what love is *not***, he reveals what truly sustains it: **humility, purity, endurance, and reliance on God**. The resulting vision of strength is one of **inner integrity and steadfastness**—a love that endures “all things,” never seeks its own reward, and radiates joy in truth rather than in its own claims. This is the model of agape love that Christians are called to live out.
