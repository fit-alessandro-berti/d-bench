# The Moral Posture of Galatians 6:1

## The Required Posture: Meekness

The posture demanded is **meekness** (Greek: *prautēs*) — a word that does not mean weakness or timidity, but strength under control; power held in the service of gentleness. It is the same word used of Christ Himself: "I am meek and lowly in heart" (Matthew 11:29).

This is deliberate on Paul's part. The immediate context (Galatians 5:15, 26) warns against "biting and devouring one another" and "provoking and envying one another." Correction done in the flesh produces more flesh. Correction done in the Spirit must itself exhibit the fruit of the Spirit — and *prautēs* is listed among that fruit (5:23). The manner of the correction must match the goal of the correction.

## Why the Three Are Inseparable

**1. Restoration — the goal.**
"Restore" (*katartizō*) is a medical and practical term: used of setting broken bones and mending torn fishing nets. It is a word of repair, not punishment. The fallen brother is a patient to be healed, not an enemy to be defeated or a case to be tried. This immediately rules out every posture of triumph, contempt, or exclusion.

**2. Meekness — the manner.**
Why must the healer be meek? Because the healer and the patient stand in the same moral condition. The word "overtaken" suggests being caught unawares, surprised mid-course — implying that falling is not a remote possibility for anyone but a live one for everyone. The one who corrects is not a superior being; he is a fellow sinner who happens, at this moment, to be standing. Meekness is simply the honest recognition of that fact. It corrects *beside* the fallen, not *over* him.

**3. Self-suspicion — the safeguard.**
"Considering thyself" (*skopeō* — scrutinize, watch closely) is the check that keeps the corrector honest. And note the ambiguity of "lest thou also be tempted": the temptation is twofold —

- The obvious one: the same sin can overtake the corrector.
- The subtler one: the act of correcting is *itself* a temptation — the temptation of pride, of feeling morally above the fallen, of spiritual self-congratulation. The corrector can fall *in the very act of correcting*.

## The Logic of Their Unity

Each element guards against the corruption of the others:

- **Without restoration as the goal**, correction degenerates into punishment, gossip, or score-settling.
- **Without meekness as the manner**, even a right goal is corrupted — one cannot heal a wound with a harsh hand.
- **Without self-suspicion**, the corrector forgets his own frailty and becomes a Pharisee — standing above rather than beside, thanking God he is "not as other men."

The verse enacts a **radical solidarity in sin**: the difference between the corrector and the corrected is not moral superiority but only momentary position — and grace. "Brethren" frames the whole scene. The one doing the restoring is addressed not as a judge but as a brother who could, tomorrow, need the same restoration himself.

In short: the posture is that of a fellow patient performing surgery with a trembling hand — aiming to mend, gentled by the knowledge that his own bones are breakable too.
