The passage treats speech as the mouth drawing on a store already kept in the heart. Words are symptoms and revelations of that store, and, in the surrounding argument, accountable choices as well.

The image is a treasury, not a mask. “Abundance” (the overflow of what fills the heart) and “treasure” (what has been laid up) say that talk is withdrawal, not invention on the spot. A good person brings forth good things from a good store; an evil person brings forth evil things from an evil store. Speech does not create the moral stockpile. It spends it. That is why Jesus can move straight from “how can you, being evil, speak good things?” to the claim that the mouth simply utters what the heart already holds in surplus.

Two nearby images lock that in. Just before, a tree is known by its fruit: you do not get good fruit from a bad tree (12:33). Just after, people will answer for every careless word, and be justified or condemned by their words (12:36–37). Fruit and treasure are both stored results that become public. The Pharisees’ charge—that Jesus casts out demons by Beelzebul—is treated as that kind of withdrawal: not a slip, but evidence of what they have been treasuring.

So the three descriptions fit, but they are not equal in the text.

- **Symptoms.** This is the verse’s main claim. Evil speech is what an evil heart naturally produces. You cannot expect good words from a heart whose surplus is evil, any more than you can expect good fruit from a bad tree. Words diagnose the store.
- **Revelations.** Because the mouth speaks from that surplus, speech discloses what was hidden. The heart’s treasure becomes audible. Justification and condemnation by words only make sense if words reliably show what has been laid up.
- **Choices.** Verses 34–35 stress necessity (“how can you…?”) more than deliberation. Agency shows up around them: people store treasure, bring it forth, and are judged for what they say. The stockpile is not an excuse. It is what they have accumulated and what they choose to spend. Careless words still count, because they come from that store.

Read together, the passage says words are symptoms that reveal the heart’s moral reserves, and those reserves are the speaker’s own—so the speech is culpable, not merely informative. The hidden stockpile explains the words; it does not absolve them.
