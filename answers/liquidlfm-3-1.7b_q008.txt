**1 John 4:20 (NIV)**  
> “If a man say, ‘I love God,’ and **hathe[s] his brother**, he is a liar: for he that loveth not his brother whom he hath seen, how can he love God whom he hath not seen?”

---

## 1. Why does the verse make the *visible* neighbor the “test” of *invisible* God?

| Element | Meaning in the verse |
|---------|----------------------|
| **“I love God”** | A declaration of love for the unseen Creator. |
| **“Hathe[s] his brother”** | An act of hostility toward a real person—someone we can see and interact with. |
| **“Who he hath seen”** | The requirement that we have *personal, observable* contact with that person. |
| **“How can he love God whom he hath not seen?”** | The logical problem: If you haven’t personally encountered God (or a clear sign of Him), how can you claim to love Him? |

### The theological point
- **God’s love is revealed in the world.** In the Old and New Testaments, God shows Himself through creation, through the lives of people, and especially through the cross‑sacrificial love of Christ.  
- **Our relationship with another human being is the primary window onto God.** When we see a brother (or sister, friend, neighbor) we recognize Him in His character, mercy, and obedience.  
- **Love for God is demonstrated by love for those who reflect Him.** If we love God but reject or despise the very person who shows us His face, we are not truly loving Him—we are merely waving at an invisible figure while ignoring the tangible reality around us.

Thus, **the “test” is love of the visible neighbor**: can we love God *through* the way we love the people we meet? If we can’t, we’re not really loving God.

---

## 2. What kind of self‑deception is it exposing?

### The hidden assumption
Many people (and even some churches) assume that:
1. **Love of God can exist independently of any outward expression.**  
2. **One can claim to love God simply by reciting Scripture or hearing sermons, without actually caring about others.**

This creates a **self‑deceptive narrative**:

| Self‑deception | What it looks like |
|----------------|--------------------|
| **“I’m already loved by God; I don’t need to prove it.”** | Ignoring the need to live out that love in daily life. |
| **“My hatred of my brother proves I’m not truly a Christian.”** | Using a negative trait (hating a neighbor) as evidence of disbelief. |
| **“God is far away; I’ll find Him later.”** | Delaying genuine relationship with God until after we’ve formed positive connections elsewhere. |

In short, the believer **confuses intellectual assent (“I love God”) with behavioral love**. The verse calls this out: *you cannot claim to love an invisible Father while actively harming a visible brother.*

### The result
- **Spiritual pride:** The person thinks they are above the moral demands of love.  
- **Hypocrisy:** Their words contradict their actions, eroding trust among friends and family.  
- **Isolation:** By refusing to engage with the world, they miss the opportunity to see God’s grace working in ordinary relationships.

---

## 3. Practical take‑away

1. **Ask yourself:** *Do I love my neighbor as much as I claim to love God?*  
2. **Examine my motives:** Am I loving God because I feel God’s presence, or because I want to avoid conflict or gain status?  
3. **Turn the test inward:** Choose to love your brother/sister/friend—not just tolerate them—but to reflect the love God has poured into us.  

When you do, the “invisible” love of God becomes *visible* in the way you treat the “visible” neighbor. The verse thus **demands humility before God’s revelation in the world**.

--- 

**Bottom line:** 1 John 4:20 teaches that true devotion to God is proven by extending that same love to the people we can see and touch. Claiming love for God while hating or ignoring one’s brother is a lie—a self‑deception that blinds us to the way God works through our relationships.
