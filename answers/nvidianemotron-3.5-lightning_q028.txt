This verse sits at the dramatic center of the Pericope Adulterae (John 8:1–11), and its moral power comes from how it simultaneously disarms a crowd, turns a mirror on the accusers, and redefines the very possibility of righteous judgment. Let’s break down each of the angles you raised:

### 1. It exposes hypocrisy (not innocence)
The line *"He that is without sin among you, let him first cast a stone"* doesn't actually produce a sinless person—it produces a silence. The accusers brought the woman to trap Jesus, but Jesus’ condition doesn't prove her innocence; it reveals the accusers' guilt. The text says they "went out one by one, beginning at the eldest" (v. 9). The sentence doesn't say "she is innocent"; it says "your own conscience disqualifies you." The destabilization comes from the fact that moral outrage is usually projective—we see sin in others to avoid seeing it in ourselves. Jesus short-circuits that mechanism.

### 2. It interrupts mob-righteousness
This is perhaps the most immediate effect. A mob can commit violence while each individual feels blameless ("I'm just following orders," "the law says this"). By making the first stone a personal, conscience-driven act, Jesus dissolves the diffusion of responsibility. The crowd doesn't disperse because the woman is freed; they disperse because the *act* of stoning now requires each man to confront his own heart. That's a fundamental breakdown of mob psychology.

### 3. It doesn't forbid judgment, but it reforms it
A common misreading is that Jesus is saying "never judge." But the narrative arc suggests otherwise: after the crowd leaves, Jesus asks the woman, *"Has no one condemned you?"* She says, *"No one, Lord."* Jesus replies, *"Neither do I condemn you. Go and sin no more."* 
He is not abolishing moral standards; He is abolishing *condemnatory judgment* that is unaccompanied by self-examination. The verse sets a precondition for judgment: sinlessness. Since no one meets it, the judgment is stayed—not because judgment is invalid, but because it’s been shown to be unworkable in the hands of the self-deceived.

### 4. The deeper destabilization: the collapse of external righteousness
What makes the sentence *deeply* destabilizing is that it refuses to let moral authority be derived from position, gender, lineage, or legalism. The Pharisees were the "keepers of the law," yet Jesus’ question exposes that their religiosity had become a weapon. 

It forces a shift from **retributive morality** ("punish the sinner") to **formative morality** ("help the sinner stop"). The sentence doesn't just let the woman go; it reorients the entire moral subject from the one being judged to the one doing the judging. It asks: *What kind of righteousness demands another's destruction?*

### Summary
It’s morally destabilizing because it:
- **Unmasks hypocrisy** by turning the spotlight inward
- **Fragments mob cruelty** by making violence a personal, conscience-bound act
- **Replaces conditional condemnation** with a standard no one can meet, without abolishing morality itself
- **Redefines righteousness** as something internal and transformative rather than external and performative

It’s a moment where a sentence doesn't just change a legal outcome—it changes the architecture of how we think about justice, guilt, and our own capacity to judge.
