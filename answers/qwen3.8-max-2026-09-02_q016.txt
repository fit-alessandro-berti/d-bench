The elder brother’s deepest problem is **not primarily anger**. Anger is the visible eruption. The deeper issue is that **he cannot bear mercy**—mercy given to someone he judges undeserving.

But to be still more precise: his deepest problem is that **he has a servant’s heart inside the father’s house**. He has turned sonship into wage-labor, obedience into a claim on the father, and love into something that must be earned. That is why mercy to the prodigal feels to him like injustice. If the father gives grace freely, then the elder brother’s whole economy of merit collapses.

So among the options you list, I would say:

- **Anger** is the symptom.
- **Envy** is one expression of it.
- **Wounded fairness** is the language he uses to justify it.
- **Pride in obedience** is the structure of his self-image.
- But the deepest exposed problem is **discomfort with mercy itself**.

Several details in the text point to this.

### 1. He speaks like a servant, not a son

He says:

> “Lo, these many years do I serve thee, neither transgressed I at any time thy commandment.”

That is the language of service, obedience, and accounting. He does not say, “Father, I have been with you.” He says, “I serve you.” He sees himself as an employee who has kept the terms of a contract. His relationship with the father is not primarily love, trust, or communion; it is duty and desert.

This is why he immediately asks for payment:

> “Thou never gavest me a kid, that I might make merry with my friends.”

He feels underpaid. He thinks his obedience has put the father in his debt. But the father replies:

> “Son, thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine.”

The tragedy is that the elder brother already had everything—yet he never seemed to enjoy it as sonship. He had the father’s presence, but not the father’s heart. He had the inheritance, but no joy in it.

### 2. He refuses to call the prodigal his brother

He says:

> “As soon as this thy son was come…”

He will not say, “my brother.” He distances himself. He defines the younger brother by his sin: “this thy son… which hath devoured thy living with harlots.”

But the father corrects him gently and firmly:

> “For this thy brother was dead, and is alive again; and was lost, and is found.”

The father insists on the family bond. The elder brother insists on the moral record. His problem is not only that he is angry at the father’s generosity; it is that he has no compassion for the brother’s restoration. He does not rejoice that the lost has been found. He resents that the guilty is being welcomed.

That is the heart of his discomfort with mercy.

### 3. His sense of fairness is real, but it is not ultimate

His complaint sounds like justice:

> “I obeyed; you never rewarded me. He sinned; you killed the fatted calf.”

From a purely human standpoint, that is not an irrational reaction. But the parable is not asking whether the father is “fair” in a commercial sense. It is revealing the father’s heart. The father does not deny the elder brother’s claim; he says, in effect, “You misunderstand everything. You were never trying to earn a place. You are my son. All that I have is yours. But your brother had to be received back from death.”

The elder brother’s problem is that he absolutizes fairness and cannot see beyond it. He wants the father to act according to desert. But the father acts according to mercy.

### 4. His anger reveals that he is also lost—just in a different way

The younger brother was lost in a far country. The elder brother is lost near the father’s house.

The younger brother strayed through rebellion. The elder brother strays through resentment. The younger brother comes home repentant. The elder brother stands outside refusing to enter.

And notice: the father comes out to both sons.

The father runs to the younger son. The father also comes out to entreat the elder son. The elder brother is offered grace too. But he refuses the feast because he cannot stand the terms on which the feast is being held: mercy for the undeserving.

That is his deepest tragedy. He is not excluded by the father’s rejection; he excludes himself by his refusal to share the father’s joy.

### 5. In Luke’s context, he represents the Pharisees and scribes

Luke 15 begins with the Pharisees and scribes grumbling:

> “This man receiveth sinners, and eateth with them.”

Then Jesus tells three parables: the lost sheep, the lost coin, and the lost sons. Each parable ends with joy over what was found. The elder brother is the final mirror. He represents those who are outwardly obedient, religious, and morally disciplined, but who cannot enter into God’s joy over repentant sinners.

Their problem is not simply that they are proud. Their problem is that they do not recognize the heart of God. They think God’s favor is earned by performance. So when God shows mercy to the lost, they feel threatened. If sinners can be welcomed by grace, then what advantage is their obedience?

That is why the elder brother’s deepest problem is **his inability to rejoice in mercy**.

### So what is the deepest problem?

It is a heart that has made obedience into a wage, the father into a creditor, and mercy into an offense.

He is not merely angry. He is not merely envious. He is not merely wounded in his sense of fairness. He is not simply proud of his obedience. All of those things are present. But they converge into this:

> **He cannot love what the father loves.**

The father loves the lost son who has returned. The elder brother loves the principle that the lost son should pay for what he did. The father says, “We must rejoice.” The elder brother says, “It is unfair.”

His deepest problem is that **he does not know the father’s heart**, even though he has been in the father’s house all along.

So if forced to choose one answer from your list, I would say:

> **His deepest problem is discomfort with mercy itself.**

But more fully:

> **His deepest problem is a self-righteous heart that has turned sonship into earning and therefore cannot enter into the father’s joy over grace.**
