Enemy-love is difficult to take seriously because it runs directly against several deeply entrenched human instincts, cultural narratives, and even common theological simplifications. Your question isolates three possible framings: sentimental, strategic, or a deeper moral strength. The most honest answer is that it is none of these in isolation, but rather a demanding practice of moral and spiritual formation that touches all three while transcending them.

### 1. Why it’s hard to take seriously
- **Psychological and evolutionary wiring:** Humans are attuned to threat detection, reciprocity, and in-group/out-group dynamics. Love toward those who harm or oppose us feels counterintuitive to survival instincts and to the natural desire for justice, boundary-setting, and self-protection.
- **Cultural and rhetorical misuse:** In popular discourse, “love your enemies” is often reduced to passive compliance, emotional suppression, or moral superiority. It can sound like sentimentality precisely because it’s frequently preached without attention to practical limits, accountability, or the reality of abuse and systemic harm.
- **Misreading the command:** The passage does not call for naivete, coercion, or the absence of boundaries. Historically, enemy-love in this tradition has been distinguished from approval, trust, or the surrender of justice. It’s a posture of the will and the heart, not a denial of reality.

### 2. Is it sentimental?
Superficially, it can be mistaken for sentimentality when it’s presented as a warm feeling or a moral platitude. But the text itself resists that reading: it couples love with concrete actions (“do good,” “pray”) and grounds it in the character of God, who “makes his sun rise on the evil and on the good.” Sentimental love is passive and self-protective; enemy-love is active, costly, and often uncomfortable. It asks for goodwill and beneficence without requiring reconciliation, agreement, or the minimization of wrongs.

### 3. Is it strategic?
There are strategic benefits to refusing retaliation: breaking cycles of violence, preserving moral credibility, creating space for de-escalation, and refusing to let an opponent dictate one’s own character. Yet the passage itself does not frame enemy-love primarily as a tactic. It’s presented as a way of becoming “children of your Father,” which points to formation and likeness rather than leverage. Strategy can accompany the practice, but if love of enemies is adopted only because it “works,” it collapses back into self-interest and loses its distinctive moral shape.

### 4. Is it a deeper form of moral strength?
This is where the text lands most naturally. Enemy-love requires discipline over the impulse to mirror harm, the courage to refuse dehumanization, and the humility to recognize shared humanity without erasing accountability. It is strength in the sense of self-mastery, imaginative breadth, and fidelity to a moral identity that is not contingent on the behavior of others. It does not replace justice, truth-telling, or protective boundaries; rather, it refuses to let injury dictate one’s own capacity for goodwill.

### In summary
Enemy-love is difficult to take seriously because it asks people to act against instinct, resists cheap sentimentalism, and cannot be reduced to a pragmatic maneuver. It is best understood as a demanding moral and spiritual practice: a way of cultivating a character that does not become what it opposes, while still acknowledging the reality of harm and the necessity of justice. Taken seriously, it is neither soft nor merely strategic, but a disciplined form of moral strength that seeks to break cycles of retaliation without abandoning truth or boundaries.
