**Short answer:**  
The Pharisee is not condemned for *false doctrine* as such. He is condemned for **self‑righteousness**—a proud trust in his own moral record which expresses itself in **comparison, contempt, and the display of his virtues as claims to merit**. Pride is the root; comparison and contempt are its relational fruits; his recital of fasting and tithing is the form his self‑display takes.

The tax collector’s posture is “truer” because it is the truthful posture of a sinner who has stopped trusting himself and casts himself entirely on God’s mercy.

---

## 1. What the parable says is the issue

Luke introduces the parable with the “certain” people:

> “…which **trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and despised others**” (Luke 18:9)

and closes with the principle:

> “Every one that exalteth himself shall be abased; and he that humbleth himself shall be exalted” (v. 14).

So the indictment is already named: **self‑trust and contempt for others**.

---

## 2. Evaluating the options

### ❌ False doctrine?
Not really, at least not formal doctrine.

The Pharisee’s practices are not heretical. He fasts twice a week and tithes “all” he possesses—both go *beyond* the bare requirements of the Law. Jesus does not say, “Your fasting and tithing are false.” In fact, elsewhere Jesus says these things should be done (Luke 11:42). His problem is not that he has wrong beliefs about God’s law, but that he uses his obedience as a basis for boasting and self‑salvation.

If we must speak of “false doctrine,” it is a **functional** false doctrine: he lives as if righteousness is something he achieves and as if God shares his disdain for “sinners.” But the parable does not target his creed; it targets his heart.

### ✅ Pride / self‑trust — the root
This is the core sin.

He “trusted in themselves” (v. 9). The prayer in vv. 11–12 is really curved inward. The Greek says he “prayed thus **with himself**” (πρὸς ἑαυτόν)—which can mean he stood by himself, but also that his prayer was self‑referential, almost a soliloquy. He addresses God, but the real subject is himself:

- “I thank thee…”
- “I am not…”
- “I fast…”
- “I give tithes…”

Notice what is missing: there is **no confession, no petition, no request for mercy, no intercession**. A prayer that asks nothing of God may be verbally orthodox, but it is not fellowship with God. It is self‑congratulation with God as an audience.

### ✅ Comparison — the instrument of pride
He measures himself horizontally, not vertically:

> “I am not as other men are… or even as this publican.”

Rather than measuring himself by God’s holiness, he measures himself by other sinners. That is how pride sustains itself: “At least I’m not like *them*.”

### ✅ Contempt — the relational fruit
The parable’s audience “despised others” (v. 9), and the Pharisee’s prayer drips with contempt:

> “…or even as **this publican**.”

He does not merely fail to love his neighbor; he uses his neighbor as a foil for his own superiority. That violates the heart of the Law (love of neighbor) and shows that his “righteousness” is really social and spiritual status‑seeking.

### ✅ Virtue as self‑display — the form it takes
His fasting and tithing are real acts of devotion. The problem is what he does with them: he displays them before God as résumé items.

He treats virtue as **capital**—as if God now owes him. But grace cannot be earned. The Pharisee comes to the temple full of his own merits; therefore he has no room to receive mercy.

---

## 3. So what “exactly” condemns him?

Not bad doctrine, but **bad trust**.

The Pharisee is condemned for **seeking to justify himself by his own righteousness and despising those who do not measure up**. In other words:

- **Pride** is the root.
- **Comparison** and **contempt** are how that pride relates to others.
- **Self‑display of virtue** is how that pride prays.

All of these add up to what the New Testament elsewhere calls “confidence in the flesh” (Philippians 3:3–9). The Pharisee is morally impressive and religiously serious, yet he goes home unjustified because he trusts in himself rather than in God’s mercy.

---

## 4. Why is the tax collector’s posture “spiritually truer”?

Because it corresponds to reality.

### His posture
- **“Standing afar off”** — a sense of unworthiness.
- **“Would not lift up so much as his eyes unto heaven”** — shame and reverence.
- **“Smote upon his breast”** — grief and repentance.

These are not magic gestures. They are the outward expression of an inward truth: *I have no standing before God except mercy.*

### His prayer
> “God be merciful to me a sinner.”

The Greek verb is **ἱλάσθητι** (*hilastheti*)—“be propitiated,” “make atonement for me.” This is covenant language. He is not saying, “God, feel sorry for me.” He is saying, in effect, “God, do for me what only you can do—cover my sin.”

He brings:
- no merits,
- no comparisons,
- no excuses,
- no résumé.

Only need.

### Why this is “truer”
It is truer because it tells the truth about **God** and about **the self**:

1. **God is holy and merciful**, not a patron impressed by spiritual achievements.
2. **Every person is a sinner** in need of grace—including the Pharisee, though he will not admit it.
3. **Justification is received, not achieved.** The tax collector’s empty hands are the only hands that can be filled.

The Pharisee’s posture is false because it denies the fundamental creature‑creature relationship: he stands before God as a self‑sufficient moral achiever. The tax collector’s posture is true because he stands before God as a guilty sinner dependent on mercy.

---

## 5. The bottom line

The parable is not saying:

- “Doctrine doesn’t matter,”
- “Fasting and tithing are bad,”
- or “The more immoral you are, the more God accepts you.”

It is saying:

> The person who trusts in his own goodness and looks down on others goes home unjustified. The person who confesses his sin and throws himself on God’s mercy goes home justified—not because groveling earns salvation, but because humility is the empty hand that receives grace.

Or, in Jesus’ own summary:

> “He that humbleth himself shall be exalted.”

The tax collector’s posture is “spiritually truer” because it is the posture of faith: the end of self‑trust and the beginning of reliance on God.
