The elder brother’s deepest problem is **a view of sonship governed by merit rather than love**. His anger, envy, wounded sense of fairness, and pride in obedience all express that deeper problem—and it makes mercy toward his brother feel like an injustice toward himself.

Notice how he speaks:

- **“These many years do I serve thee.”** He describes his relationship with his father in terms of service rendered.
- **“Neither transgressed I … thy commandment.”** His obedience becomes a claim upon the father: *I have earned better treatment.*
- **“Thou never gavest me a kid.”** His brother’s welcome exposes his own accumulated resentment.
- **“This thy son.”** He distances himself from his brother precisely when the father is restoring him.

His complaint is not simply that wrongdoing matters. It does. The younger son really has squandered his inheritance. The elder brother’s trouble is that he cannot distinguish **celebrating a person’s restoration from rewarding that person’s wrongdoing**. To him, the feast looks like a verdict that faithfulness counts for nothing.

The father answers that misunderstanding tenderly: **“Son, thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine.”** The younger son’s welcome does not erase the elder son’s belonging or dispossess him. The father then says **“this thy brother”**—inviting him to recover the relationship his resentment has denied.

So **discomfort with mercy is the deepest visible expression of his problem**, but its root is his merit-based understanding of the father’s love. He can accept generosity as payment for obedience; he struggles to accept it as joy over someone who was lost and has been found.

The crucial contrast is not obedience versus disobedience. It is **obedience that shares the father’s heart versus obedience that keeps an account against him**.

And the ending remains open. The father comes out and entreats the elder brother, too. He is not merely exposed as proud; he is invited into joy. The unanswered question is whether he will receive his brother—and discover that being a son is more than being a deserving servant.
