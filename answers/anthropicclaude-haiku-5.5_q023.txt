The pairing of "way" and "thoughts" is a deliberate piece of Hebrew parallelism, and it covers the whole person. The verse does not say "the wicked forsake his deeds" alone. It moves from the visible path a person walks to the inner world that directs the walk.

**Way and thoughts as a pair**

- **"Way"** (*derek*) is the outward course of life: habits, practices, the direction one's conduct takes. Isaiah often uses it for behavior (1:16–17; 59:8).
- **"Thoughts"** (*machashavah*) is better rendered "plans," "schemes," or "purposes" than fleeting mental images. It denotes deliberate reasoning about what to do and how to justify it. In Isaiah 59:7 the wicked are described as having "thoughts of iniquity," meaning plotting, not just stray fancies.

The two terms also echo the chapter's own closing contrast. In 55:8–9, God says "my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways." So the call to abandon *our* way and *our* thoughts is a call to be reoriented toward *God's* way and thoughts.

**What this implies**

1. **Outward conduct alone is not enough.** Repentance is not mere behavioral reform. A person can stop a sinful act while still planning, justifying, and desiring it. The verse anticipates the later teaching that sin begins in the heart (Proverbs 4:23; Jeremiah 17:9; Matthew 5:21–28). Turning from wrongdoing requires turning from the reasoning that produced it.

2. **Inner imagination and purpose are morally real.** The inner life is not a private zone exempt from accountability. What a person intends and rationalizes shapes what he does, and God addresses it directly. The inward turn is therefore not optional spiritual decoration but the root of genuine return.

3. **Mercy addresses the whole person.** Because the problem extends to thoughts as well as ways, God's pardon is described as "abundant." The mercy is proportionate to the depth of the need. This matters because no human can reform his own inner life sufficiently by willpower. The promise is that God pardons, and pardon reaches what behavior alone cannot reach.

4. **Mercy is grounded in God's character, not in the perfection of the repentance.** The verse says God "will have mercy" and "will abundantly pardon." The sinner's turning is the condition, but the reliability of mercy rests on God's own nature. This is why the call is urgent and hopeful at once: "seek ye the Lord while he may be found," yet the one who returns will find God ready to show compassion.

**A caution**

The verse should not be read as saying that repentance can be completed by an act of inner resolve apart from grace. The thoughts in view are not simply corrected by self-discipline; they are reoriented by the same God who pardons. Read with 55:1–3 and 55:10–11, the chapter presents repentance as responding to a free invitation, with the word of God itself accomplishing what it promises.

**In short:** "Way" names what a person does, and "thoughts" names what he plans and justifies. Calling the wicked to forsake both shows that real repentance reaches the root of sin, and promising abundant pardon shows that God's mercy is equal to reaching that root.
