It connects speech with the heart as an **overflow of a disposition**, not as a simple mechanical stamp of the heart.

A few points of clarification:

- **Greek shape:** “out of the abundance/fullness of the heart the mouth speaks” (*ek toutou perissoï tou kardias*); the good person “brings forth” good from the “good treasure” (*thēsauros*), and the evil person brings forth evil from the evil treasure. The “treasure” language is vivid, not forensically precise: the heart is less a locked vault to be weighed on a scale than a full reservoir whose contents spill into speech.

So “hidden moral stockpile” is a good picture, but it should not be pressed into a deterministic claim such as “my words reveal exactly what I am, like a fingerprint.” The text says less about measurable inner stores than about orientation: a whole inner state tends to issue in words.

The passage can be read as **three things at once**:

1. **Symptomatic:** speech is evidence of what is loose in the heart. Harsh words, flattering lies, cynicism, and sharp judgment are not merely “bad style”; they are signs that something is already amiss. A good heart does not mean perfectly harmless speech, but its natural stream differs.

2. **Chosen:** this is not pure nature. The offending claim is addressed to a “generation” and denies exemption. The speaker is not allowed to say, “It just came out,” as a complete moral answer. Speech is a voluntary act with consequences; the heart shapes it, but it does not erase responsibility.

3. **Revelational:** words are a partial window into character. The mouth “brings forth” what the heart contains, so speech functions as a revelation of the speaker’s orientation. At the same time, it is an incomplete and fallible window: one can be hypocritical, self-deceiving, or practiced in masks; the heart must still be examined rather than read off like a report card.

The sharpest reading: **speech is both evidence and practice.** Looking at the mouth, we can infer something about the heart; but speaking is also something the heart does—forming us as much as it discloses us. The passage warns against innocent exteriority while refusing a crude psychology in which the heart is an ungoverned reservoir.

For ethics, the implication is not “speak carefully and the inner problem disappears.” It is the reverse-adjacent point: words reveal whether the inner orientation is good or vicious, but speech can also disciplin, correct, and realign that orientation. In short: **speech is symptom, choice, and revelation—together, not as one explanation exhaustively.**
