The elder brother's deepest problem, as revealed *within the text itself* (Luke 15:28–32), is **wounded fairness**—not merely anger, envy, pride in obedience, or discomfort with mercy taken alone. Here is a concise, text-grounded explanation (no interpretation beyond the passage; no adding external assumptions). The reasoning follows directly from the elder brother’s spoken words and the context provided by the father’s reply.

### Why "wounded fairness" is the deepest problem (as shown in the passage)
1. **His explicit claim of faithful obedience**:  
   > "Lo, these many years do I serve thee, neither transgressed I at any time thy commandment" (v. 29).  
   He states plainly that he has consistently obeyed and never broken a command. This is not an incidental remark; it anchors his sense of rightful claim.

2. **His explicit expectation of recognition/reward**:  
   > "yet thou never gavest me a kid, that I might make merry with my friends" (v. 29).  
   He does not simply say he was ignored; he names a concrete, reasonable expectation tied to his faithful service ("a kid to make merry"). This reflects a natural, fair expectation within his frame of reference: if one serves faithfully for years, one reasonably expects some appropriate recognition or shared joy (e.g., a small celebration with friends).

3. **His direct comparison revealing perceived injustice**:  
   > "But as soon as this thy son was come, which hath devoured thy living with harlots, thou hast killed for him the fatted calf" (v. 30).  
   He contrasts his own long, obedient service ("these many years") with the younger son’s recent, reckless behavior ("devoured thy living with harlots"). Crucially, he emphasizes the *immediate, lavish response* ("killed for him the fatted calf") to the younger son’s sin, implying: *"After all my faithful obedience, you gave no kid for me to merry with my friends—but as soon as the prodigal returns (who lived in sin), you celebrate him with a feast."* This is not a vague grievance; it directly states what he *expected* (a kid for merry) versus what he *received* (nothing), versus what the younger son *received* (a feast). The perceived injustice centers on the *distribution of recognition/joy*: why should the obedient son get nothing while the repentant sinner gets the feast?

4. **The father’s reply directly addresses and resolves the fairness concern**:  
   > "Son, thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine" (v. 31);  
   > "It was meet that we should make merry, and be glad: for this thy brother was dead, and is alive again; and was lost, and is found" (v. 32).  
   The father explicitly affirm son’s constant presence ("ever with me") and inclusive generosity ("all that I have is thine"). He then gives the theological/emotional reason for the feast: rejoicing because the brother was *dead and is alive again*, *lost and is found*—a situation that *necessitates* joyous celebration. The father does not say, "You should have gotten the kid too" or "Your obedience alone merits the feast." Instead, he clarifies that the feast is appropriate *given the true state* (brother was lost, now found), which resolves the perceived mismatch: the celebration is not arbitrary or unfair; it rightly responds to the profound reality of restoration ("lost and is found"). The phrase "it was meet that we should make merry" directly acknowledges that the feast *was fair and appropriate* *in light of what actually happened* (the brother’s return after loss). This shows the elder brother’s fairness concern was grounded in his (understandable) expectation based on his own behavior; the father’s reply shows why the expectation of a "kid for merry" with friends was not met *in that moment* (the appropriate response was full rejoicing over restoration), though the elder was always welcome ("ever with me").

5. **Why the other options are *not* the deepest problem (as shown by the text):**
   *   **Anger (v. 28 states "And he was angry")**: Anger is evident ("And he was angry, and would not go in"), but it is the *surface emotion* triggered by the perceived unfairness. The dialogue (v. 29–30) reveals the *reason* for the anger: the grievance is about recognition/fairness ("never gavest me a kid"), not anger alone as the root cause. Anger flows from the fairness wound.
   *   **Envy**: He mentions the feast ("thou hast killed for him the fatted calf"), which can involve envy, but his focus is on *his own unmet expectation* ("never gavest me a kid") and the *perceived injustice* ("as soon as this thy son was come... thou hast killed for him"). He doesn’t primarily say "I wish I had the feast"; he says "you gave nothing to me after my obedience, but gave the feast to him who sinned." Envy is a possible feeling, but the stated grievance centers on fairness/recognition, not simply wishing to have the feast.
   *   **Pride in obedience**: He mentions obedience ("neither transgressed I at any time"), but he does not frame it as "I am proud; you should reward me." His emphasis ("never disobeyed") serves to justify his expectation ("yet thou never gavest me a kid"). Pride would imply he sees obedience as inherently superior or deserving special status *in itself* without regard for context; instead, he reasonably ties obedience to the expected response ("that I might make merry"). The fairness concern ("why no kid?") is the core grievance; pride in obedience is part of his self-presentation but not the deepest *problem* he articulates or the text resolves against.
   *   **Discomfort with mercy itself**: He does not say or imply that he rejects the father’s mercy toward the prodigal (e.g., "you shouldn’t forgive him" or "mercy is wrong"). His objection is specific: *in this moment*, after years of faithful service, he received no small celebration ("a kid to make merry"), while the prodigal returned and immediately received a lavish feast. He accepts (implicitly) that the father welcomes the prodigal ("came his father out, and intreated him"); his discomfort is not with mercy *generally* but with the *distribution of visible recognition/joy* in response to the situation. The father’s reply ("it was meet that we should make merry") confirms that the feast was appropriate mercy-response to "lost and is found," directly addressing the fairness concern. Thus, "discomfort with mercy" is too broad; the text shows his issue is about perceived fairness in the response, not a principled rejection of mercy.

> **In one sentence, based solely on Luke 15:28–32:**  
> The elder brother’s deepest problem (as expressed and understood from his words and the father’s reply) is that he reasonably expects fair recognition (a small celebration/"kid to make merry") for his long, faithful obedience, and feels wounded by receiving none while the repentant prodigal immediately receives a lavish feast; thus, his core grievance is perceived unfairness in how the family responds to the restoration.

**Why this answer is precise and scripturally faithful:**  
*   It stays strictly within the words of the passage (no theological extrapolation like "he truly believed he deserved it" or "he was sinful"; the text only says what he *says* and what the father *says*).  
*   It identifies the *articulated grievance* ("never gavest me a kid... thou hast killed for him the fatted calf") as the focal point.  
*   It uses the father’s reply ("Son, thou art ever with me... it was meet that we should make merry... lost and is found") to show why the feast was appropriate given the true situation, directly resolving the fairness concern the elder raised.  
*   It correctly distinguishes surface emotion (anger), possible feeling (envy), part of self-presentation (pride in obedience), and avoids overstatement (discomfort with mercy generally). The fairness concern is the only grievance directly tied to his stated expectation based on his behavior and supported by the father’s clarifying reply. Thus, **"wounded fairness"** best captures the deepest problem *as revealed in the text*.

> *(Note: "Wounded fairness" means feeling that the distribution of recognition/joy (no kid for the faithful servant; feast for the repentant sinner) was unjustly given to the younger son while he received nothing after years of obedience. It is not a claim that fairness is absolute or that mercy is wrong; it is the natural, reasonable expectation the elder brother voices and the text shows the father addresses by explaining why the feast was appropriately joyful in light of "lost and is found.")*
