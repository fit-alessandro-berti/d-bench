Luke 6:37 is not an abolition of moral reality, it is a prohibition of a certain *heart posture* toward moral reality.

Jesus is not saying moral judgments never matter. He is assuming they do. The whole Sermon on the Plain is about mercy vs. hardness, generosity vs. stinginess, forgiveness vs. unforgiveness. If moral judgments never mattered, there would be nothing to forgive.

The Greek makes the distinction clear. *Krino* is to judge, to separate, to evaluate. *Katakrino* is to condemn, to judge down, to pronounce final guilt. Jesus says do not *krinete* and you will not be *krinesthe*; do not *katakrinete* and you will not be *katakrinesthe*. The reciprocity is not "don't see wrong", it is "don't use the power of seeing wrong the way God uses it."

**Discernment is judgment in the service of love. The appetite to condemn is judgment in the service of self.**

Discernment asks: *What is true here, and how do I love in light of it?*

It is humble. It begins with the log in your own eye, Luke 6:42. It distinguishes between a person and an action, and between an action and a final identity. It is necessary for protection, guidance, and healing. You must discern that abuse is wrong to stop it. You must discern that gossip is destructive to refrain from it. You must discern that a friend is in error to speak the truth to them in love. Paul can say "Judge nothing before the time" Rom 14:4 and also "Do you not judge those inside?" 1 Cor 5:12.

Discernment is also self-referential. It is used first on oneself. It seeks restoration, not exile. Jesus himself discerns constantly: He names the Pharisees hypocrites, calls Herod a fox, and tells the adulterous woman "Neither do I condemn you." Discernment can say "This is wrong" and still stay in relationship.

The appetite to condemn is judgment in the service of self-justification. It asks: *How can I feel better, safer, more righteous by putting you down?*

It is proud, final, and enjoys the act. It does not want to understand, it wants to win. It moves quickly from "this act is wrong" to "you are worthless and beyond reach." It is the moral voyeurism of the crowd at the foot of the cross: they judge with pleasure.

Condemnation is not discernment made sharp, it is discernment made cruel. It refuses the beam removal in Matthew 7:5, so it cannot see clearly. It judges to sever rather than to heal. It withholds mercy while demanding it for itself.

That is why Jesus pairs the negative with a positive command: *forgive, and you will be forgiven.* The alternative to condemnation is not moral blindness, it is merciful sight. You still see the sin, but you see the person as God sees them: in need of mercy that you yourself have received.

To say "judge not" means moral judgments matter so much that we are not fit to wield them as final verdicts. Only God condemns finally. Our job is to discern rightly enough to love rightly, and to forgive as we have been forgiven.
