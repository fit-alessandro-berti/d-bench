Galatians 6:1 (KJV): "Brethren, if a man be overtaken in a fault, ye which are spiritual, restore such an one in the spirit of meekness; considering thyself, lest thou also be tempted."

1. The moral posture required

The verse describes correction as something done by a fellow-sinner, not by a superior. Every clause is arranged to keep the corrector from standing above the person being corrected.

- "Brethren" frames the whole act inside kinship. The one who has fallen is not an offender before a judge but a brother among brothers. Correction is a family duty, and family relationship is prior to the fault.

- "Overtaken in a fault" (prolēmphthē en tini paraptōmati) pictures someone caught, surprised, or outrun by a sin—a stumble or false step (paraptōma) rather than a settled, defiant rebellion. The verse assumes the sin is real and must be dealt with, but it also presumes the fallen person is a victim of his own weakness as much as its author. That presumption shapes the tone: one does not stand over a man who has tripped and lecture him; one helps him up.

- "Ye which are spiritual" is not a title of rank. In context (5:16–26) the "spiritual" are those who walk by the Spirit and bear its fruit—love, longsuffering, gentleness, goodness, meekness, temperance. Paul is saying: the very people who are qualified to correct are the ones whose character makes them least likely to do it harshly. If the correction is done in pride or contempt, that itself proves the corrector is not acting "spiritually" but according to the flesh, whose works include "strife, envyings, wrath" (5:20). The qualification and the manner are inseparable: you are only fit to correct in the measure that you correct meekly.

- "Restore" (katartizete) is a word of repair and setting right—used of mending nets (Matt 4:21) and, medically, of setting a broken bone. The aim is not punishment, exposure, or the vindication of the community's standards, but the mending of the person, so that he is fit again for use and in his place again in the body. The goal governs the method: you do not set a bone with a hammer.

- "In the spirit of meekness" (en pneumati praütētos). Meekness is not softness about sin; it is strength that refuses to assert itself against a weaker person. It is the disposition of one who knows he has no standing of his own before God and therefore has no standing to lord over another. It is listed as fruit of the Spirit in 5:23, so the phrase can be read both as "a meek spirit" and as "the Spirit's own gentleness" acting through the corrector.

- "Considering thyself, lest thou also be tempted" (skopōn seauton). The verb means to fix one's gaze, to keep watch. Notice the shift to the singular: Paul moves from the plural "ye" to "thyself"—each individual corrector must turn the inspection inward. The reason given is not sentimental but sober: you are made of the same stuff, and you may be tempted in the same way (or tempted, in the very act of correcting, to pride, harshness, or Schadenfreude).

Taken together, the posture is one of humble, gentle, restorative solidarity: firm about the fault, tender toward the person, and suspicious of oneself.

2. Why restoration, meekness, and self-suspicion are kept together

Paul does not offer these as three separable virtues; they hold one another in place. Remove any one and the others collapse into something else.

(a) Restoration needs meekness, because the goal determines the means. If the aim is to mend a person, harshness is not merely unkind, it is counterproductive: it drives the fallen into shame, defensiveness, or despair, and confirms the very alienation that sin has begun. A bone set roughly does not heal. Meekness is the only manner in which a real restoration can be accomplished, because it lets the fallen person remain a brother while being corrected.

(b) Meekness needs self-suspicion, because otherwise meekness is only a technique. A corrector who believes himself invulnerable can adopt a gentle tone, but it will be condescension—the gentleness of the healthy toward the sick, not of one patient toward another. Genuine meekness is rooted in the knowledge that "I could be where you are." "Considering thyself" is what turns gentleness from a manner into a truth about the relationship. It is also what keeps the corrector from the double sin Jesus warns of in Matthew 7:1–5: noticing the mote in a brother's eye while a beam sits in one's own.

(c) Self-suspicion needs restoration, because otherwise it becomes an excuse for inaction. If Paul had only said "consider yourself, lest you also be tempted," the natural conclusion would be: "Who am I to say anything?" But the command is to restore. Awareness of one's own weakness is not a reason to leave a brother in his fault; it is the qualification for helping him out of it rightly. Humility that refuses to act is not humility but neglect (cf. Lev 19:17, where failing to rebuke a neighbor is itself counted a failure of love).

(d) Restoration without self-suspicion turns into judgment. The one who forgets that he is himself a sinner will treat the fault as evidence about the other person's inferiority rather than about the common human condition. The correction then serves the corrector—his sense of righteousness, his position in the community—rather than the fallen. This is precisely the Galatian danger: a community tempted toward law-keeping as a mark of status, "desirous of vain glory, provoking one another, envying one another" (5:26), the verse immediately preceding. 6:1 is Paul's remedy: a form of moral seriousness that cannot be turned into a means of self-exaltation.

3. The underlying logic

Paul is insisting that the corrector and the corrected stand on the same ground. The only difference between them is that one has been "overtaken" today and the other has not been—yet. This is why the passage continues, "Bear ye one another's burdens" (6:2) and "if a man think himself to be something, when he is nothing, he deceiveth himself... let every man prove his own work" (6:3–4). Correction is a burden-bearing act done by someone who knows he is nothing in himself, and who is keeping watch over his own work while helping with his brother's.

The moral posture, then, is neither permissive tolerance (which would abandon the brother to his fault) nor superior judgment (which would abandon the corrector to his pride). It is the posture of a fellow-patient who has been given the skill to set a bone, who does it gently because he knows how much it hurts, and who checks his own limbs while he works because he knows he could fall on the same ground tomorrow. Restoration gives the act its purpose, meekness its manner, and self-suspicion its honesty; together they make correction an act of love rather than of power.
