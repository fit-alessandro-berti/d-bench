The power praised here is **kenotic power**—the power of self-emptying, of *not* grasping (*harpagmos*) what is rightfully one’s own, and instead pouring oneself out as gift. It is the power of the cross: not the ability to compel obedience from a throne, but the authority that flows from giving oneself away until there is nothing left to take.

This is a radical inversion of what we usually mean by “greatness.” In the ancient world—and in our own—power is *possession*: the capacity to seize, hold, and dominate. The text explicitly contrasts Christ’s divine prerogative (“equal with God”) with Adam’s sin of grasping (*harpazein*) at that equality. But Christ does not seize. He *empties* (*ekenōsen*). The “form of God” (*morphē theou*) is not abandoned but hidden within the “form of a servant” (*morphē doulou*). The power on display is not the power to make others kneel, but the power to kneel—and to remain there, even unto death.

**Why does this unsettle us more than domination?**

Because domination is *legible*. It follows a script we understand: the strong take, the weak submit, fear maintains the order. Open domination at least offers clarity—you know where you stand, and you know whom to fear or flatter. It is predictable, transactional, and ultimately brittle because it depends on violence or the threat of it.

But humility joined to greatness **collapses the map**. When power refuses to display itself as power, it becomes ungraspable. You cannot seize it, bargain with it, or control it. A God who could annihilate but instead washes feet is terrifying precisely because such love operates outside the economy of force. It is unpredictable. It makes no demands you can satisfy through submission; instead, it makes a claim on your entire being that cannot be negotiated.

Moreover, this kind of greatness **indicts us**. When we witness a humility that does not weaken but *transfigures*—that turns the cross into a throne—it exposes the pettiness of our own grasping. Our “legitimate” authorities, our status, our carefully guarded privileges suddenly look like the “robbery” the text warns against: desperate attempts to hold onto a power that was never ours to keep. The humble great one does not threaten our position; he reveals that we never truly possessed power in the first place, only the illusion of it.

Finally, this power is unsettling because it **demands conversion rather than compliance**. Domination asks for obedience; it can be resisted or obeyed from a distance. But the mind of Christ—the *phronēsis* of self-emptying love—asks us to become participants in the kenosis. It does not leave us spectators admiring a spectacle of virtue. It pulls us into the vulnerability, making us co-responsible for the world’s redemption. That is far more disturbing than any tyrant: a Lord who makes us partners in his weakness, who cannot save the world without our feeble, willing hands.

In short, open domination is frightening because it can hurt you. Humility joined to greatness is frightening because it can *change* you—and because it reveals that the only power worth having is the power to lay everything down.
