**External compliance changes what a person does; a “new heart” changes what moves them to do it.** Someone can keep a command out of fear, habit, or social pressure while remaining inwardly resistant to it. In Ezekiel, the heart is not just the seat of feeling; it is also where a person thinks, wills, and chooses. God’s promise is not that obedience will become unnecessary, but that it will come from a people made responsive to him. Verse 27 makes that link explicit: “I will put my spirit within you, and cause you to walk in my statutes.”

**Stone and flesh make that difference tangible.** A stone does not feel or respond; living flesh does. Here “flesh” is not a name for sinfulness but an image of living, responsive humanity. The violence of the contrast—taking one heart out and giving another—shows how deep the problem goes. Israel does not merely lack instructions; it already has them. What it lacks is a heart that responds to them. That is why the decisive verbs belong to God: “I will take away,” “I will give,” “I will put.” The promise is not simply that God will demand better behavior, but that he will give his people the inward capacity to live differently.
