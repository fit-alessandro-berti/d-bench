## The Treasury Metaphor

The controlling image is commercial and agricultural at once. The word translated "treasure" (*thesauros*) means a storehouse or vault — the heart as a repository of accumulated moral content. "Abundance" (*perisseuma*) suggests the *overflow*, the surplus that spills over the rim. And "bringeth forth" (*ekballō*, "casts out" or "puts forth") is active — not seeping, but drawing out and producing, like a hand withdrawing from a vault or a tree putting forth fruit.

So the picture is: an invisible storehouse, filled over time, from which a person actively draws what the mouth delivers.

## Words as Symptoms

Jesus' rhetorical question — "how can ye, being evil, speak good things?" — assumes a kind of causal necessity. The address "generation of vipers" is telling: a viper's venom comes from glands within; the mouth is merely the exit point. The Pharisees' slander ("he casts out demons by Beelzebub") wasn't an isolated verbal error but a fever revealing the disease. This is why the parallel in Luke 6:45 uses orchard imagery: thistles don't yield figs. On this reading, speech is diagnostic — the visible symptom of an invisible condition.

## Words as Revelations

The heart is a vault no one can see into. Speech is the only public access. The Pharisees' elaborate piety concealed their inner state from the crowd, but their words conducted an involuntary audit. This is the revelation function: the tongue as a window into the treasury, exposing what religious performance hides. Notably, it also works in the other direction — Jesus treats their blasphemy against the Spirit (12:31–32) as a *word-sin* that disclosed a *heart-state*, and verse 36 warns that even "idle words" will be entered into evidence at the judgment.

## Words as Choices

Yet the passage refuses to reduce speech to mere symptom. "Bringeth forth" is an act of agency — one draws from the treasury, selects, delivers. And the immediate context makes this unmistakable: verses 36–37 declare that people will be "justified" or "condemned *by thy words*." You cannot be condemned for a symptom. Words are conduct, and conduct is judged.

## All Three — and How They Cohere

The apparent tension (if words are symptoms, how can they be blameworthy? if choices, how is the "evil treasure" an excuse?) dissolves once you notice that the *treasure itself is accumulated*. A treasury implies deposits made over time. This suggests:

- **Past choices** filled the vault — the stockpile is the sediment of a history of decisions.
- **Present choices** draw from it — speech is free action, but exercised within the inventory available.
- **The drawing reveals the vault** — every utterance is simultaneously an act and a disclosure of what that history has deposited.

Words, then, are *chosen symptoms of chosen stockpiles*. All three models are facets of one picture.

## The Practical Implication

The passage's logic forbids fixing speech at the level of speech. If the mouth only dispenses what the heart has banked, then verbal self-control is treasury management — guarding the deposits (cf. Prov. 4:23, "out of it are the issues of life"; Matt. 6:21, "where your treasure is, there will your heart be also"). The question the text finally poses is not "What are you saying?" but "What have you been storing?"
