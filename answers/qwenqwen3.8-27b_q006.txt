The “self” Jesus commands people to deny is not the whole person—body, mind, gifts, emotions, relationships, or the person created in God’s image. It is the self that would make itself its own center, master, and savior: the “I” that says, *My life, my safety, my success, my comfort, my reputation, and my plans have the final say.*

In Mark’s context, this is especially clear. Just before this passage, Jesus says he must suffer, be rejected, be killed, and rise again. Peter responds with the human instinct: *God forbid, Lord! This shall not happen to you!* Jesus answers, “Get behind me, Satan!” Peter is not trying to follow Jesus; he is trying to rescue Jesus from the cross. That is exactly the “self” Jesus rejects in verse 34: the self that wants to avoid cost, preserve comfort, control the situation, and keep Christ on safer terms.

So the self being denied is:

- the self that wants to be safe above all;
- the self that wants to be in control;
- the self that defines worth by success, approval, status, or strength;
- the self that treats Christ as a means to one’s own happiness rather than as Lord;
- the self that would “save” itself by refusing the cross.

The word “life” in verse 35 is Greek *psychē*, often translated “soul” or “self.” The paradox is this: the person who tries to preserve his own life as the ultimate thing will lose it; the person who lets his own self be taken up on a cross for Christ’s sake and the gospel’s sake will find or save it. This is not a call to self-harm, self-abasement, or masochism. It is a call to transfer lordship from the self to Christ.

Why does this sound liberating to some?

Because the self is often a tyrant. Modern life puts enormous pressure on us to manage ourselves: achieve, perform, be seen, be liked, be independent, be successful, be happy on schedule. The self becomes a project we must constantly maintain. In that context, Jesus’ command can be heard as freedom. It says: *You do not have to carry yourself. You do not have to earn your worth. You do not have to make your life secure by your own efforts. You can stop worshipping your own survival, image, and autonomy.*

For some, the cross sounds like the end of self-management. The person who has been exhausted by pride, fear, ambition, social approval, or the need to control life may hear in this passage a release: if I die to myself, I can live by something larger than myself. The cross also gives suffering meaning. It says that pain, loss, humiliation, and even death are not necessarily meaningless; they can be taken up in the way of Christ, who was killed and raised.

That is why the same words can feel like liberation: they break the power of the self-idol.

Why, then, does it sound offensive to others?

First, modern Western culture often treats the self as precious in a way that makes “deny yourself” sound like an attack on self-worth, mental health, or personal dignity. If one’s culture says, “Love yourself,” “Believe in yourself,” “Put yourself first,” “Heal your wounds,” “Set boundaries,” and “Protect your peace,” then Jesus’ command can sound dangerous or self-negating. It can be heard as a call to shame, passivity, or self-destruction.

Second, the cross is genuinely offensive. In the first century, the cross was an instrument of shame and execution. To tell someone, “Take up your cross,” was to point toward possible suffering, persecution, and even martyrdom. That offends the human instinct to preserve life and avoid pain. It also offends religion that prefers a comfortable God, because this command ties discipleship to the crucified Messiah.

Third, these words have been misused. Throughout history, “deny yourself” and “take up your cross” have sometimes been twisted to justify abuse, excessive suffering, unrealistic martyrdom, silence in the face of injustice, or the crushing of legitimate self-care. For people who have been hurt by religion in that way, the passage can sound threatening rather than freeing.

So the same command can sound liberating or offensive depending on where a person stands. If a person is burdened by self-assertion, performance, fear, or the tyranny of self-protection, it may sound like freedom. If a person is threatened by loss of autonomy, has been abused in the name of religion, or lives in a culture that treats the self as the highest value, it may sound offensive.

A necessary distinction must be made: denying the self is not the same as neglecting the self. A person can care for their body, mind, relationships, and dignity while refusing to make the self an idol. Boundaries, rest, therapy, advocacy, and self-protection are not necessarily the opposite of Christ’s command. The issue is not “self” versus “no self.” The issue is self-worship versus Christ-worship.

In short: the self being denied is the self that would be its own God—the self that seeks to save itself by avoiding the cross. The self being saved is the self that finally belongs to God, is freed from the tyranny of self-preservation, and is drawn into the life of the crucified and risen Jesus.
