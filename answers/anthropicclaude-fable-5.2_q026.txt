**All three, though not equally and not at the same moment.** The passage uses two different images, and each supports a different reading.

## The two images

- **Overflow.** "Abundance" translates *perisseuma*, meaning surplus or what spills over. Here speech is not so much decided as leaked: whatever fills the heart comes out at the mouth.
- **Storehouse.** "Treasure" is *thesauros*, a treasury or storeroom. Here the person is an agent who "brings forth" (*ekballei*) goods from stock. That is a deliberate act, but one limited by what is on the shelves.

## How each reading fits

**Symptom.** The rhetorical question "how can ye, being evil, speak good things?" implies incapacity, not just reluctance. This matches the tree-and-fruit image in the preceding verse: fruit doesn't choose its kind. On this reading, bad speech is not the disease but its presentation.

**Revelation.** The heart is hidden, and speech makes it visible. "The tree is known by his fruit" (v. 33) is a claim about knowledge, with words as evidence. This explains why the next verses say people will give account for "every idle word." Careless, unguarded speech is the most revealing because it is least managed.

**Choice.** This is the subtlest of the three. The passage holds people accountable ("by thy words thou shalt be justified... condemned," v. 37), which makes little sense if words are purely involuntary. But the choice seems to lie mostly upstream. A treasury is accumulated over time, through what one dwells on, loves, and rehearses. By the time you speak, much of the choosing has already been done.

## The synthesis

The passage suggests a sequence: **you choose what you store, what you store determines what overflows, and what overflows reveals what you are.** Words are symptoms in their origin, revelations in their function, and choices in their accountability.

This also explains why the passage doesn't recommend simply policing one's speech. If the mouth only reports on the heart, editing the report changes nothing. The remedy implied by "make the tree good" (v. 33) is to change the inventory.

One tension remains. Jesus's opponents could presumably say pious-sounding things. So the claim isn't that evil people never produce good words, but that the heart eventually betrays itself, as theirs just had in attributing a healing to Beelzebub.
