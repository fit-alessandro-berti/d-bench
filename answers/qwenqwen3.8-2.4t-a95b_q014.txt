

The text says **both**, but its emphasis is closer to **treasure training the heart** than merely revealing it.

Jesus does not say, “Where your heart is, there your treasure will be.” He reverses the expected order:

> “Where your treasure is, there **will your heart be** also.”

That reversal is important. It suggests that treasure is not only a symptom of what we already love; it is also a means by which our love is formed. What we store, secure, and value repeatedly will eventually draw and shape the heart.

So the saying works like this:

- **Treasure reveals the heart** because what we choose to store shows what we truly value.
- **Treasure trains the heart** because our habits of valuing, keeping, and pursuing things teach our hearts where to love, trust, and dwell.

The heart follows treasure. Treasure becomes a kind of spiritual gravity.

---

## 1. Treasure reveals the heart

In biblical language, the “heart” is not merely the seat of emotions. It is the center of the person: thought, desire, will, loyalty, and moral orientation. So when Jesus speaks of the heart, he is asking: *What is the ruling affection of your life? What do you serve? What do you trust?*

“Treasure” is what we store up as valuable, secure, and worthy of future confidence. In the ancient world, treasure could include money, garments, grain, precious metals — things one kept for security or status. Jesus points out that earthly treasure is exposed to moth, rust, and thieves. It is fragile. It can decay or be taken.

Therefore, if someone’s treasure is on earth, that reveals that their security, hope, and desire are bound to perishable things. If someone’s treasure is in heaven, that reveals that their hope is bound to God and God’s kingdom.

In that sense, treasure is diagnostic. If you want to know what a person loves, look at what they protect, accumulate, spend toward, and secure for the future. Treasure exposes the heart’s allegiance.

---

## 2. Treasure trains the heart

But the saying is not only diagnostic. It is also formative.

Jesus commands:

> “Lay not up for yourselves treasures upon earth… but lay up for yourselves treasures in heaven…”

This implies that we are not simply to wait until our hearts are right. We are to redirect our treasure, because the heart will follow. The future tense is significant: “there **will** your heart be also.” The heart is not static. It moves toward what we invest in. It becomes attached to what we store.

This is why spiritual formation is never merely internal. Jesus connects the heart to concrete practices: giving, praying, fasting, seeking, serving. In the immediate context of Matthew 6, “treasure in heaven” is closely tied to acts done before the Father rather than before people:

- giving to the needy in secret,
- praying before the Father rather than for human admiration,
- fasting without ostentation,
- depending on God rather than anxious self-provision.

These practices do not merely express a heavenly-minded heart; they train one. Generosity teaches the heart to love God and neighbor. Secret devotion teaches the heart to desire God’s approval rather than human praise. Trust teaches the heart to rest in God rather than in wealth.

So treasure is a school for the heart. What we repeatedly choose to value forms what we love.

---

## 3. Possession: what we store becomes what we trust

The word “treasure” is tied to possession, but not in a simple sense of owning objects. Treasure is possession treated as **security**. It is what we keep for ourselves as a reserve against the future, a source of confidence, a sign of success, or a foundation for identity.

Earthly treasure says, implicitly:

> “My life is secured by what I can accumulate and protect.”

Heavenly treasure says:

> “My life is secured by God, and what matters most is what God keeps.”

Jesus does not condemn possession as such. He commands a relocation of treasure. The problem is not having things; the problem is making things into ultimate security. Earthly treasure becomes dangerous when it becomes the place where the heart seeks rest.

This is why Jesus contrasts earthly treasure with heavenly treasure so sharply:

- Earthly treasure can be corrupted.
- Earthly treasure can be stolen.
- Earthly treasure cannot bear the weight of the human heart.

Heavenly treasure, by contrast, is kept by God. It is not exposed to decay or theft. It is secure because it belongs to God’s economy rather than to the fragile economy of human control.

So possession becomes tied to the heart because what we possess as “treasure” is what we are really trusting. Possession is not neutral. It becomes spiritual when it becomes the place where we seek safety, significance, or future hope.

---

## 4. Attention: treasure draws the eye

Though “attention” is not named explicitly, it is deeply implied. Treasure draws attention. What we store, we watch. What we value, we think about. What we fear losing, we guard.

Earthly treasure produces anxious attention. Moth, rust, and thieves mean that earthly treasure must be monitored. It requires protection. It creates vigilance. It can make a person inwardly restless, suspicious, fearful, and preoccupied.

This connects directly to what follows in Matthew 6:

> “The light of the body is the eye…”  
> “No man can serve two masters…”  
> “Take no thought for your life, what ye shall eat…”

The surrounding passage is full of images of attention: the eye, the lamp, the master, worry. Treasure fixes the eye. Where treasure is placed, the gaze returns. And where the gaze returns, the heart learns to dwell.

So attention becomes the bridge between possession and love.

Possession says: “This is mine.”  
Attention says: “This is where I keep looking.”  
Love says: “This is where I belong.”

A person may think they merely own something, but if that thing constantly occupies their thoughts, fears, ambitions, and plans, it has begun to possess them. Treasure can become an owner of the heart.

Earthly treasure demands constant attention but gives no lasting rest. Heavenly treasure invites attention toward God and, because it is secure, allows the heart to rest.

---

## 5. Love: the heart becomes bound to what it treasures

Finally, love enters as the deepest movement of the heart. In Scripture, love is not only feeling. It is loyalty, devotion, attachment, and service. To love something is to be bound to it, to orient one’s life around it, to desire its good and one’s union with it.

Jesus says the heart will be where the treasure is. That means love follows value. What we consistently treat as valuable, our heart learns to love. What we consistently serve, our heart becomes attached to.

This prepares for Jesus’ later statement:

> “No man can serve two masters… Ye cannot serve God and mammon.”  
> — Matthew 6:24

“Mammon” is wealth treated as a master, a rival claimant to the devotion that belongs to God. The issue is not merely money; it is what commands the heart. Treasure becomes idolatrous when it becomes ultimate. It becomes holy when it is ordered toward God.

So love is tied to treasure because treasure is what competes for the heart’s ultimate loyalty. The heart cannot be everywhere. It will be “there” — where its treasure is.

If treasure is in perishable things, the heart becomes bound to what perishes.  
If treasure is in God and God’s kingdom, the heart becomes bound to what endures.

---

## 6. The chain: possession, attention, love

The passage ties these three together in a kind of spiritual chain:

1. **Possession** — What we “lay up” reveals and forms what we trust.
2. **Attention** — What we trust captures our gaze, concern, and mental energy.
3. **Love** — What continually holds our attention becomes the home of the heart.

Or in reverse:

- Love desires treasure.
- Treasure demands attention.
- Attention deepens love.

There is a cycle: we love what we value, we value what we pursue, and pursuing it makes us love it more. Jesus interrupts that cycle at a decisive point: **choose your treasure**. Do not wait passively for the heart to feel rightly. Put your treasure where it can train the heart rightly.

That is why the command is so practical. Jesus does not begin by saying, “Change your heart directly.” He says, “Lay up treasure in heaven.” He knows that the heart is formed by what we store, seek, and serve.

---

## 7. So: revelation or formation?

The best answer is:

> **Treasure reveals the heart as a symptom, and trains the heart as a school.**

It reveals because our treasure shows what we actually value. It trains because our treasure teaches our heart where to dwell, whom to trust, and what to love.

The text is not saying that the heart is neutral and treasure merely displays it. Nor is it saying that treasure mechanically forces the heart. Rather, treasure creates a gravitational field. The heart is drawn into it, shaped by it, and eventually bound to it.

Thus Jesus’ warning is both searching and merciful. It is searching because it asks: *What are you storing? What is your heart becoming attached to?* It is merciful because it invites us to place our treasure where it cannot decay, where it cannot be stolen, and where it will draw our hearts toward God rather than toward anxiety, greed, and loss.

In short:

> **What we possess captures our attention; what captures our attention forms our love; and what forms our love determines where our heart truly lives.**
