The parable itself gives the key in its introduction:

> “...unto certain which **trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and despised others**”  
> — Luke 18:9

So the deepest thing Jesus condemns is **self-righteousness**: a religious pride that trusts in its own moral and ritual performance before God, and that expresses itself in contempt toward another person. The other things you name — comparison, self-display, misuse of virtue — are all present, but they flow from that root.

## 1. What exactly condemns the Pharisee?

It is not that he fasts. It is not that he tithes. Jesus does not say, “This man was wrong to fast,” or, “This man was wrong to tithe.” Those were legitimate acts of Jewish piety. The problem is what those acts have become in his heart.

### a. He trusts in himself

The text says the parable was addressed to people who “trusted in themselves that they were righteous.” That is the central issue.

The Pharisee approaches God as though he has a claim upon God. His prayer is not really a request for mercy. It is a spiritual résumé:

> “God, I thank thee, that I am not as other men are... I fast twice in the week, I give tithes of all that I possess.”

He is not saying, “God, have mercy on me.”  
He is saying, in effect, “God, look at me. Look at how good I am.”

His religion has become a system of self-justification. He does not stand before God as a sinner needing grace. He stands as someone who believes he has earned his standing.

### b. He despises others

The introduction says these people “despised others.” The Pharisee demonstrates this immediately:

> “...or even as this publican.”

He does not merely thank God that he is morally serious. He defines his righteousness by comparing himself negatively with another person — and not just any person, but the tax collector, someone already despised socially and religiously.

This is important: the Pharisee’s sin is not only vertical, toward God. It is also horizontal, toward his neighbor. His piety does not make him merciful. It makes him contemptuous.

That is a major clue that something is deeply wrong. True righteousness in Scripture does not produce scorn for sinners. It produces grief over sin, fear of God, and mercy toward others.

### c. He uses virtue as self-display

The Pharisee’s prayer is addressed outwardly to God, but its real center is himself. Some translations bring out the force of the Greek by saying he “prayed to himself” or “prayed about himself.” Even if one does not press that phrase too far, the point is clear: his prayer is self-absorbed.

He turns thanksgiving into boasting. Instead of saying, “God, thank you for preserving me and helping me,” he says, “I am not like other people.” His virtue has become a mirror in which he admires himself.

That is why “use of virtue as self-display” is definitely part of the problem. He has taken good religious practices and made them into badges of personal superiority.

### d. He compares himself by the wrong standard

The Pharisee measures himself against “other men” — extortioners, unjust men, adulterers — and especially against the publican. But he is not measuring himself directly against the holiness of God.

That is why he feels secure. If your standard is “better than that tax collector,” you can feel righteous without ever confronting the deeper demands of God’s law: humility, mercy, love, truth, justice, and the fear of God.

Jesus elsewhere rebukes this kind of religion:

> “Ye Pharisees make clean the outside of the cup and the platter; but your inward part is full of ravening and wickedness.”  
> — Luke 11:39

And:

> “Ye tithe mint and rue and all manner of herbs, and pass over judgment and the love of God.”  
> — Luke 11:42

The Pharisee’s problem is not merely that he does religious things. It is that he uses religious things to avoid the deeper matter of the heart.

### e. His “doctrine” is functionally false

You ask whether it is false doctrine. In one sense, the Pharisee does not deny God. He thanks God. He believes in fasting, tithing, temple worship, and moral obedience. So he is not condemned here for formal heresy.

But his theology is deeply distorted. He assumes that God accepts people on the basis of their religious performance. He treats God as a creditor who owes him approval because of his moral achievements.

That is a false view of justification, even if it is not expressed as a theological proposition. He lives as though he can justify himself. He does not come seeking mercy. He comes expecting vindication.

So if we must summarize the Pharisee’s condemnation in one phrase, it is this:

> He trusted in his own righteousness before God and despised his neighbor.

Or even more simply:

> He exalted himself.

Jesus says as much at the end of the parable:

> “Every one that exalteth himself shall be abased; and he that humbleth himself shall be exalted.”  
> — Luke 18:14

The Pharisee exalted himself by making himself the center of his prayer, the standard of his righteousness, and the judge of another man. That is what condemns him.

## 2. Why is the tax collector’s posture spiritually truer?

The tax collector does three things that reveal the truth of his heart.

### a. He recognizes his distance from God

> “And the publican, standing afar off...”

He does not come forward confidently. He does not assume he has a place of honor. He stands at a distance, like someone who knows he is unworthy to draw near.

This is not a prescribed bodily posture for all prayer. The Bible also speaks of boldness before God through mercy. But here the posture expresses a spiritual reality: he feels the weight of his sin in the presence of the holy God.

### b. He will not even lift his eyes

> “...would not lift up so much as his eyes unto heaven...”

Again, this is not a rule that we must always pray with downcast eyes. But in this case, his body tells the truth. He is ashamed. He is not performing humility. He is actually burdened.

His posture says, “I have no claim. I have no righteousness to present. I cannot even look up as though I were clean.”

That is spiritually true because it matches reality. He really is a sinner. He really needs mercy. He really has nothing to offer God as leverage.

### c. He asks for mercy

> “God be merciful to me a sinner.”

This is the heart of the parable. The Greek word behind “be merciful” is related to the idea of propitiation or atonement. He is not merely saying, “God, feel sorry for me.” He is appealing to God’s mercy, to God’s way of dealing graciously with sinners.

He does not say:

- “God, I am better than others.”
- “God, look at my good deeds.”
- “God, I will do better if you give me another chance.”
- “God, judge me according to my sincerity.”

He says:

> “God, be merciful to me, the sinner.”

He identifies himself honestly. He does not soften the word. He does not say, “I have made mistakes.” He says, “I am a sinner.”

That is spiritually truer than the Pharisee’s prayer because it tells the truth before God. The Pharisee’s prayer is full of illusion. The tax collector’s prayer is full of honesty.

## 3. Why does the tax collector go home justified?

Jesus says:

> “I tell you, this man went down to his house justified rather than the other.”

“Justified” here means declared righteous, accepted, acquitted before God. The shocking thing in the story is that the one who goes home justified is not the morally impressive religious man, but the despised sinner who throws himself on mercy.

Why?

Because God does not justify people on the basis of self-achieved righteousness. God justifies those who come to Him repentantly, acknowledging their need for mercy.

The Pharisee asks for nothing, so he receives nothing.  
The Pharisee confesses nothing, so he is forgiven nothing.  
The Pharisee presents his own righteousness, so God leaves him to it.

The tax collector brings nothing but his sin and his plea for mercy — and God accepts him.

This does not mean the tax collector is justified because his humility is a superior virtue. That would turn humility into another work, and the parable would collapse into a different kind of Pharisaism: “God, thank you that I am not proud like that Pharisee.”

No, the tax collector is justified because he receives mercy. His humility is not a merit. It is an empty hand. It is the posture of someone who stops defending himself and casts himself on God.

## 4. So which of your options is the answer?

You listed several possibilities: false doctrine, pride, comparison, contempt, or the use of virtue as self-display.

The best answer is:

> The Pharisee is condemned for self-righteous pride before God, which expresses itself in comparison, contempt for others, and the use of virtue as self-display.

More specifically:

- **False doctrine?** Not primarily as an explicit doctrinal error, but he holds a false theology of merit in practice.
- **Pride?** Yes. This is the root sin. He exalts himself before God.
- **Comparison?** Yes. He measures himself by another man rather than by God.
- **Contempt?** Yes. The text says such people “despised others,” and the Pharisee scorns the tax collector.
- **Use of virtue as self-display?** Yes. His fasting and tithing become grounds for boasting rather than expressions of devotion.

But if one wants the sharpest biblical summary, it is this:

> He trusted in himself and despised others.

That is why Jesus concludes:

> “Every one that exalteth himself shall be abased; and he that humbleth himself shall be exalted.”  
> — Luke 18:14

## 5. The deepest contrast

The parable contrasts two kinds of religion.

The Pharisee’s religion says:

> “I am righteous because of what I do, and therefore I am better than others.”

The tax collector’s prayer says:

> “I am undone, and my only hope is God’s mercy.”

The Pharisee is full of himself.  
The tax collector is empty before God.

The Pharisee presents achievements.  
The tax collector presents a wound.

The Pharisee asks for approval.  
The tax collector asks for mercy.

The Pharisee leaves still in his sins, because he never admitted he needed forgiveness.  
The tax collector leaves justified, because God gives mercy to the humble.

So the tax collector’s posture is spiritually truer because it corresponds to the way God actually relates to human beings. God does not receive us because we have made ourselves impressive. He receives us when we come honestly, repentantly, and dependent upon His mercy.
