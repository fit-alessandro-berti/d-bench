# Trust Without Surrendering the Mind: Proverbs 3:5–6

## What the Words Suggest

- **"Trust" (*batach*)** carries the sense of security, of resting your full weight on something. It describes relational reliance, not assent to a proposition.
- **"Lean" (*sha'an*)** means to support oneself on something, like a staff or a wall. The image is of what bears your weight when you stand.
- **"Understanding" (*binah*)** is discernment, the capacity to judge between things. Proverbs elsewhere praises it lavishly.
- **"Acknowledge" (*yada*)** is simply "know." In all your ways, *know him*. This is intimate, ongoing awareness, not a ritual nod.
- **"Direct" (*yashar*)** means to make straight or level. It promises a path that is true, not necessarily one that is easy or fully explained in advance.

The contrast, then, is not *thinking versus believing*. It is about **what you lean on**: God, or your own discernment as a self-sufficient, final authority.

## Why This Cannot Be Anti-Intellectualism

The surrounding book makes an anti-intellectual reading nearly impossible:

- "Get wisdom, get understanding" (4:5, 7). The very word *binah* is commanded as a treasure to pursue.
- "If thou seekest her as silver, and searchest for her as for hid treasures" (2:4). Wisdom demands effort, study, and searching.
- "The fear of the Lord is the *beginning* of knowledge" (1:7). Reverence is the foundation of knowledge, not its ceiling.
- "In the multitude of counsellors there is safety" (11:14; 15:22). Guidance often comes through deliberation, consultation, and evidence.

The key word in 3:5 is **"thine own."** Verse 7 makes it explicit: "Be not wise *in thine own eyes*." The target is intellectual *autonomy* and *self-enclosure*, not intellect.

## The Kind of Trust Being Asked For

It is:

1. **Wholehearted.** "All thine heart" means the whole inner person, including mind, will, and desire. You bring your reasoning into the trust rather than checking it at the door.
2. **Comprehensive.** "All thy ways" covers business, family, speech, money, and plans, not just "spiritual" matters. Proverbs is a notoriously practical book.
3. **Relational.** The trust is in a *person* who is known, not in a formula or an institution's pronouncements.
4. **Weight-bearing.** When your analysis runs out, or conflicts with what God has made known, your security rests on him rather than on your cleverness or control.

## Humble Self-Distrust vs. Anti-Intellectualism

| Humble self-distrust | Anti-intellectualism |
|---|---|
| Thinks hard, but holds conclusions as correctable | Treats not thinking as a virtue |
| Knows reason is finite and bent by desire, fear, and pride | Assumes thinking itself is the danger |
| Seeks counsel, evidence, and correction | Avoids questions that might unsettle |
| Submits its judgments to a higher wisdom | Submits to its own gut or group, labeled "faith" |
| Can say "I was wrong" | Uses "trust God" to end conversations |
| Studies *because* truth belongs to God | Fears study *as if* truth might threaten God |

**Humble self-distrust** is a realistic assessment of the self. My perspective is limited (I can't see the future or the whole picture), my motives are mixed (I tend to reason toward what I already want), and my judgment is shaped by circumstance. So I don't make myself the final court of appeal. This is actually an *intellectual* virtue, the same one that makes good scientists and scholars: awareness of bias and openness to correction.

**Anti-intellectualism** is often a counterfeit of this humility. It sounds pious ("don't overthink it, just trust") but frequently amounts to *leaning on one's own understanding* in disguise. It trusts one's instincts, preferences, or tribe and simply refuses to examine them. Ironically, the person who won't think is often the one most "wise in his own eyes," because he has insulated his views from any challenge.

## A Useful Test

Ask what your trust does *to* your thinking:

- Does it make you **more** willing to learn, listen, weigh evidence, and admit error? That is the humility of Proverbs 3.
- Does it make you **less** willing, turning "faith" into a shield against inconvenient facts or hard questions? That is self-trust wearing religious clothing.

## In Summary

The verse asks you to **use** your understanding without **leaning** on it, to think as a dependent creature rather than an autonomous authority. This is the classic stance later summarized as *fides quaerens intellectum*, "faith seeking understanding" (Anselm, drawing on Augustine). Faith is not the replacement for understanding but the posture in which understanding is pursued. The mind is not abandoned. It is put in its proper place: working hard, and resting its weight on God.
