# The Power of Kenosis

The passage Paul quotes here (likely an early Christian hymn, older than the letter itself) is built on a deliberate symmetry: *form of God* → *form of a servant*; equality with God → death on a cross. The Greek word behind "made himself of no reputation" is *ekenōsen* — he emptied himself — and the hymn is careful to define the emptying by what follows the "but": he emptied himself *by taking* the form of a servant. The descent is not subtraction but redirection. He did not pour out his divinity; he poured himself out into the lowest place divinity could go.

## What kind of power is praised here?

Not power as capacity, but power as *renunciation of the use of capacity*. The crucial phrase is "thought it not robbery" — *harpagmon*, something to be seized, plunder to be exploited. The claim is that equality with God was not treated as an asset to be leveraged. Domination is easy: anyone with strength can seize. What is praised here is the one thing strength cannot do by itself — suspend itself, freely, for the sake of those below it.

Three features distinguish this power:

**It reveals rather than forfeits greatness.** The hymn's logic is not "despite being God, he humbled himself" but "his humility shows what God is." If the Son is the exact imprint of the divine nature, then the cross is not a detour from divinity but its disclosure. The unsettling theological claim buried here: omnipotence's truest expression is the capacity to be held, struck, and killed — strength made perfect in weakness.

**It reproduces itself rather than subjugating others.** Domination produces subjects; this power produces imitators. "Let this mind be in you" — the passage does not ask for worship of an achievement but recruitment into a pattern. It works like a seed, not a scepter: it spreads by being copied, not by being obeyed.

**It is vindicated, not self-asserted.** The verses just beyond this quotation (2:9–11) supply the hinge: *therefore* God exalted him. The exaltation is not seized; it is conferred. The descent is real — all the way to a slave's death, the most shameful execution Rome had — and the ascent belongs to Another. The power being praised is power that can afford to wait.

## Why is humility joined to greatness more unsettling than open domination?

Because domination can be located, opposed, and morally condemned — and this cannot.

**It removes your position.** A tyrant lets you stand somewhere: below him, but morally above him. You retain the victim's high ground. But someone who is genuinely great and genuinely humble takes that ground away. His renunciation makes your grasping visible by contrast. The cross functions as a mirror: you cannot look at it without seeing the difference between his self-disposal and your self-protection. Domination takes your freedom; sacrificial greatness makes a claim on your heart — and convicts you if you withhold it. It demands not compliance but *transformation*, and that is a far more total demand.

**It escapes every form of leverage.** We manage people through the economy of exchange — threats, bribes, flattery, status. A dominator wants something you can give or withhold. But someone who has already accepted death, already renounced reputation, has stepped outside that economy entirely. There is no handle on him. This is why the passage can feel almost frightening in its freedom: a person you cannot buy, threaten, or promote is sovereign in a way tyrants only pretend to be.

**It breaks the script.** We know how power behaves: it strikes back, it clutches, it ascends. Kenotic power refuses the expected move, and the refusal of a script is inherently unnerving — like a fighter who wins by yielding. The hymn inverts every hierarchy of value (descent as the road to exaltation, the slave's death as the throne of glory), and an inverted map leaves the reader without bearings.

**It invites a suspicion that can never fully close.** Nietzsche's objection is the sharpest form of this: perhaps this humility is the deepest power move of all — the weak conquering the strong by moral inversion, a "will to power" so formidable it captured Rome itself. The suspicion is genuinely hard to dismiss, because strategic self-abasement does exist and false humility is common. What is striking is that even the hostile reading testifies to the passage's potency: the unsettling feeling is precisely that this might be *more* powerful than domination, not less — that it conquers by dying rather than by killing, and that nothing in our ordinary arsenal answers it.

**It collapses the distance we need.** God on a throne can be managed from below; hierarchy, however cruel, preserves our position. But a king who kneels to wash feet — who enters our shame and takes the slave's death in our place — abolishes the distance. Intimacy is more exposing than rule. The tyrant sees your obedience; the crucified one has seen the worst of us from inside it, and stayed.

## The final unsettling move

The passage does not leave this power as a spectacle to admire. "Let this mind be in you" converts description into summons. The reader is not permitted to remain an observer of the kenosis — the hymn ends by asking whether you will empty yourself too. That is perhaps the deepest reason it unsettles: open domination asks only that you submit. This asks that you *follow*.
