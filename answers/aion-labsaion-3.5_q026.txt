# The Treasury of the Heart: Speech as Overflow

## The Metaphor Itself

Jesus uses two related economic images: *abundance* (Greek *perisseuma*, "surplus/overflow") and *treasure* (*thesauros*, a storehouse or stockpile). The picture is hydraulic and fiscal at once: the heart is a reservoir that has been filled over time, and the mouth is simply the spigot. Speech doesn't *create* one's moral contents—it *displaces* them. What comes out was already in.

Notably, *thesauros* is the same word Jesus uses in Matthew 6:19–21 ("lay up for yourselves treasures in heaven... for where your treasure is, there will your heart be also"). The two passages form a matched pair: you become what you stockpile, and your speech audits the account.

## Words as Symptoms

The rhetorical question—"how can ye, being evil, speak good things?"—asserts a kind of incapacity. This is the diagnostic logic of the viper image (echoing John the Baptist in 3:7): a serpent's bite expresses venom that was manufactured and stored in glands it never chose to have. Words, in this register, are like a pulse or a fever—readings of an internal condition the speaker did not consciously author in the moment.

## Words as Choices

Yet the passage cannot stop there, and its context won't allow it. The Pharisees' accusation that Jesus cast out demons by Beelzebub was not an involuntary reflex—it was a deliberate interpretive decision, a strategic slander. And the verses immediately following (12:36–37) make speech legally consequential: "every idle word... by thy words thou shalt be justified, and by thy words thou shalt be condemned." Words are treated as *deeds* with moral weight, subject to judgment. A mere symptom isn't arraigned; an act is.

## Words as Revelations

The "treasure" is by definition hidden—concealed in the heart. The mouth is the aperture through which the vault's contents become public. Speech here has an epistemic function: it discloses what neither the speaker nor the hearer could otherwise inspect. This is why Jesus treats the Pharisees' words as evidence admissible against them—their slander *revealed* a settled orientation that their religious performance had been concealing.

## All Three — In a Feedback Loop

The passage's genius is that it refuses to let these three categories separate. The relationship is diachronic:

1. **Past choices accumulate into treasure.** The storehouse is stocked through a history of decisions, loves, and habits—what the virtue ethic tradition would call *hexis* or *habitus*, settled character.
2. **The treasure determines the overflow.** What has been accumulated sets the horizon of what can be sincerely spoken; the evil heart *cannot* produce genuinely good fruit.
3. **Each act of speech is a new choice that re-deposits into the treasury.** Every word both draws down the account and adds to it.

So words are revelatory symptoms of accumulated character *and* accountable choices that further form that character. The symptom and the act are two faces of one process. This also carries a sober implication for self-knowledge: the mouth is the one window into one's own vault. If you want to know what you've been stockpiling, listen to what spills out when you're tired, provoked, or unguarded—the abundance always finds the exit.
