John treats love for the visible brother/neighbor as a test of love for the invisible God because, in his theology, love is not a private feeling or a verbal claim; it becomes concrete in action. God is invisible, so a claim like “I love God” can easily remain abstract, self-directed, and unverifiable. But the neighbor is visible, particular, and inconvenient. There the reality of one’s love is exposed.

Several things are at work:

1. **God’s command is concrete.** Love of God cannot be separated from obedience to His command to love one another. To hate someone God loves while claiming to love God is a contradiction.

2. **The visible is the arena of the invisible.** The invisible God is not loved by a mystical feeling that bypasses His visible image and His visible people. Love for God is embodied in how one treats the brother or neighbor actually present.

3. **The immediate point is especially sharp.** In 1 John, “brother” primarily means a fellow believer. To hate a member of God’s family while claiming love for the Father is incoherent: one claims to love the Father but despises His children.

4. **This is not salvation by neighbor-love.** John is not saying that loving the neighbor earns God’s love. Rather, love for the brother is the fruit and evidence of abiding in God, who first loved us (1 John 4:7–12, 19).

The self-deception being exposed is **religious verbalism and false assurance**: saying or feeling “I love God” while the life is marked by hatred, contempt, unforgiveness, prejudice, or indifference toward real people. It is the illusion that one can love an invisible God while avoiding the costly, concrete demands of loving visible human beings. John calls such a person a liar because the claim contradicts the life. The verse exposes a piety that uses God as an abstraction to evade the neighbor—loving an idea of God while hating His image or His children.
