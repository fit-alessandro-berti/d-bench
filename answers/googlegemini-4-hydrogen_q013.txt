The passage rejects worship so violently—using words like *hate*, *despise*, and *noise*—because the worship in question is not merely **incomplete**; it is **counterfeit and complicit**. Asking for “better balance” would imply that the rituals had positive value on their own and simply needed a bit more charity added to them. Instead, the prophet presents this worship as actively offensive to God.

### Why Such a Sharp Rejection Instead of a Call for Balance?

1. **The offerings are funded by injustice.**  
   Throughout the book of Amos, the wealthy elite of Israel are condemned for crushing the poor, rigging the courts, taking bribes, and exploiting the needy (e.g., Amos 2:6–7; 5:11–12; 8:4–6). The “fat beasts,” grain offerings, and lavish festivals are financed directly by that exploitation. For God to accept those sacrifices would be akin to accepting stolen goods or blood money—it would make God complicit in the oppression.

2. **Worship was being used as a substitute for obedience.**  
   A call for “balance” assumes people are trying to do both and falling short. In Amos, the people were using extravagant ritual to *replace* ethical living. They treated liturgy as a shield against accountability, assuming that because their shrines were packed and their offerings expensive, God was obligated to bless and protect them (Amos 5:14, 18). Only total rejection can shatter that illusion of religious security.

3. **Justice is the prerequisite, not an add-on.**  
   Verse 24 contrasts sporadic religious spectacles with an ever-flowing river (“a mighty stream”—literally, a perennial wadi that never runs dry). Justice (*mishpat*) and righteousness (*tsedaqah*) are not one side of a scale to be balanced against temple worship on the other. In the prophetic tradition, ethical treatment of one's neighbor is the very foundation that makes worship valid; without it, the singing is just empty noise.

### What Kind of Moral and Spiritual Fraud Is Being Exposed?

* **Transactional / “Bribery” Religion:**  
  Treating God like a corrupt official or pagan deity who can be bought off with flattery, music, and expensive meat. It reduces worship to a mechanical transaction: *we give God impressive ceremonies, and God overlooks our cruelty and guarantees our prosperity.*

* **Compartmentalization (The Sacred–Secular Split):**  
  Dividing life into a “religious sphere” (feast days, assemblies, hymns) and a “real-world sphere” (business, law courts, treatment of the poor), while pretending God only cares about what happens in the former. The text exposes the fraud of claiming deep devotion to a God whose core character—justice and mercy—is violated as soon as the worship service ends.

* **Performative Piety Masking Systemic Oppression:**  
  The fraud isn’t just individual hypocrisy; it is cultural self-deception. Loud songs and crowded festivals create an atmosphere of fervent spirituality that convinces both the worshipers and onlookers that the society is godly. The passage strips away the aesthetic beauty of the liturgy to show what it actually is when divorced from justice: a pious smokescreen for a predatory social order.
