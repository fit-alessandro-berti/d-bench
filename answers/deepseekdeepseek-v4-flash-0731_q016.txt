The elder brother’s deepest problem is **self-righteousness — a heart that has reduced sonship to a wage.**  

His anger, envy, and sense of wounded fairness are all symptoms of that root issue. He says, “I serve you... I never disobeyed... you never gave me a kid” — as if obedience were a contract that obligates the father to reward him. He has, in his mind, earned his place by performance. He is not living as a son but as a hired servant.

So when the father shows mercy to the younger son, the elder brother is not merely angry at the waste or the feast. He is **uncomfortable with mercy itself**, because mercy threatens his whole system of earned reward. If the father freely forgives the prodigal, then the elder brother’s “faithful service” has not actually purchased anything. Grace makes his self-righteous debt-collecting obsolete.

The father’s reply exposes the real problem:  
> “Son, you are always with me, and all that I have is yours.”

He already had the father, the home, the inheritance — but he didn’t know, love, or trust his father as a son. His deepest problem is not that he obeyed too much, but that he **obeyed without love and judged without mercy**. He wanted the father’s gifts, not his heart. In short: **his need was not a better reward; it was to understand grace.**
