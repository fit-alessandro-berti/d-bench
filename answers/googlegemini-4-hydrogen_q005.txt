Matthew 5:44–45 is one of the most counterintuitive demands in ethical history. Even among those who revere the text, it is often quietly treated as an impossible ideal, a poetic exaggeration, or a rule meant only for saints. 

Understanding why it is so hard to take seriously—and what kind of ethic it actually represents—requires separating cultural caricatures of "love" from the radical claim the passage is making.

---

### 1. Why Enemy-Love Is So Difficult to Take Seriously

**It violates our deepest evolutionary and psychological wiring.**
Human beings are wired for reciprocity and threat defense. When someone harms us, our nervous system triggers fight-or-flight, and our moral instincts demand *lex talionis*—an eye for an eye, or at least proportional pushback. Fairness feels like a baseline of survival: you cooperate with cooperators and punish defectors. Enemy-love asks a person to override survival instincts and refuse the primal satisfaction of retaliation.

**It seems to insult justice.**
At a moral level, enemy-love can feel offensive to victims. If someone commits cruelty or injustice, telling the harmed party to "love" and "bless" the perpetrator sounds dangerously close to excusing evil, erasing accountability, or demanding that the vulnerable become doormats. Real-world experience also warns us that unguarded kindness toward predators often invites further exploitation.

**Modern language equates "love" with warm feelings.**
In English, "love" usually implies affection, liking, or approval. Commanding someone to feel warm affection toward an abuser or a bitter rival isn't just difficult—it is psychologically incoherent. As long as enemy-love is heard as an emotional requirement ("try to feel fond of people who hurt you"), people naturally dismiss it as unrealistic.

---

### 2. Sentimental, Strategic, or a Deeper Moral Strength?

#### Why it is *not* sentimental
Sentimentality ignores harsh reality in favor of comforting feelings. This passage does the exact opposite: it explicitly names *enemies*, *cursing*, *hatred*, and *persecution*. There is no illusion that enemies are secretly nice people. 

In the Greek New Testament, the word used here is *agape*—not romantic attraction (*eros*) or warm friendship (*philia*), but an active, willed commitment to another person's well-being. Notice that Jesus immediately translates "love" into concrete actions: *bless*, *do good*, *pray*. You don't have to like an enemy or invite them into your home to refuse to wish them ruin. Far from being soft or naive, it assumes real hostility and demands discipline in the face of it.

#### Is it strategic?
There is undeniably a strategic dimension to enemy-love. Thinkers and leaders like Mahatma Gandhi and Martin Luther King Jr. demonstrated that refusing to mirror an oppressor’s hatred can expose injustice, disarm moral complacency, and break multigenerational cycles of vengeance. Psychologically and socially, retaliation usually escalates conflict; unilateral goodwill can interrupt it.

However, reducing enemy-love to a *strategy* misses the core of Matthew 5:45. A strategy is judged by whether it works: "I will be kind to my enemy so that they soften, convert, or lose power." What happens when the enemy does *not* change, or responds with greater cruelty? 

Jesus grounds the command not in tactical payoff, but in the nature of God: *"for he maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good, and sendeth rain on the just and on the unjust."* The sun and rain are given regardless of whether the recipient becomes grateful or righteous. The ethic isn't transactional; it doesn't depend on the enemy's response to be valid.

#### A deeper form of moral strength
Taken seriously, enemy-love is best understood as a profound form of moral strength—specifically, **sovereignty over one's own character**:

* **Refusing to be shaped by your enemy:** Hatred is reactive; it allows the person who wronged you to dictate who you become. By returning cruelty for cruelty, you let your enemy remake you in their image. Enemy-love is the strength to act from your own moral center rather than being pulled into a mirror-image of their hostility.
* **Separating boundaries from malice:** Serious interpreters (from Augustine to Dietrich Bonhoeffer to King) have noted that loving an enemy does not mean approving of their actions, surrendering justice, or submitting passively to abuse. You can resist someone’s harmful actions firmly—even physically or legally—while refusing to dehumanize them or desire their ultimate destruction. In fact, resisting someone's evil can be part of willing their good.
* **Operating from abundance rather than scarcity:** Retaliation treats dignity and power as a zero-sum game ("if you hurt me, I must diminish you to restore myself"). The metaphor of sun and rain portrays a goodness so secure in itself that it doesn't need to hoard blessing as a weapon or a reward only for allies.

### Summary
Enemy-love is hard to take seriously because it clashes with instinct, looks reckless in a cruel world, and gets confused with weak sentimentality. Yet stripped of illusions, it isn't soft feeling or merely a clever tactic—it is the radical refusal to let another person's malice strip you of your own humanity.
