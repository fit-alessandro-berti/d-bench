## Short answer
It does all of these things except forbid judgment. What it does at a deeper level is change the question from *"Is she guilty?"* to *"Who is fit to be the instrument of punishment?"*

## What it doesn't do: abolish judgment
Jesus never says the act wasn't sin. He ends with "Go, and sin no more," so the moral category stays intact. The sentence regulates the *standing of the judge*, not the existence of wrongdoing. Read as "never judge anyone," it would contradict the rest of the scene and John 7:24 ("judge righteous judgment").

## What it does do

**1. Exposes a trap and its hypocrisy.** The woman is being used as a prop to catch Jesus between Mosaic law and Roman authority. The man involved is conspicuously absent, though the law names both parties. The accusers' zeal is already selective, so the sentence makes visible what was hidden: they aren't really concerned with justice.

**2. Breaks up mob dynamics.** A crowd diffuses responsibility. "Let him first cast" turns a collective into individuals, each of whom must ask, *"Am I the one who does this?"* The mob dissolves, not by argument but by forcing private self-examination. That the elders leave first suggests that longer self-knowledge makes self-deception harder.

**3. Turns the law against its misuse.** Deuteronomy 17:7 has the witnesses throw first, which makes them stake their own integrity on the charge. Jesus takes the law's logic seriously and asks whether these witnesses can bear that weight.

## The deeper disruption

- **It moves moral authority from the act to the agent.** Most moral systems ask whether the verdict is correct. Jesus asks what relationship the punisher has to the same failing. Even a correct verdict can be delivered in a corrupt way, as a means of self-exoneration.
- **It can't be rebutted on its own terms.** To reject it, you have to claim you qualify. Every objection becomes a confession.
- **It restores the woman as a person.** She has been treated as a case and a symbol. Afterward Jesus speaks to her directly. The phrase "Neither do I condemn you" comes *before* "sin no more," so mercy precedes the demand rather than being its reward.
- **It exposes the psychology of condemnation.** Punishing others is often a way to reassure ourselves that we belong on the right side of the line. The sentence threatens that comfort.

## The real difficulty
Taken as a universal rule, it seems to dissolve courts, since no judge is sinless. But the scene isn't legislating institutions. It's rebuking a particular posture: self-righteous, instrumental condemnation. The challenge is to tell *judging as a role* (necessary, accountable) from *judging as self-elevation* (corrosive). It can also be misused as a *tu quoque* silencer ("who are you to criticize?"), which Jesus himself doesn't do, since he names sin while refusing to condemn.

## One more wrinkle
Many scholars note this passage is missing from the earliest manuscripts and floats around in others. Whatever its origin, the story was kept because it captures something the tradition couldn't bring itself to lose: truth and mercy held together, with the one holding the stone required to be honest first.
