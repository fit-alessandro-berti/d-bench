It is **both**, but in context it is less a detached theory that people are hopelessly bad than a warning not to treat the human heart as its own trustworthy judge.

In Jeremiah 17, the issue is where people place their trust: the passage contrasts the person who relies on human strength with the person who trusts the Lord. “Heart” here means more than feelings; it includes thought, will, and intention. The heart can mislead its owner—so “Who can know it?”—but verse 10 answers that God can: he searches the heart and judges people by their ways and their deeds. The passage therefore warns against self-deception while locating final judgment with God.

That doesn’t mean we can never speak about motives, or that everyone’s motives are equally corrupt. It means we should speak **humbly and provisionally**:

- Describe what you can observe, and distinguish it from what you infer: “You did X” is firmer than “You did it because you wanted Y.”
- Allow for mixed motives and incomplete self-knowledge: “I meant to help, but I may also have wanted approval.”
- Test an account of motives against patterns, consequences, Scripture, and honest feedback—not only against how it feels from the inside.
- Avoid using the verse to claim certainty about another person’s hidden intentions. Jeremiah’s point is not that people get to pronounce judgment on one another’s hearts; it is that God sees what we cannot.

So the passage is realistic about self-deception, with a sober view of human nature. Its practical effect should be neither cynicism nor paralysis, but humility: speak truthfully about what you intend, while staying open to the possibility that your own account is incomplete.
