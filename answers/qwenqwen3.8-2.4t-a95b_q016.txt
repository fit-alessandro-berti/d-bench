

The elder brother’s **deepest problem is discomfort with mercy itself**—but that discomfort is rooted in **self-righteous, transactional obedience**.

In other words:

- **Anger** is the visible symptom.
- **Envy** appears when he complains, “You never gave me a kid.”
- **Wounded fairness** is the language he uses to justify his resentment.
- **Pride in obedience** is the ground on which he stands: “These many years I have served you, and never disobeyed you.”
- But the deepest issue is that **he cannot rejoice in the father’s mercy toward the undeserving**.

He does not merely dislike the younger brother’s sin; he resents that grace is being lavished on someone who, in his eyes, does not deserve it. Mercy offends him because it threatens his whole economy of merit. He believes his faithfulness should earn him special recognition. So when the father celebrates restoration rather than retribution, the elder brother feels cheated.

Several details show this clearly.

First, he **refuses to go in**:

> “And he was angry, and would not go in.”

That is more than bad temper. It is a refusal to participate in the father’s joy. The father comes out to him, just as he earlier ran to the younger son. The father is merciful to both. But the elder brother will not enter the feast of reconciliation.

Second, he describes his relationship to the father in terms of service, not sonship:

> “These many years do I serve thee.”

He sounds like a servant who expects wages, not a son who shares the father’s heart. His obedience has become a claim against the father rather than a communion with him.

Third, he says:

> “Neither transgressed I at any time thy commandment.”

This is the language of external righteousness. He has kept the rules, but his heart is far from the father’s love. He has been near the father for years, yet he does not know the father’s compassion.

Fourth, he cannot call the prodigal “my brother.” He says:

> “This thy son.”

But the father corrects him:

> “This thy brother.”

The elder brother has cut himself off relationally. He will not identify with the one who was lost. He wants justice for “this thy son,” but the father insists on mercy for “this thy brother.”

Finally, the father says:

> “Son, thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine.”

This shows the tragedy. The elder brother already had everything. He did not need to be jealous. The father’s love had not been diminished by the younger son’s return. The elder brother’s real poverty was not that he lacked a kid; it was that he lacked the father’s heart of mercy.

So, if choosing from your list, the best answer is:

> **Discomfort with mercy itself.**

More precisely, his deepest problem is a **proud, mercenary righteousness that cannot rejoice when grace is shown to the undeserving**. He is not lost in the far country like the prodigal; he is lost inside the father’s house. He is close to the father by duty, but distant from him in love. He keeps the commandments, but he does not share the joy. And that is why he stands outside, angry at mercy.
