Because for Isaiah the fault is not in one level only. “Way” points to the outward-running life—habit, policy, repeated choice; “thoughts” points to the interior architecture that generates that way. The pairing says that moral failure is both a direction and a formation: a person who “walks” wrongly is usually doing so under a ruling imagination. So repentance is not only “stop doing bad things”; it is also a turning of what the soul makes itself into—what it desires, prizes, imagines to be true, and plans as its way of life.

That implies:

- **Outward conduct matters, but is not self-sufficient.** A person can restrain visible “wickedness” while remaining governed by corrupt desire and imaginative self-deception. The “way” must be forsaken, but forsaking the acts only superficially would leave the root intact.
- **Inward imagination is moral causality, not mere privacy.** The text does not treat “thoughts” as harmless mental noise. They are the thoughts of the unrighteous man, which function as the inner pattern from which outward decisions flow. Repentance therefore means confronting what the person has desired and believed, not only listing what he has done.
- **Mercy is offered to the whole person.** God’s promise is “to our God, for he will abundantly pardon.” Mercy is not merely forgiveness of discrete transgressions; it is provision for the seeker’s whole identity and direction. God invites the wicked to return and not merely to show that he deserves less guilt.
- **Yet mercy is not a command to pretend one is already clean.** The “thoughts” are included precisely so that repentance cannot be reduced to outward conformity. The Christian/prophetic logic is not “fix your thinking first, then get forgiven,” but “God gives mercy to the one who turns toward him,” knowing that way and thought are bound together.
- **There is urgency and possibility at once.** “Seek the Lord while he may be found” frames repentance as timely; “call upon him while he is near” suggests nearness as the stage of return; “he will abundantly pardon” then removes the shame-finality of the record. Repentance is not escape from judgment by good works, but a shift in orientation toward the God who pardons abundantly.

So the passage’s chief implication is this: moral life is a whole-person disorder and whole-person recovery. It is not just a problem of deeds, but of imagined good, desire, and purpose. And mercy is not first a reward for perfection of inner life; it is mercy offered to the very one whose inner and outer ways have turned away. In that sense, repentance becomes both a change of course and a renewal of desire: a turning that may change what is imagined, what is chosen, and what is sought.
