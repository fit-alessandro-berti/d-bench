It is almost the opposite of saying that moral judgments never matter. In fact, Jesus’ warning is possible only because moral judgment matters very much. The verse is not a ban on moral discernment; it is a ban on a certain kind of moral posture.

The difference is between **discernment** and **condemnation**.

## What Jesus forbids

When Jesus says, “Judge not, and ye shall not be judged: condemn not, and ye shall not be condemned,” the second phrase clarifies the first. He is not saying, “Never make moral judgments.” He is saying, “Do not take the seat of the final Judge.” The Greek word *krino* can mean judge, evaluate, or decide, but in this context it carries the force of *passing a verdict against someone* — declaring them guilty, worthless, or beyond hope.

That is a role that belongs to God, not to us. We are not the judge of a person’s soul or eternal destiny. When we condemn, we act as if we have the authority to lock someone into a verdict and throw away the key. Jesus is warning against that.

## What Jesus does not forbid

Jesus elsewhere commands us to judge — with the right kind of judgment. He says, “Judge not according to appearance, but judge righteous judgment” (John 7:24). He says to recognize false teachers by their fruit. In the same sermon where He says “Judge not,” He also says, “Beware of false prophets” and “By their fruits you shall know them” (Matthew 7:15-16). You cannot know a tree by its fruit without making a moral judgment about that fruit.

Jesus also tells us to remove the log from our own eye before we concern ourselves with the speck in our brother’s eye. That assumes there *is* a speck. It assumes moral faults are real. The problem is not seeing the fault; the problem is the self-righteous appetite to use that fault as a weapon against someone else.

## Discernment vs. the appetite to condemn

Discernment asks:

> Is this act good or evil? Is this path wise or destructive? Is this teaching true or false?

And then it asks:

> What is the loving response?

The appetite to condemn says:

> I have found someone who is wrong, and it makes me feel right. I have found someone who is sinful, and it makes me feel clean. I will label them, categorize them, and discard them.

Discernment is **humble**. It can say, “This is wrong, and I am also a sinner in need of grace.”

Condemnation is **proud**. It says, “This is wrong, and I am the one who has the right to declare him completely guilty.”

Discernment leaves room for God. It can say, “This sin is real, but I am not the judge of this person’s final destiny.”

Condemnation plays God. It says, “This sin is real, and this person is hopeless.”

Discernment is motivated by love. It cares enough to confront, to protect, to call to repentance.

The appetite to condemn is motivated by self-elevation. It enjoys judging. It feeds on the discovery of faults in others.

## If moral judgments never matter, why forgive?

The last part of the verse is decisive: “Forgive, and ye shall be forgiven.”

Forgiveness does not mean “what you did doesn’t matter.” Forgiveness means *the exact opposite*: what you did mattered enough to need forgiving. If sin were unreal, forgiveness would be meaningless. If morality were irrelevant, the cross would be nonsense.

Jesus is not saying that there is no such thing as good and evil. He is saying that we are not the ones who get to write the final verdict. The standard of judgment is real and terrifyingly serious. That is why we must not use judgment as a weapon — because “with the same measure that ye mete withal, it shall be measured to you again” (Luke 6:38).

So the difference is simple:

> Moral judgments matter deeply. But the **appeal to condemn** is a sin.

We are called to see good and evil clearly, to love truth, to call sin what it is — and then to leave the final verdict to God, to forgive as we have been forgiven.
