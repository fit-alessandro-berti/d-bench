In Matthew 6:1, the problem is not visibility itself, but **the craving—and intentional design—to be seen**. 

The verse itself makes this distinction through the crucial qualifying phrase: *"to be seen of them."* Jesus is not forbidding acts of goodness that happen to occur in public; he is warning against performing goodness *for the purpose of* attracting an audience.

We know visibility itself is not the issue because just one chapter earlier, in the very same sermon, Jesus tells his listeners: *"Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father which is in heaven"* (Matthew 5:16). Taken together, the two verses establish a clear rule of thumb: **Visibility is good when it directs attention to God, and dangerous when it directs attention to the self.**

What makes public goodness morally dangerous, then, is the subtle psychological and spiritual trap that visibility creates. This danger unfolds in a few key ways:

### 1. It turns virtue into theater
The Greek phrase translated as *"to be seen"* (*pros to theathenai*) shares a root with the English word **theater**. In the very next verse (6:2), Jesus refers to those who practice ostentatious charity as *"hypocrites"*—a word that literally meant "stage actors." Public goodness becomes dangerous when the moral life shifts from genuine character to a performance. Once you start acting for an audience, you begin caring more about the *appearance* of goodness than the *reality* of it.

### 2. It instrumentalizes goodness (and other people)
True almsgiving or righteousness is supposed to be an act of love toward a neighbor and devotion toward God. When done to be seen, the good deed ceases to be an end in itself and becomes a tool for self-promotion. Worse, the people being helped are subtly reduced to props in a personal reputation-building exercise.

### 3. It substitutes human applause for divine approval
Jesus warns that those who perform for an audience *"have no reward of your Father which is in heaven."* In the following verses, he adds that those who seek human praise *"have their reward"*—using a Greek commercial term (*apechousin*) meaning "paid in full." The danger here is spiritual impoverishment: by trading the quiet approval of God for the fleeting validation of the crowd, a person settles for a cheap substitute. Once the applause ends, the transaction is closed.

### 4. It corrupts even good intentions
Even if a person does not start out seeking praise, public visibility creates a powerful temptation for the ego to hijack the act. It is notoriously difficult to be applauded for humility or generosity without beginning to savor the applause. This is why Jesus’s practical remedy in the verses that follow (giving in secret, praying behind closed doors) acts as a diagnostic test: doing good where no one can see strips away the ego's incentives, ensuring that the motive remains pure.

In short, visibility is morally neutral, but the human ego is not. Public goodness is dangerous because it constantly tempts the heart to change its audience—turning an act of love into a bid for admiration.
