

Enemy-love is difficult to take seriously because it cuts against almost everything in us that equates justice with retaliation, safety with distance, and moral seriousness with punishment. Our instincts say: *Return harm. Protect yourself. Make the wrongdoer pay.* Jesus says something startlingly different: *Refuse to let the enemy determine your character. Do good. Pray. Bless.*

That sounds either sublime or naïve. And the reason it is hard to take seriously is not merely that it is hard to do, but that it threatens our ordinary moral logic.

### 1. Why enemy-love is so difficult

Enemy-love is difficult because it asks us to do several deeply counterintuitive things at once.

**It refuses revenge while still taking evil seriously.**  
Many people assume that if we do not hate our enemies, we are minimizing the wrong they have done. But enemy-love does not say, “What they did does not matter.” It says, “I will not allow their evil to become the law of my heart.” That is a much harder stance than retaliation.

**It requires strength without domination.**  
We often think strength means overpowering the other person—making them suffer, silencing them, humiliating them, forcing them to acknowledge us. Enemy-love offers another kind of strength: the strength to remain human, to resist bitterness, and to will the enemy’s good without surrendering truth or justice.

**It exposes vulnerability.**  
To pray for an enemy is to admit that they still matter before God, and that I am not the final judge of their soul. That is frightening. It means stepping out of the position of absolute moral superiority.

**It interrupts group loyalty.**  
In most communities, loving “our own” is easy. The enemy helps define the group. To love the enemy can therefore feel like betrayal. It disrupts the simple boundary between “us” and “them.”

**It demands a longer hope than we naturally possess.**  
Enemy-love often cannot see immediate results. The enemy may not repent. The persecution may not stop. The wound may not heal quickly. So enemy-love requires a hope that is not dependent on visible success.

That is why it feels so hard to take seriously. It is not merely a moral rule; it is a different way of living in the world.

### 2. Is it sentimental?

It becomes sentimental when we reduce it to warm feelings toward people who have harmed us. But the text does not say, “Feel affection for your enemies.” It says:

> “Love your enemies, bless them that curse you, do good to them that hate you, and pray for them which despitefully use you, and persecute you.”

These are actions, not emotions.

Enemy-love in Matthew 5 is not primarily sentimentality. It is not the denial of pain, the suppression of anger, or the pretense that abuse is harmless. It is not saying, “I love you” while refusing to name injustice. Sentimentality avoids conflict; enemy-love often enters it truthfully but redemptively.

Real enemy-love can include lament, protest, truth-telling, boundaries, and even justice. It does not require the victim to become the abuser’s companion or to pretend harm has not occurred. It means refusing to answer evil with the same spirit by which it was committed.

So no: in its deepest form, enemy-love is not sentimental. Sentimentality is cheap. Enemy-love is costly.

### 3. Is it strategic?

There can be a strategic dimension to enemy-love. It can disarm hostility. It can expose injustice. It can create moral space for repentance. It can prevent cycles of violence. In that sense, enemy-love can be profoundly wise.

But if enemy-love is only strategy, it becomes fragile. If praying for the enemy does not “work,” if blessing does not produce reconciliation, if doing good is met with more hatred, then strategy collapses into disappointment. A purely strategic enemy-love says, “I will love you because it will change you.” But Jesus does not ground enemy-love primarily in its effectiveness.

He grounds it in the character of God:

> “That ye may be the children of your Father which is in heaven: for he maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good, and sendeth rain on the just and on the unjust.”

The point is not merely, “Love your enemies because it will produce better outcomes.” The point is: *Love your enemies because this is what it looks like to belong to the family of God.*

God’s sun and rain are not rewards for virtue. They are gifts of common grace. God sustains people who do not deserve it. That does not mean God is indifferent to evil; it means God’s goodness is not exhausted by retribution. His kindness is meant to lead toward repentance, not merely to confirm merit.

So enemy-love may have strategic effects, but it is not mainly a strategy. If it is only strategy, it is still self-centered: *I love you because it benefits me or controls you.* Gospel enemy-love says, *I love you because you are before God, and I too am before God.*

### 4. Or is it a deeper form of moral strength?

Yes. Enemy-love is a deeper form of moral strength because it requires the hardest kind of freedom: freedom from being ruled by the one who wounded you.

Hatred can feel powerful, but it often enslaves. The enemy continues to occupy the mind, shape the emotions, and determine the future. Resentment gives the offender ongoing authority over the wounded person. Enemy-love resists that. It says, “You have harmed me, but you will not define the direction of my soul.”

That is not weakness. It is moral sovereignty under God.

Enemy-love requires:

- **Courage** to remain open to good where evil has occurred.
- **Self-command** not to answer cruelty with cruelty.
- **Truthfulness** not to call evil good.
- **Humility** to remember one’s own need for mercy.
- **Hope** that the enemy is not finally closed beyond redemption.
- **Faith** that God sees, judges, and can restore.

This is why enemy-love is not passive. It is active: *bless*, *do good*, *pray*. It is not the absence of moral conviction. It is conviction purified of malice.

### 5. The deepest reason: enemy-love is theological

Matthew 5:44–45 does not present enemy-love as a heroic moral achievement detached from God. It presents it as participation in the life of God.

> “That ye may be the children of your Father which is in heaven.”

The purpose is not simply ethical improvement. It is family resemblance.

God gives sun and rain to those who do not deserve it. He sustains the unjust as well as the just. This is not moral carelessness. It is divine patience. It is the goodness of God that refuses to reduce every human being instantly to their worst act.

Enemy-love, then, is not primarily about summoning extraordinary human willpower. It is about becoming the kind of people whose lives reflect the Father. It says: if God has not treated me according to my worst moments, I cannot treat others only according to theirs.

That is why enemy-love is especially hard. It is not merely a command to obey; it is a transformation to undergo. It requires us to believe that God’s goodness is real, that justice belongs to him, and that our identity is found not in our grievances but in being children of the Father.

### 6. What enemy-love is not

To take enemy-love seriously, we must distinguish it from several distortions.

Enemy-love is **not**:

- approving of evil;
- staying under abuse;
- pretending justice does not matter;
- suppressing righteous anger;
- refusing to protect the vulnerable;
- forgiving without truth;
- becoming morally indifferent;
- giving evil people unrestricted access to one’s life.

Enemy-love can coexist with firm boundaries, legal consequences, public rebuke, and even resistance. What it rejects is hatred as a settled disposition and revenge as a final goal.

It says: *I will oppose you, if necessary, but I will not demonize you beyond hope. I will seek truth, but not make hatred my home. I will defend the innocent, but I will not let bitterness become my righteousness.*

### 7. The answer

Enemy-love is difficult to take seriously because it seems to ask the impossible. And in one sense, it does. It asks us to transcend the natural economy of retaliation and enter the divine economy of grace.

It is not sentimental, because it does not depend on pleasant feelings or deny reality.  
It is not merely strategic, because it is not ultimately grounded in results.  
It is a deeper form of moral strength, because it requires courage, truth, humility, and hope.

But even “moral strength” is not enough if we mean only unaided human resolve. In Matthew 5, enemy-love is possible only as a share in the character of God. It is the strength of those who believe that the Father’s sun rises even on the evil, and his rain falls even on the unjust—not because evil is trivial, but because God’s goodness is more fundamental than our retaliation.

So enemy-love is finally neither weakness nor naïveté. It is the most difficult and most divine form of freedom: the refusal to let evil have the last word in the heart.
