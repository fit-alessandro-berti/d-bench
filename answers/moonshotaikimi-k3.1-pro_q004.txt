The passage calls for **wholehearted, relational, and practical reliance on God**, not the suspension of thought.

- **“Trust in the LORD with all thine heart”** means undivided confidence in God’s character and wisdom. In Proverbs, the *heart* includes thought, desire, and choice—not merely emotion. This trust is therefore more than assent; it governs how one lives.
- **“Lean not unto thine own understanding”** pictures resting one’s weight on a support. The warning is not *“Do not use your understanding,”* but *“Do not make your own judgment your ultimate, self-sufficient support.”* Human discernment is real but limited, biased, and prone to self-deception.
- **“In all thy ways acknowledge him”** makes that trust comprehensive: God is not consulted only in religious matters or emergencies, but recognized and obeyed in every sphere of life.
- **“He shall direct thy paths”** promises guidance, though not necessarily an infallible inner voice, freedom from uncertainty, or an easy life.

This is **humble self-distrust**, not self-hatred or chronic indecision. It says: “I can reason, but I am not infallible; I must remain teachable and correctable before God.” Such humility may actually produce *more* careful thinking—study, honest self-examination, attention to wise counsel, willingness to admit error, and obedience when God’s wisdom challenges one’s preferences.

**Anti-intellectualism**, by contrast, treats learning, questions, evidence, or criticism as threats. It can even become a disguised form of self-reliance: refusing correction while labeling one’s own impulses or prejudices “God’s guidance.” Proverbs itself rules out that reading, repeatedly commanding its readers to pursue wisdom, knowledge, instruction, and understanding (see, for example, 4:5–7).

In short: **the command is not “stop thinking,” but “stop treating your own thinking as the final measure of wisdom.” Use your mind, but do not make an idol of its autonomy.**
