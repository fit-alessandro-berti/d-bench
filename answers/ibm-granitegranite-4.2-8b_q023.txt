Isaiah 55:6–7 says:  
> “Seek ye the Lord while he may be found, call ye upon him while he is near:  
> Let the wicked forsake his **way**, and the unrighteous man his **thoughts**: and let him return unto the Lord, and he will have mercy upon him; and to our God, for he will abundantly pardon.”

The phrase “forsake his way” and “forsake his thoughts” together describe repentance comprehensively because in biblical language (consistent with usage throughout Isaiah, Psalms, and other OT passages):

1. **“Way” refers to outward conduct, behavior, and pattern of living.**  
   In OT usage, *dā’āh* (Hebrew for “way,” often translated “way,” “path,” “conduct”) consistently describes a person’s habitual course of action—what they do, how they act in practice (e.g., Prov. 2:15; Prov. 10:9; Jer. 7:24). Thus, “forsake his way” means ceasing from the habitual, outward pattern of wrong behavior or conduct associated with wickedness (e.g., injustice, deception, idolatry, selfish pursuit).

2. **“Thoughts” refers to inward imagination, attitudes, and inner disposition.**  
   In OT usage, *ma’aseh* or *lum’ah* (Hebrew terms for inner life) and the verb *to think/meditate* (e.g., Psalm 119:10; Prov. 4:23) describe what a person actively holds, imagines, or sets the mind upon. “Thoughts” here means the ongoing inner orientation—the unjust or self-serving mental attitudes, desires, and imaginings that drive or sustain the outward conduct (e.g., plotting, rationalizing, longing for wrong). Thus, “forsake his thoughts” means ceasing to dwell on, pursue, or entertain those inward attitudes.

3. **Why both terms are included together in repentance:**  
   The passage explicitly links the two: *“Let the wicked forsake his way, and the unrighteous man his thoughts.”* This indicates repentance is not merely changing one surface action while keeping inner motives; it requires turning away from the *whole integrated pattern*: the outward behavior *and* the inward mindset that produces it. True repentance addresses the root (inner thought/attitude) *and* the fruit (outward conduct), because what a person thinks consistently shapes what they do. Omitting either would be incomplete (e.g., stopping a wrong action but still habitually thinking/justifying it would not fully turn from wickedness; stopping thoughts but continuing outward wrong conduct would not fully turn from wickedness). The phrase “return unto the Lord” implies a wholehearted turning, consistent with both inner and external alignment.

4. **What this implies about mercy:**  
   The text states directly: *“and he will have mercy upon him; and to our God, for he will abundantly pardon.”* Because repentance here is defined as forsaking *both* outward conduct (“way”) *and* inward imagination/attitudes (“thoughts”), the possibility of mercy stated in verse 7 is directly implied by the scope of the call to return. God’s mercy (“he will have mercy”) and abundant pardon (“he will abundantly pardon”) are not contingent on achieving perfection in a single aspect (only outward behavior *or* only inner thought); they are offered to the one who responds by genuinely turning from *both*—the habitual way of living *and* the habitual inner orientation. Thus, the inclusion of both terms logically supports the promise: *if* the person truly forsakes both (i.e., genuinely returns in wholehearted repentance), God will mercifully pardon them abundantly. The phrase “while he may be found” and “call ye upon him while he is near” underscores the timely, accessible nature of this mercy—it is available to the one who seeks and returns in repentance (forsaking both way and thoughts), not to those who persist in either outward conduct or inward thought without turning.

**In short, and directly answering the question:**  
> Repentance here involves both “way” (outward conduct) and “thoughts” (inward imagination/attitudes) because biblical language uses “way” for habitual action and “thoughts” for habitual inner orientation; repentance therefore requires turning away from *both* the pattern of doing wrong *and* the pattern of thinking/imagining wrong. This holistic call (forsaking both) logically implies that God’s promise of “mercy” and “abundant pardon” applies to the person who genuinely returns by forsaking *both*—it affirms mercy is possible for one who truly turns from both outward conduct and inward thought, consistent with the timely invitation (“while he may be found,” “while he is near”).
