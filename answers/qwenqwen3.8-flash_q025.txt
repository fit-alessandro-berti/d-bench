The morally dangerous thing in Matthew 6:1 is **not simply that righteousness or almsgiving is visible**. The danger is that the act becomes a **performance for human approval**—that the person does good *in order to be seen*. Visibility can be neutral or even good; **craving recognition is what corrupts the deed**.

## 1. The key is the purpose, not merely the location

The Greek of Matthew 6:1 can be rendered:

> “Take care not to do your righteousness before men, **for the purpose of being seen by them**…”

Modern translations usually read “righteousness” rather than “alms,” though KJV follows the older English rendering tied to the following verses about giving. If the verse begins with “righteousness,” then the warning is broad: **all religious or moral activity can become corrupted when its aim is public display**.

The decisive phrase is “to be seen of them.” Jesus is not saying, “Never let anyone see your good deeds.” He is saying, “Do not arrange your good deeds as a spectacle.”

So the problem is not:

> “I did good where someone saw me.”

The problem is:

> “I did good so that I would be seen.”

## 2. Public goodness is dangerous because the audience can replace God

Public acts of piety—giving, praying, fasting, serving—can easily turn into moral theater. The “before men” audience becomes the true judge, the true reward-giver, the true audience. The actor is no longer standing before God but performing for the gallery.

That is why the immediately following verse says:

> “When thou dost thine alms, sound not a trumpet before thee, as the hypocrites do… that they may have glory of men.”  
> — Matthew 6:2

The word “hypocrite” here evokes a stage actor: someone playing a role rather than being who they seem. Public goodness becomes morally dangerous when it becomes a means of acquiring reputation, status, admiration, or moral credit.

## 3. The craving can distort the goodness itself

Once the motive shifts from God and neighbor to self-advertisement, even outwardly generous acts can become distorted.

For example, one may:

- give to what is visibly impressive rather than truly needed;
- serve in ways that build reputation more than relationships;
- help people who can be seen and thanked while ignoring those who cannot;
- turn charity into a kind of consumption, where the donor’s image matters more than the receiver’s dignity;
- secretly measure worth by how much others notice one’s righteousness.

In that sense, public goodness can be morally dangerous because it provides a stage on which pride can dress itself as virtue. The action looks generous, but its hidden engine may be vanity.

## 4. Jesus is not commanding invisibility

Jesus elsewhere does not condemn visibility itself. In the Sermon on the Mount, just before this passage, He says:

> “Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father which is in heaven.”  
> — Matthew 5:16

That passage makes the distinction clear. In Matthew 5:16, good works are seen **so that God may be glorified**. In Matthew 6:1, religious acts are seen **so that the self may be glorified**.

So visibility is not automatically sinful. Goodness can and should sometimes be public: for accountability, encouragement, witness, transparency, stewardship, or the common good. The danger is not that men see you. The danger is that you live for their seeing.

## 5. The “reward” language confirms the motive

> “Otherwise ye have no reward of your Father which is in heaven.”

This does not mean God refuses to accept every publicly visible act. Rather, it means that if the act was performed primarily to obtain human praise, then human praise is the only reward it receives. The applause becomes the payment in full.

The heavenly reward is missing because the heart was not oriented toward God. The act may have benefited someone, but it failed as an act of true righteousness before God if its controlling motive was “be seen.”

## Answer to the question

The problem in Matthew 6:1 is **not visibility itself**, but the **craving to be seen**.

Public goodness is morally dangerous because it can become a performance in which the audience replaces God. When we do good in order to be noticed, praised, admired, or validated, the external virtue can become inwardly corrupt. But if a good deed is done sincerely before God, and public visibility happens to follow—without being the aim—then visibility is not the sin. The sin is making one’s righteousness a show for human eyes.
