The verse itself supplies the answer in its grammar. The Greek reads *pros to theathēnai autois* — "in order to be watched by them." *Theathēnai* is the verb behind our word "theater": the danger Jesus names is not that goodness happens to occur where people can see it, but that it is *staged* for an audience. The following verses confirm the image — the offenders are *hypokritai*, literally "stage actors," who sound trumpets before giving. Visibility is the stage; the craving is the performance.

The reward logic makes this precise. Those who give to be seen "have their reward in full" (*apechousin* — a term used on commercial receipts meaning "paid in full"). The point is not that God spitefully withholds something from show-offs; it's that the show-off has chosen a currency and been paid in it. Applause was the entire purpose of the act, so applause exhausts it. What's corrupted is not the act's effect on the recipient — the poor person still gets fed — but the doer's relationship to the act: it has been converted from a gift into a transaction, and the transaction is already closed.

This is why visibility per se cannot be the problem. One chapter earlier, Jesus says nearly the opposite: "Let your light shine before men, that they may see your good works" (5:16). Same visibility, different aim: there the seeing is meant to terminate in God's glory; here it terminates in the doer's glory. The same deed can face two directions, and what makes it dangerous is which way the heart is oriented. What matters is less the audience of the act than the audience of the motive.

That said, the verse doesn't treat visibility as neutral either — and this is the subtler point. Jesus' remedy is an extravagance of secrecy: don't let your left hand know what your right hand is doing (6:3). That's not practical advice; it's a deliberately impossible image, and its point is that the craving to be seen penetrates deeper than the crowd. We can perform for an audience of one — ourselves. The "take heed" of the opening is warning against something self-deceptive, and the gaze we most need to escape is internal. So visibility functions as the temptation's terrain: human attention reliably feeds vanity, and the prescription to give "in secret" is less about hiding than about starving the craving of its food.

So the direct answer: the craving is the disease; visibility is the stage. But the verse counsels leaving the stage — not because stages are evil, but because the actor in us is persistent enough that we sometimes need the audience physically removed in order to remember whom the act was for.
