The short answer: in Matthew 5, enemy-love is **not a soft sentiment** and **not merely a useful strategy**. It is a **costly, active, moral and spiritual strength**—but it is hard to take seriously because it cuts against our ordinary instincts for fairness, self-protection, and retaliation, and because we often confuse love with liking or approval.

### Why it feels so hard to take seriously

**1. It seems to offend justice.**  
If “love” means “approve of someone” or “pretend they did no wrong,” then loving an enemy sounds immoral. It sounds like telling a victim to excuse abuse or oppression. But Jesus is not commanding approval, affection, or passivity. He commands concrete actions: *bless, do good, pray.*

**2. It has been sentimentalized.**  
We often hear “love” as warm feelings. But Jesus does not say, “Feel fondly toward your enemies.” He says, *Love them*—in the active, costly sense of seeking their true good. The sentimental version of enemy-love is usually fake and collapses under real pressure.

**3. It has been turned into a tactic.**  
Sometimes “love your enemies” is used as a strategy: be nice to them to shame them, win them over, or look morally superior. That can become manipulation. Jesus does not ground enemy-love primarily in its results.

**4. It is rarely modeled.**  
Because even religious communities usually practice ordinary reciprocation—love those who love you—enemy-love can seem unrealistic or utopian.

### Not sentimental

The New Testament word is *agapē*: not primarily an emotion, but a settled intention to will the genuine good of another. Jesus makes this concrete:

> “Bless them that curse you, do good to them that hate you, and pray for them which despitefully use you.”

That is not passivity. It is moral action. It can include truth-telling, confrontation, protection of victims, and pursuit of justice. Jesus himself rebuked oppressors and wrongdoers, yet he also prayed for his killers. That is not sentimentality; that is costly love.

### Not merely strategic

Enemy-love can have strategic effects. It can break cycles of revenge, disarm hostility, and create space for reconciliation. But if it is practiced only to get results, it ceases to be love and becomes a technique.

Jesus gives a different reason:

> “That ye may be the children of your Father which is in heaven: for he maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good, and sendeth rain on the just and on the unjust.”

In other words, enemy-love is **imitation of God**. God gives sun and rain to all, not because God is weak or naïve, but because God is God. The point is not that loving enemies makes you a child of God by earning it; the point is that such love shows the family likeness.

### A deeper moral strength

This is the real answer. Enemy-love is a deeper moral strength because it is harder than retaliation. Retaliation is easy in the sense that it lets the enemy set your emotional agenda. Enemy-love requires the freedom to absorb evil without passing it on, to will the enemy’s good without excusing their evil, and to respond creatively rather than reflexively.

It is also cruciform. Jesus commands nothing he did not himself do. On the cross he prays, “Father, forgive them.” That was not weakness. It was the most costly expression of divine love.

So enemy-love is difficult to take seriously because it is far more demanding than either sentiment or strategy. Understood biblically, it is a severe, God-like moral strength: a love that does not deny evil, but refuses to become evil in response.
