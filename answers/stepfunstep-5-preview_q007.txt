The command in Luke 6:37 is often mistaken for a form of moral quietism—as if Jesus were telling his followers that ethical distinctions are unimportant, or that one ought not to notice the difference between cruelty and kindness. Read that way, the verse is incoherent. It appears in a sermon full of moral instruction, it is followed two verses later by the parable of the speck and the log (which assumes that specks and logs are real and harmful), and it contains the positive imperative “forgive,” which is meaningless unless some wrong has actually been committed that requires pardoning.

The distinction the text is actually drawing is between **discernment** and **the appetite to condemn**. One is a mode of sight; the other is a mode of hunger.

### 1. Discernment: seeing clearly
Discernment is the capacity to perceive that an action, a policy, or a state of affairs is destructive, false, or unjust. It is evaluative, but its primary orientation is toward *truth* and, often, toward the good of the person or community involved. Discernment can be quiet, reluctant, and slow. It is willing to say, “This is wrong,” while simultaneously acknowledging, “I do not know everything that led here.” 

In the Synoptic tradition, Jesus himself practices this kind of judgment constantly. He calls certain behaviors contrary to God’s will, warns of consequences, and distinguishes the wise from the foolish. Yet his most famous act of moral clarity—the encounter with the woman caught in adultery (John 7:53–8:11)—shows the shape of discernment without condemnation. He names the reality (“sin”) but refuses to become the executioner. He looks at the situation without the log of self-righteousness in his own eye.

Discernment, in short, preserves the reality of moral categories. It can name evil without devouring the evildoer.

### 2. The appetite to condemn: the hunger for verdicts
The “appetite to condemn” is something different. It is not simply the belief that something is wrong; it is the *desire* that the wrongdoer fall, be excluded, or pay in full. It is prosecutorial rather than diagnostic. Its pleasure lies not in truth or healing but in the verdict itself.

This appetite reveals itself in several recognizable ways:

*   **It is finalistic.** It wants the case closed, the person typed, the category fixed. It speaks of “those people” or “that kind of person.”
*   **It is self-exalting.** It requires an audience, or at least a secret satisfaction that “I am not like that.” It forgets the log in its own eye.
*   **It crowds out mercy.** It cannot forgive, because forgiveness requires an openness to the future that condemnation forecloses.
*   **It usurps a role.** In the biblical frame, ultimate judgment belongs to God. To condemn is to act as an eschatological judge before the time appointed.

When Jesus says, “Judge not… condemn not,” he is not prohibiting the ability to tell right from wrong. He is warning against letting that ability become an *appetite*—a source of personal delight, social power, or spiritual superiority.

### 3. Why this is the opposite of moral nihilism
Far from meaning that moral judgments never matter, the verse presupposes their seriousness so intensely that it warns against human beings mishandling divine justice.

*   **Forgiveness proves that wrongs are real.** You cannot forgive a person for walking in a straight line. The command “forgive, and ye shall be forgiven” only makes sense if offenses, debts, and injuries are actual. The verse does not dissolve morality; it assumes a moral universe in which violations have weight.
*   **The measure principle intensifies ethics.** Jesus follows the command with the famous measure-for-measure principle (v. 38). That is not a threat of moral indifference; it is a warning that the standard by which we judge others will be applied to us. To take this seriously is to recognize that one’s own moral conduct is under observation—hardly a license to stop caring about right and wrong.
*   **It protects the vulnerable from moralism.** A community in which the appetite to condemn runs rampant is not a morally serious community; it is a cruel one. History is full of regimes that claimed moral purity while destroying people. The prohibition of condemnation is a safeguard of compassion, ensuring that moral language does not become a weapon of expulsion.

### 4. The practical difference
| **Discernment** | **The Appetite to Condemn** |
|---|---|
| Sees a sin | Sees a sinner as finished |
| Is content to say, “This is harmful” | Wants to say, “You are beyond reach” |
| Can coexist with grief | Usually coexists with contempt |
| Is attentive to context | Is driven by category |
| Leaves room for repentance | Locks the door |
| Is done in the quiet of conscience | Often needs an audience |
| Fears for the other’s soul | Feels superior to it |

### Conclusion
Luke 6:37 is not a command to moral blindness. It is a command to moral sobriety. It tells us not to make verdicts our vocation, not to take pleasure in the downfall of others, and not to confuse the clarity of our vision with the right to execute it. 

The person who has truly discerned the depth of human failings—beginning with their own—finds that condemnation tastes like ash. What remains is not indifference, but the difficult, lucid mercy of one who knows exactly how real the wrong is, and chooses forgiveness anyway.
