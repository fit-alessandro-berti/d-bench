It does both, and the text refuses to separate them.

“Treasure” here is not just a budget noun. The verb is a store/save/keep word: earthly life is not simply *owned*; it is *laid up*, gathered, guarded, made secure. So the issue is not whether someone kept money somewhere, but what they are storing, for whom, and with what attention. Possession is the outward act; attention is the sustained concentration; love is the orientation of the whole life. Matthew collapses all three into one sentence: *where your treasure is, there will your heart be also.*

That can be read as reveal: where what you gather and guard is, there is the deepest shape of your trust. If what is saved is security, status, appetite, reputation, or escape from death, then the heart is there too. Moth, rust and thieves are a diagnostic, not a lecture: earthly treasure cannot keep its keeper.

But “reveal” cannot be the whole meaning. Matthew is not only asking us to inspect our possessions; he is commanding us to change where treasure is laid up. The “lay not” / “lay” pairing makes treasure a practice, not a symptom. In that sense treasure also trains the heart. To keep saving, guarding, and trusting for earthly goods trains a heart anxious about decay. To lay treasure up in heaven trains a heart to make enduring trust, covenant life, and the Kingdom the object of desire. Anticipation and orientation show up again and again in the sermon: care for security, fear of loss, attention directed to the thing that can vanish.

So the text is neither a secular psychological inventory nor an escape from the material world. It is a yoking statement: the outward economy, the inner gaze, and the object of love are bound together. Possession shows what is saved; attention shows what is watched and guarded; love shows what is desired enough to be kept.

In Matthew’s setting, that means earthly treasure can never finally anchor the heart, because it can be lost, stolen, or worn out. Heavenly treasure cannot be secured in the same sense, because it is not a better bank account. It is the placing of trust where loss cannot undo it, where the heart is not built around what can vanish. The heart is not mechanically “moved” by the object; it consents. But where the treasure is kept is exactly where desire begins to be formed.

In one sentence: treasure reveals the heart by showing where trust is stored, and it trains the heart by exercising the same act of saving, waiting, and valuing that desire works by. Matthew 6:19–21 binds possession to attention and attention to love—because, for the self in Matthew’s Jesus, those are not three facts about life. They are three views of what the heart is doing when it says “mine.”
