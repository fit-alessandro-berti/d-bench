In Romans 12:20–21, victory over evil is defined not as physical retaliation, retaliation, or domination, but as the **moral and spiritual triumph of good over evil through active, self-giving love**. The passage redefines "conquest" as a transformation of the enemy, not their destruction.

Here’s how the passage develops that idea:

1. **Direct, concrete action instead of retaliation**  
   Paul commands practical care: feed the hungry enemy, give the thirsty one drink. This is not passive indifference; it is active compassion toward someone who has wronged you. The "victory" comes from refusing to be consumed by hatred, revenge, or the cycle of evil.

2. **The unusual image of "coals of fire"**  
   In the ancient context, placing burning coals on an enemy's head likely symbolized either intense shame/conviction or, more commonly, the duty of passing a fire-soaked brand to warm a neighbor's fire (a sign of hospitality and generosity). Either way, it means the enemy is confronted with the reality of your goodwill, which can lead to remorse, compassion, or a change of heart. The "heaping of coals" is not a weapon; it is the weight of undeserved kindness.

3. **The decisive command: "Be not overcome of evil, but overcome evil with good"**  
   This is the theological heart of the passage. Victory over evil is not defeating the enemy with evil (violence, retaliation, dehumanization), but defeating evil by refusing to let it define you. You overcome by embodying the good that refuses to be corrupted. In Paul's framework, evil is overcome not by matching its methods, but by outlasting it in love.

### Is the command weak, manipulative, or genuinely strong?

**Genuinely strong**, when understood in its original and biblical context.

- **Not weak**: It demands extraordinary courage. Loving an enemy requires the strength to absorb harm without retaliating, to do good to those who mistreat you, and to trust that God will handle justice. That is not passivity; it is active resistance to the logic of violence and retribution.
- **Not manipulative**: The purpose is not to control, shame, or use the enemy. Paul does not promise the action will force a specific reaction or serve your own agenda. He says it may *lead* to the enemy's conviction or change, but the command is about your own faithfulness and the work of good itself. The love is directed toward the other person, not toward manipulating them.
- **Spiritually strong**: It redefines power. In a world that equates strength with force, this passage insists that the true victor is the one who holds onto goodness in the face of evil. It is strong because it breaks the cycle of retaliation, restores human dignity, and leaves final judgment to God.

In short, the passage defines victory over evil as **refusing to be corrupted by evil, actively doing good to those who oppose you, and trusting that the just and compassionate God will bring about the true resolution**. It is not weak conquest; it is the strong, costly, and transformative way of conquering evil rather than being conquered by it.
