Repentance here is not merely a change of behavior; it is a turning of the whole person back to God.

The Hebrew terms create a deliberate pair: the wicked person’s **“way”** and the unrighteous person’s **“thoughts.”**

- **“Way”** means one’s outward path, conduct, habits, relationships, and visible lifestyle.
- **“Thoughts”** means more than passing mental images. It points to inner plans, intentions, desires, imaginations, and the mental world that fuels action.

So repentance involves both because sin is both acted and imagined. A person may change outward behavior while still clinging inwardly to pride, lust, resentment, unbelief, or self-trust. Conversely, someone may have changed feelings or ideas without yet abandoning the sinful course of life. True repentance renounces the whole system: the deeds and the mentality that produces them. It is not just “stop sinning,” but “return to the Lord.”

This has several implications:

- **Outward conduct matters.** Real repentance produces moral change, not merely regret or religious emotion.
- **Inward imagination matters.** God deals with the heart and mind. The imaginations, fantasies, schemes, and narratives in which evil is cherished must be renounced, not just concealed or suppressed.
- **Mercy is deeper than shame.** God does not invite only “acceptable” sinners. The one whose deeds are tangled and whose thoughts are disordered is still called to return: “let him return unto the Lord, and he will have mercy.” The promise is not grudging pardon, but **abundant pardon**.

Thus the verse holds together two truths: repentance must be thorough—outwardly and inwardly—but God’s mercy is greater than the sin being forsaken. This is why the call is urgent: seek Him while He may be found, because His pardon is more than sufficient.
