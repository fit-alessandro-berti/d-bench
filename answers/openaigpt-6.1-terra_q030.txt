Paul describes love partly by what it refuses to do because pride, envy, irritability, and suspicion can turn relationships into contests over status or self-protection. In the context of a Corinthian church troubled by rivalry, love is not merely a warm feeling; it is a way of treating others that resists the urge to make oneself the center.

The strength proposed here is **self-mastery exercised for another’s good**. Patience and kindness are not presented as weakness, but as the capacity to bear frustration without retaliating, to celebrate another’s good without envy, and to seek truth rather than victory. “Thinketh no evil” can also suggest not keeping a running account of wrongs.

This love is resilient, but not blind. Its hope and willingness to trust belong alongside its rejoicing in truth. The passage need not mean believing every claim or submitting to abuse; enduring love can also require discernment, honesty, and firm boundaries.
