The image is sharp because it is at once absurd, physical, and diagnostic.

### 1. The absurd disproportion

A *mote* is a speck of dust or chaff — tiny, irritating, hard to see. A *beam* is a timber, the kind that holds up a roof.

Jesus reverses the expected scale. The fault-finder has perfect eyesight for the smallest flaw in someone else, and total blindness to the enormous thing protruding from his own face. It is visual comedy: a man with a plank sticking out of his eye leaning in to perform delicate eye surgery on his friend.

That disproportion is the point. Hypocrisy is not simply “having faults while noticing faults in others” — everyone does that. It is gross imbalance in attention: minute scrutiny outward, zero scrutiny inward.

The verbs underline it: “why *beholdest* thou the mote... but *considerest not* the beam?” He studies the brother; he does not even notice himself.

### 2. It is in the *eye*

Jesus does not choose a speck on a coat and a beam on a shoulder. Both objects are in the organ of sight itself.

That means the beam does not just make the hypocrite morally compromised; it makes him *perceptually unfit*. He literally cannot see clearly. His judgment is distorted by the very thing he refuses to deal with, yet he confidently offers to fix someone else’s vision.

There is also an intimacy to it. Removing something from an eye requires closeness, steadiness, gentleness, and clear sight. The would-be helper is proposing the most delicate operation while in the least fit condition to perform it.

### 3. “Thou hypocrite” names play-acting

In Greek, *hypokrites* was an actor — someone speaking behind a mask. The hypocrite here is playing a role: the concerned, discerning brother who says, “Let me pull out the mote out of thine eye.”

His offer of help sounds kind, but it is a performance of moral superiority while his own major problem remains untouched. Jesus unmasks it not by questioning whether the brother has a mote — he may well have one — but by exposing the false position from which the “help” is offered.

### 4. The key is “first... and then”

The passage does *not* end with, “So leave thy brother alone.” It ends with:

> “*first* cast out the beam out of thine own eye; *and then* shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote out of thy brother’s eye.”

That sequence is essential. Moral correction is not forbidden; it is reordered.

- **First self-examination, then fraternal correction.** You cannot credibly or safely address in another what you are willfully ignoring in yourself.
- **The qualification for helping is clear sight, not sinlessness.** Jesus does not demand that you reach perfection before you ever help anyone. The beam removed is what allows you to *see clearly*.
- **Having removed your own beam changes how you remove a mote.** Someone who has faced his own larger fault will approach his brother with humility rather than contempt, and with gentleness rather than theatrical superiority. He knows the operation hurts.

Note also that the other person is still called “thy brother.” This is not distant denunciation of enemies or outsiders. It is correction inside a relationship of mutual responsibility — but responsibility begins at home.

In short, Jesus is not teaching “never judge” or “everyone has problems, so say nothing.” He is teaching that self-deceived, self-excusing judgment is blindness disguised as insight. Remove the beam, gain clear sight, *then* — as a humbled brother, not an actor — you may help remove the speck.
