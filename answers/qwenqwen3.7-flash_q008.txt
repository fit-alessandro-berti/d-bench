First John 4:20 operates at the intersection of Johannine theology and pastoral ethics. To answer your two questions, we need to look at the verse’s immediate context, its underlying theological framework, and the psychological-spiritual dynamics John addresses.

### 🔹 Why love of the visible neighbor serves as a test of love for the invisible God

1. **The Nature of God as Love (vv. 7–8)**  
   John grounds his argument not in abstract duty but in divine ontology: *“God is love.”* If God’s essential nature is love, then anyone truly connected to Him will participate in that same reality. Love for God cannot remain isolated; it necessarily overflows into love for those who share His image and have been redeemed by His presence. Vertical and horizontal love are causally linked in John’s theology.

2. **The Incarnational Pattern (vv. 9–11)**  
   God’s love was not revealed through a distant vision but through the tangible, historical self-giving of Jesus Christ. *“God showed his love among us in that he sent his only Son…”* If believers claim communion with the invisible God but refuse the visible imitation Christ modeled, their claim breaks the incarnational logic of the faith. Loving the unseen God faithfully requires loving those He has visibly placed in our sphere of responsibility.

3. **The Imago Dei Principle**  
   Though not explicitly quoted here, the Jewish-Christian assumption behind John’s argument is that every human bears God’s image (Gen 1:26–27; cf. James 3:9). To “hate” (strongly resent, reject, or refuse compassion toward) a brother is to slight the One whose image they bear. You cannot honor the invisible Archetype while despising the visible creature made in His likeness.

4. **Johannine Epistemology: Truth Embodied**  
   Throughout 1 John, genuine knowledge of God is validated by practice: *“Whoever says ‘I know him’ but does not keep his commandments is a liar”* (2:4); *“Let us not love in word or talk but in deed and in truth”* (3:18). For John, faith that refuses relational manifestation is epistemologically suspect. The visible neighbor functions as the empirical test ground where invisible love either proves real or reveals itself as counterfeit.

### 🔹 What kind of self-deception is being exposed?

John is diagnosing a specific form of spiritual illusion common in communities where orthodoxy had become detached from orthopraxy:

1. **Moral Dissociation (Compartmentalized Faith)**  
   The deceiver separates worship from ethics, treating love for God as a private, devotional experience while excusing hostility, indifference, or contempt toward actual people. This mirrors the Pharisaic error Jesus condemned: honoring God with lips while the heart clings to exclusion and disdain (Matt 15:8; Jas 1:27).

2. **Conceptual Idolatry**  
   Lovers of God may begin to worship an idea of God rather than the God who commands concrete love. When devotion becomes purely aesthetic, emotional, or doctrinal, it escapes relational accountability. Hatred toward a visible brother reveals that the “God” being loved is actually a projection, not the Living God who defines Himself as self-giving love.

3. **Avoidance of Covenant Accountability**  
   Loving imperfect people requires patience, forgiveness, humility, and sacrifice. Hating a brother is often easier because it preserves spiritual self-image without demanding costly obedience. The deceiver uses lofty religious language to mask relational cowardice or pride.

4. **Confusing Experience with Obedience**  
   John repeatedly warns against mistaking spiritual feelings, gifts, or confessions for saving faith (1:6, 2:4, 3:19–20). Emotional highs or intellectual assent do not validate covenant loyalty. Only love that acts as Christ loved confirms true union with Him.

### 📖 In Summary
First John 4:20 treats love for the visible neighbor as the unavoidable stress-test for love of the invisible God because **God makes Himself known relationally, not abstractly**. The self-deception exposed is the attempt to maintain religious authenticity while evading the relational obedience that defines true communion with God. As John concludes immediately afterward: *“And this is his commandment, that we should believe in the name of his Son Jesus Christ and love one another… And whoever keeps his commandments abides in him, and he in them”* (1 John 3:23–24). Love for God and love for people are not two commands; they are one covenant reality lived in two dimensions.
