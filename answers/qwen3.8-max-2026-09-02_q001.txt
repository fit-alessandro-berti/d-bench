Genesis 4:6–7 presents moral agency as **responsible, addressable, and called to rule**. Cain is not treated as a passive victim of feelings or forces. God speaks to him as someone who can hear, understand, and choose.

Evil is not presented merely as:

- **impulse**: Cain’s anger is real, but the text does not reduce evil to an involuntary feeling;
- **beast**: the imagery is animal-like — sin “crouches” or “lies” at the door — but it is not a literal animal;
- **habit**: sin has not yet become settled character; it is waiting at the threshold;
- **temptation only**: it is temptation, but more intense than a passing lure.

The best description is: **sin as a personified, predatory power or temptation seeking mastery**.

The Hebrew phrase is striking: “sin crouches at the door” — *ḥaṭṭāʾt rōbēṣ*. The verb suggests an animal lying in wait, ready to spring. Sin is pictured as something near, active, desirous: “its desire is for you.” It wants Cain — not merely to annoy him, but to master him.

Yet the text does not say sin will inevitably master him. It says:

> “you must rule over it.”

That is the key statement on moral agency. The human person is portrayed as one who **can and should resist**, who is not excused by the presence of temptation. The door metaphor is important: sin is at the threshold, not yet fully inside. The person stands at the point of consent.

So the responsibility placed on Cain is severalfold:

1. **He must examine his inner state** — “Why are you angry? Why is your face fallen?”  
   His anger is not morally neutral; it is a warning sign.

2. **He must do well** — “If you do well, will you not be accepted?”  
   The text assumes a real possibility of right action.

3. **He must not yield to sin’s desire** — sin seeks to rule him, but he is called to rule it.

4. **He is accountable for his response** — the danger is not that temptation exists, but that he may surrender authority to it.

In short, Genesis 4:7 presents a severe but clear picture of moral agency: **evil is real, aggressive, and predatory, but the human being remains responsible to master it.** The text does not allow Cain to say, “I could not help it.” Temptation is powerful; consent is still his.
