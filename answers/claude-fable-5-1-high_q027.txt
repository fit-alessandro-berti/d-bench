Short answer: none of the five candidates is the root, and one of them is not even present. The Pharisee is not condemned for false doctrine, and his virtues are not condemned as virtues. What condemns him is the stance he has taken toward God and neighbour, which Luke names in the frame verse before the parable begins: he "trusted in himself that he was righteous, and despised others." Pride, comparison, contempt and self-display are four visible symptoms of that single underlying stance. The tax collector is treated as spiritually truer not because his feelings are more sincere but because his posture matches reality: he stands before God as one who has nothing to offer and needs to be given something, which is what both men in fact are.

Working through the candidates one by one:

1. False doctrine. Ruled out. Nothing the Pharisee says is theologically wrong. He thanks God rather than boasting outright, which is the correct form. Fasting twice a week and tithing on everything (going beyond the letter of the Torah) were regarded as pious excellence, not error. Jesus never disputes his facts. He is not an extortioner or adulterer; the tax collector very likely was dishonest. The parable works precisely because the Pharisee is doctrinally and morally "right" and still goes home unjustified. If his problem were bad theology, the shock of the ending would vanish.

2. Pride. Closer, but too vague as stated. His pride is not ordinary vanity about talent or status. It is pride in his righteousness specifically, and it takes a religious form: a prayer that is really an inventory. Luke's tell is the phrase "prayed thus with himself" (pros heauton). The Greek is ambiguous between "standing by himself" and "praying to himself," and Luke probably intends the pun. The Pharisee addresses God, but the true audience and the true subject of the prayer is himself. He asks God for nothing. There is no petition, no confession, no need. A prayer with no request in it is not really addressed to anyone but the speaker. That is the real content of "pride" here: self-sufficiency before God.

3. Comparison. This is the mechanism by which the pride expresses itself. Note that the Pharisee cannot describe his righteousness positively; he can only describe it negatively, as not being other people: "not as other men are, extortioners, unjust, adulterers, or even as this publican." His identity is entirely relational and downward-looking. This is a real diagnostic point: righteousness that can only be measured by looking at who is beneath you is not being measured against God at all. But comparison is a symptom. A person secure in God's mercy does not need to rank himself, so the compulsion to rank reveals that the security is resting on something else.

4. Contempt. This is the moral fruit and the reason the frame verse mentions it explicitly ("despised others," exoutheneo, to treat as nothing). Contempt is what pride does when it meets a neighbour. The Pharisee's comparison is not merely descriptive; it is a dismissal. "Even as this publican" turns a fellow worshipper in the same temple into a prop for his own prayer. So contempt is not the root, but it is where the parable's ethical edge lands: the man who is confident of his righteousness has stopped seeing the other man as a person to whom mercy is due. Luke pairs this with the surrounding material (the unjust judge, the children, the rich ruler) to make the point that the kingdom is for those who know their need, and that the sure sign of not knowing your need is that you look down on those who do.

5. Virtue as self-display. This is partly right but needs a correction. It is not that his virtue is only a performance; he really does fast and tithe. The problem is what the virtue has become for him: evidence in his own favour, a case to be filed before God. His works have moved from being a response to God into being grounds for standing before God. The Pharisee treats his righteousness as a possession ("all that I possess," and the fasting and tithing belong to the same list). Once righteousness is a possession, it is something you can be proud of, compare, and use to look down on others. So "self-display" is a good name for the outward form, but the inward fault is self-reliance, which is exactly what the frame verse says: he "trusted in himself."

So the answer to "what exactly condemns him" is: misplaced trust. He has trusted in himself that he was righteous. Everything else (pride, comparison, contempt, display) follows from where his trust rests. Paul's later language is exactly this: he seeks to establish his own righteousness rather than to receive God's.

Why the tax collector's posture is treated as truer:

First, because it is accurate. The tax collector is a sinner, and he says so. He does not even claim the general category ("a sinner") but, in the Greek, "the sinner" (to hamartolo): as far as he is concerned, he is the sinner in the room. He is not comparing himself to anyone. He has no interest in the Pharisee at all. His eyes are down, so he cannot look around; his whole attention is on God and on his own need. The Pharisee's prayer is about the Pharisee and about the publican; the publican's prayer is about God and himself only. That is what right orientation in prayer looks like.

Second, because it is a real prayer, that is, a request. "God be merciful to me" (hilastheti moi, "be propitiated toward me," the word associated with atonement and the mercy seat) is the only petition in the parable. He asks for the one thing that can actually be given: mercy. He does not attempt a defence, a mitigation, or a promise of reform. He simply throws himself on God's willingness to forgive. This is what faith is in Luke's gospel: not a doctrine correctly held, but a person casting himself on God with empty hands.

Third, because his body tells the truth. He stands far off (he does not presume on nearness), he does not lift his eyes (he does not claim the right to look), and he beats his breast (a gesture of grief and self-accusation, elsewhere in Luke reserved for the crowd at the crucifixion). None of this is a technique; it is what a man looks like when he knows what he is. The Pharisee's posture ("stood and prayed") is upright and secure; it also tells the truth, but the truth it tells is that he thinks he is standing on his own.

Fourth, and this is the point of the verdict: "this man went down to his house justified." Justified is a courtroom and covenant word: declared in the right, accepted. The tax collector is not declared righteous because his humility is a superior form of virtue that outbids fasting and tithing. That reading would just make humility the new merit and reproduce the Pharisee's mistake. He is justified because he has taken the only posture in which righteousness can be received rather than claimed. Righteousness in the parable is a gift, and the gift can only go to open hands. The Pharisee's hands are full, of his own works. The tax collector's are empty, so God fills them.

The closing line Luke attaches (v.14) confirms this: "every one that exalteth himself shall be abased; and he that humbleth himself shall be exalted." Humbling oneself is not self-hatred. It is accepting the true position of a creature and a sinner before God, and asking for what only God can give. Exalting oneself is not confidence as such; it is trusting in oneself and making others the measure of one's standing.

One warning the parable builds in: the reader who finishes it thinking "thank God I am not like that Pharisee" has just become him. The only safe way to read it is from the tax collector's position, which is what Luke intends.
