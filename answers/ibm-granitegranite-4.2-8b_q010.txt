**1. What kind of power is being praised?**  
The passage praises *self-determined humility exercised within true greatness*. Specifically:  
> Christ "was in the form of God" (i.e., possessed the full, inherent status and authority of God), yet "thought it not robbery to be equal with God." He *voluntarily* relinquished that status—not because he was weak, powerless, or unaware of his equality, but by conscious choice: "made himself of no reputation, took upon him the form of a servant, ... became obedient unto death." Thus, the praised power is **God’s sovereign, full divine nature choosing, freely and intentionally, to empty itself and serve in human likeness**. It is not hidden, coerced, or diminished *by circumstance*; it is actively surrendered *by will*. The praise centers on this deliberate, humble use of greatness—not domination, not mere suffering, but the conscious decision to set aside divine equality for service.

**2. Why can humility joined to greatness feel more unsettling than open domination?**  
Open domination (e.g., a ruler plainly claiming and asserting power over others) feels more predictable and easier to evaluate because its claim to power is *explicit and self-assertive*. We can readily see, resist, accept, or critique it based on visible action and stated intent ("I am in charge"). It aligns with common expectations about power: power *uses* authority.

Humility joined to greatness—as in Christ—feels unsettling because it *subverts that expectation in a quiet, internal, and paradoxical way*:  
> It shows the holder of supreme power *choosing not to assert, display, or claim that power on their own behalf*. Instead, they actively *lower themselves* (emptying reputation, taking a servant’s form, enduring death), while still *possessing* the full reality of who they are (in the form of God). The power itself is not hidden or false; it is real and sovereign. But the *expression* of that power is consistently *withdrawn, surrendered, and directed toward service*. This creates cognitive and emotional tension:  
> - It challenges the usual link between power and visibility/assertion (we expect powerful people to *use* power; here the powerful *releases* it).  
> - It feels counter-intuitive: why would the most powerful being willingly become "of no reputation" and take on the lowest form? It seems *unnecessary* from a pragmatic standpoint (why not stay visible and authoritative?).  
> - It reveals depth: humility here is not passivity or weakness but a *deliberate, courageous choice* ("thought it not robbery"), grounded in love and purpose (serving others). This depth can feel surprising or profound, prompting reflection ("How can true greatness choose to be small?").  
> - It lacks performative cues. Open domination is usually obvious (titles, commands, displays). Christ’s humility is quiet ("made himself of no reputation," "found in fashion as a man"); the greatness remains implicit but real, making the contrast sharper and harder to dismiss as mere modesty. The unsettlingness arises because it meets our expectation of power with its opposite *on purpose*, revealing a deeper, quieter kind of authority that governs through service rather than claim.

**In short:** The passage praises *God’s sovereign power consciously choosing humility—voluntarily emptying divine equality for human service*. Humility joined to greatness feels unsettling because it quietly reverses the everyday expectation that power *asserts* itself; instead, it *releases* itself, which is paradoxical, counter-intuitive, and reveals a deeper depth of choice that surprises us. Open domination, by contrast, aligns with the usual visible expression of power, making it easier to recognize and evaluate, even if critiqued.
