**The power being praised**

In Philippians 2:5‑8 the author is highlighting **the paradoxical power of humility that flows from a divine identity**.  
- Christ “was in the form of God” yet “thought it not robbery to be equal with God.”  
- He “made himself of no reputation” and “took upon him the form of a servant,” adopting the human likeness.  
- By “being found in fashion as a man, he humbled himself, and became obedient unto death, even the death of the cross.”

This is **the power of self‑emptying obedience**—the ability to *be* fully God while simultaneously *choosing* to live out His humanity by submitting to death. In other words, it is the **power of humility that generates greatness**, or the **power of Christ’s incarnational identity expressed through servant‑like humility**.

---

### Why “humility + greatness” feels more unsettling than “open domination”

| Aspect | Open domination (arrogance) | Humility + Greatness (Christ’s example) |
|--------|------------------------------|------------------------------------------|
| **Source of authority** | Self‑assertion, “I am above.” | Divine authority exercised *through* human vulnerability. |
| **Attitude toward others** | Dominates, imposes will, seeks recognition. | Serves, puts others first, seeks reconciliation. |
| **Expectation of the believer** | Pride, confidence, control. | Dependence on God, reliance on grace, surrender. |
| **Resulting effect** | Threatens the dignity of the oppressed; breeds fear. | Transforms the believer into a model of love and sacrifice; inspires hope. |

1. **It challenges our ordinary notions of power.** We tend to associate power with domination—holding the throne, commanding respect. Seeing someone who is *both* God and man yet *chooses* to be a servant flips that script. The very idea that a being of supreme worth can *reject* its own greatness and adopt a low status is startling.

2. **Humility paired with greatness creates a tension that forces spiritual growth.** True greatness isn’t measured by how much we can command; it’s measured by how readily we *yield* to a higher purpose. When we join humility with the divine attributes, we discover that our “strength” comes from surrender, not from force. This can feel unsettling because it demands we abandon the instinctive drive to dominate.

3. **“Open domination” is inherently self‑centered and alienating.** If Christ were openly ruling the world with his own will, he would be a tyrant. The gospel instead presents Him as the *example* of how to exercise authority responsibly—by loving, suffering, and serving. That image is far less comfortable to the heart than the familiar, domineering posture.

4. **The cross underscores the ultimate cost of such power.** By “becoming obedient unto death, even the death of the cross,” Christ shows that the greatest expression of his greatness is *self‑giving* obedience. The willingness to die for us transforms ordinary human power into a salvific, redemptive force—a transformation that most people find terrifying.

---

### Bottom line

Philippians 2:5‑8 praises **the power of humility that springs from divine greatness**—the ability to be fully God yet choose to live as a servant, to put Himself in the likeness of men and to submit to death. This humility‑plus‑greatness dynamic is unsettling because it shatters the habit of viewing power as domination; it reveals that true greatness is not about claiming supremacy but about surrendering to love, service, and the cross. Open domination, by contrast, is arrogant, exclusive, and antithetical to the gospel’s call to humility and self‑emptying.
