The elder brother's deepest problem is not his anger — his anger is only the symptom.

In Luke 15:11-32, Jesus is answering the grumbling of the Pharisees and scribes in Luke 15:1-2: *“This man receiveth sinners, and eateth with them.”* In the parable, the younger son = the tax collectors and sinners, the Father = God, and the elder brother = the Pharisees who are listening to the story.

All five things you listed are present in him, but they are layered on top of each other:

### 1. Anger is the surface — what everyone sees
> “And he was angry, and would not go in” v.28

The Greek is *orgisthe de* — he became enraged. He refuses to participate in the father's joy. He separates himself from the household even while standing in the field. Anger is the eruption, not the root.

### 2. Wounded fairness and envy are the felt experience — what he would say is wrong
> “...thou never gavest me a kid, that I might make merry with my friends: But as soon as this thy son was come... thou hast killed for him the fatted calf.” v.29-30

He keeps accounts. A kid vs. a fatted calf. My friends vs. his harlots. My faithful service vs. his wasteful rebellion. He feels his loyalty has gone unrewarded while sin has been rewarded.

And his complaint is revealingly inaccurate. According to v.12 the father had already divided his living — his *bios* — between them. And in v.31 the father corrects him: *“Son, thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine.”*

Under Deuteronomy 21:17, as the elder he would have received the double portion. The fatted calf *was already his*. The kid he wanted for his friends was already his to take. His sense of deprivation was an illusion — he lived as a servant in his father’s house rather than as a son.

### 3. Pride in obedience is the structure — how he justifies himself
> “Lo, these many years do I serve thee, neither transgressed I at any time thy commandment” v.29

Two telling words:

1. **“I serve thee”** — The word is *douleuo* — to serve as a slave. Not *“I have loved thee as a son.”* He has reduced sonship to servitude, relationship to contract, love to labor.

2. **“neither transgressed I”** — No younger son, and no honest Pharisee, could say this with truth. It is self-righteousness. He believes his outward compliance has put the father in his debt.

He also will not say “my brother.” He says *“this thy son.”* v.30 — he disowns the brother to accuse the father.

This is the second kind of lostness in the chapter. The younger son was lost by *leaving* — prodigality, rebellion. The elder son is lost by *staying* — religion without love. One ran from the father's house geographically, the other was distant relationally while never leaving the field.

### 4. Discomfort with mercy itself is the root — what explains everything else

This is why his pride cannot tolerate the celebration:

> “It was meet that we should make merry, and be glad: for this thy brother was dead, and is alive again” v.32

The father does not say “it was meet because he has now earned it.” He says it was meet because he was *dead* and is *alive*. Grace by definition is not fairness. Fairness would have left the younger son in the far country. Mercy is what brings him home.

The elder brother wanted a father who pays wages for obedience. What he got was a father who gives gifts to the dead when they repent — and that offends a wage-earner’s heart.

He cannot rejoice because joy at that table requires two confessions he will not make:

1. “I too need mercy, not just wages” — and
2. “My brother does not need to earn mercy any more than I do.”

That is why the parable ends without a resolution. The younger son's story is resolved — he came in. The elder son is left standing outside, being entreated by the father who came out to him in v.28 just as he once came out to welcome the younger.

The question Jesus leaves hanging for the Pharisees — and for the reader — is: Will you come in?

In short: **Anger is the eruption. Envy and wounded fairness are the language. Pride in obedience is the architecture. But the foundation is a refusal to understand sonship as grace — and therefore an inability to rejoice when grace is given to another sinner.**
