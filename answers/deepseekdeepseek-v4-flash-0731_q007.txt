It’s not the same at all. In fact, Luke 6:37 itself is a moral judgment: it says that some ways of judging are wrong and that mercy is right. If moral judgments never mattered, Jesus wouldn’t say “judge not,” and He wouldn’t say “forgive”—forgiveness only makes sense when real wrong has happened.

So the issue isn’t whether we make moral judgments. The issue is *how* we make them and *what we do with them*.

## What Jesus is forbidding

Jesus is forbidding the self-righteous, merciless attitude of condemnation. The word He uses for “condemn” is a legal word—it means to pass sentence, to write someone off as guilty with no appeal. He is warning us against the appetite to condemn: the desire to sit in the judge’s seat, to expose, to punish, to feel superior.

He says this right after: “Be merciful, just as your Father is merciful.” So the command is not “don’t have standards.” It’s “let your judgments be shaped by mercy, the way God’s judgment toward you is shaped by mercy.”

The next verse says, “Forgive, and you will be forgiven.” That shows that Jesus is not talking about moral indifference. He is talking about how we treat people when they *are* guilty. Do we forgive, or do we condemn?

## Moral judgments are necessary

The Bible repeatedly commands moral discernment:

- Jesus says, “Do not judge by appearances, but judge with right judgment” (John 7:24).
- Paul tells Christians to “test everything; hold fast what is good” (1 Thessalonians 5:21).
- Jesus says we will know false prophets “by their fruits” (Matthew 7:15–20)—which requires evaluating a person’s teaching and behavior.
- In Matthew 18, Jesus gives a whole process for confronting sin in the church. That requires discerning that sin is real.

So moral judgments absolutely matter. Without them, there is no justice, no protection for the vulnerable, no truth, no repentance, and no forgiveness.

## Discernment vs. the appetite to condemn

The difference is not *whether* you judge. It’s *what your heart is doing* when you judge.

**Discernment** is like a doctor diagnosing a disease. It names what is wrong in order to heal. It is humble because the doctor also knows he is not immune to disease. It leaves final judgment to God. It is grieved by sin, not thrilled by it.

**The appetite to condemn** is different. It uses moral truth as a weapon. It enjoys seeing someone else exposed. It assumes the worst motives. It is blind to its own faults. It does not want restoration—it wants punishment. It acts as if it holds God’s final verdict.

Jesus makes this clear in the next passage:

> “Why do you look at the speck in your brother’s eye, but do not notice the log in your own eye? … First take the log out of your own eye, and then you will see clearly to take out the speck in your brother’s eye.”  
> —Luke 6:41–42

Notice: Jesus doesn’t say “there is no speck.” He says the speck is real. But He says you first deal with your own sin, so that you can help your brother with humility, not condemn him with pride.

## The bottom line

Luke 6:37 is not saying “moral judgments never matter.” It is saying:

> Judgment without mercy is dangerous. Discernment is good. Condemnation is toxic. The standard you use on others is the standard that will be applied to you.

So make moral judgments. Name sin truthfully. Seek justice. But do it as someone who has also been forgiven—not as someone who wants to condemn. That is the difference between discernment and the appetite to condemn.
