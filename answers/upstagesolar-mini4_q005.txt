Enemy-love is difficult to take seriously not because it is a vague or sentimental ideal, but because it collides with our lived experience, psychological defenses, and rational caution. Yet when examined closely, it is none of those things. It is not merely a feeling of warmth toward someone who has harmed you, nor a cynical tactic to manipulate people into liking you. It is better understood as a disciplined form of moral strength—one that requires the hardest kind of inner work.

### 1. Why it is hard to take seriously
**It contradicts our natural survival instincts and sense of justice.** Human psychology is wired to distinguish friend from foe, to defend ourselves, and to resent those who harm us. The desire for fairness, reciprocity, and proportional response is deeply rooted. When someone curses you, hates you, or persecutes you, the intuitive moral response is anger, withdrawal, or retaliation. Enemy-love asks us to step outside that immediate moral equilibrium, which is psychologically costly.

**It feels counterintuitive to empathy.** We tend to think of love as something that naturally flows toward the lovable. Enemy-love means loving people who, by every social and moral metric, are not currently deserving of affection. This makes it seem either naive, sentimental, or dangerously detached from reality. It challenges our unexamined assumption that morality is about who we should care about, rather than about what kind of persons we should become.

**It can be weaponized or misunderstood.** In practice, calls for enemy-love can be used to pressure victims into passivity, or to frame justice as weakness. When stripped of its theological foundation, it can sound like an advice column platitude: “Just forgive and move on.” That misreading makes the teaching seem sentimental because it is often offered without the durability, discipline, and structural context that make it meaningful.

### 2. Why it is not merely sentimental
Sentimentality implies that enemy-love is a pleasant feeling, a temporary emotion, or a way to make oneself feel good. But Jesus’ command does not ask for a warm emotion. It asks for three concrete actions: **bless, do good, and pray**. These are not feelings; they are practices. They require behavioral choice, restraint, and sustained effort. You can hate someone and still choose to wish them well, to treat them justly, and to intercede for them. Sentimental love flows easily; enemy-love requires the will to override natural aversion. That is not sentiment—it is discipline.

### 3. Why it is not merely strategic
A strategic reading would say: “Be nice to your enemies because it benefits you, makes them uncomfortable, or earns you social respect.” But Jesus’ stated purpose in the text is not personal gain, social approval, or even moral superiority. He says: “That ye may be the children of your Father which is in heaven.” The goal is **imitation of God**, not optimization of one’s own life. If enemy-love were strategic, the reward would be a better outcome for the practitioner. But the reward or meaning here is identity: becoming like a God who does not distinguish between the deserving and the undemanding, the good and the evil. Strategy serves the self; this command surrenders the self’s immediate posture and aligns it with a higher moral pattern. That is not manipulation. It is formation.

### 4. Why it is a deeper form of moral strength
At its core, enemy-love is a form of moral strength because it redefines power. It refuses to let injustice define you, refuses to let your adversary control your emotional or spiritual state, and refuses to lower yourself to the level of retaliation. It requires:

- **Self-mastery:** The capacity to choose love in the face of enmity, rather than being hijacked by instinct.
- **Justice beyond retaliation:** It does not say to ignore harm or abandon boundaries. It says to do good to those who hate you, which implies a commitment to the well-being of others even while maintaining distance or accountability.
- **Theological realism:** It acknowledges the evil in the world (evil and good, just and unjust equally receive sun and rain) but refuses to let that reality make one cynical or cruel. It holds the tension between human brokenness and divine grace.

In this sense, enemy-love is not weakness disguised as love. It is the courage to break the cycle of enmity, the strength to absorb hostility without returning it, and the moral imagination to see an enemy not merely as a threat, but as a fellow creature made for redemption. It is, as the text says, becoming “the children of your Father”—a transformation of identity that begins with a decision, sustained by practice, and rooted in something beyond the self.

### Conclusion
Enemy-love is hard to take seriously because it asks us to act against our deepest instincts and cultural assumptions. But it is not sentimental because it is action, not feeling. It is not strategic because its aim is divine imitation, not self-interest. It is a deeper moral strength because it demands self-giving discipline, refuses the logic of retaliation, and reorients the human heart toward a pattern of love that only becomes sustainable when grounded in something larger than oneself. The difficulty of taking it seriously is precisely what makes it serious: it is not a theory to be admired, but a practice to be lived, one blessing, one good deed, and one prayer at a time.
