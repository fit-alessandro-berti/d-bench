This verse is often misread as: "Morality is relative, so never say anything is wrong."

Jesus is saying almost the opposite. Notice the logic in the verse itself:

> Judge not... condemn not... **forgive, and ye shall be forgiven.**

Forgiveness only makes sense if *something really was wrong*. If moral judgments never mattered, there would be nothing to forgive. Jesus isn't abolishing right and wrong — he's addressing *how* we handle right and wrong in others.

Context helps. Luke 6:36-42 is all one thought:

36. "Be ye therefore merciful, as your Father also is merciful."
37. Judge not, condemn not, forgive, give.
38. ...with the same measure that ye mete withal it shall be measured to you again.
39-42. ...why beholdest thou the mote that is in thy brother's eye, but perceivest not the beam that is in thine own eye?

He doesn't say "pretend there is no mote." He says "take the beam out of your own eye first, so you can see clearly to help your brother."

That's the difference between **discernment** and **condemnation.**

In Greek, Luke actually uses two different words: *krinete* (judge) and *katadikazete* (condemn, pass final sentence upon). Jesus uses discernment constantly himself — "judge righteous judgment" (John 7:24), "ye shall know them by their fruits" (Matt. 7:16), reproving Pharisees, naming sin, calling to repentance. The New Testament never commands blindness.

What he forbids is the *appetite* to condemn.

Think of it like this:

**1. Discernment diagnoses. Condemnation sentences.**
Discernment says: "That action is dishonest, harmful, unfaithful — it's wrong." It names reality to protect truth and to help heal.
Condemnation says: "You *are* that wrong, and that's all you are. You're finished." It collapses the person into their failure and closes the door.

God reserves that final sentencing for Himself. James puts it bluntly: "There is one lawgiver, who is able to save and to destroy: who art thou that judgest another?" (James 4:12)

**2. Discernment begins with self. Condemnation begins with superiority.**
Discernment asks: Am I seeing clearly? What is my part in this? Am I holding the other person to a standard I excuse in myself?
The condemning spirit loves the speck in the other eye because it distracts from the beam in our own. It feels good to expose, to be certain, to be above.

Jesus says your measure becomes your mirror: the harsh, unforgiving measure you use on others will be the measure that judges you.

**3. Discernment aims to restore. Condemnation aims to enjoy punishing.**
The test is motive and destination:
- Do I want to *understand in order to help*? Or do I want to *catch in order to dismiss?*
- Am I relishing being right? Am I gossiping, savoring someone's downfall, refusing context, refusing complexity, refusing mercy?
- If they repented, would I be disappointed or delighted?

Paul gives the discernment-model: "Brethren, if a man be overtaken in a fault, ye which are spiritual, restore such an one in the spirit of meekness; considering thyself, lest thou also be tempted." (Gal. 6:1)

Restoration *requires* judgment — you can't restore what you won't name. But it is judgment bathed in meekness and self-knowledge.

So a practical check when you feel that tug to judge:

1. **Is it true?** Discernment loves truth, not rumor. Get facts, see fruit over time.
2. **What is my posture?** Am I above them, or alongside them under the same mercy?
3. **Act vs. identity:** Can I name the wrong without branding the person as irredeemable?
4. **What is my end goal?** Exposure for its own sake, or forgiveness, healing, and right-making?

That’s why Luke 6:37 is not moral indifference. It’s moral seriousness *plus* mercy. Judge the action clearly, refuse the pleasure of damning the person, and stay open to the same forgiveness you will one day need yourself.

Like the woman taken in adultery in John 8: Jesus neither denies what she did, nor hands her over to her accusers: "Neither do I condemn thee: go, and sin no more." Truth and mercy, held together.
