The passage rejects worship so sharply because the problem is not **too much worship and too little justice**. It is worship being offered as though injustice did not fundamentally contradict the worshippers’ relationship with God. Amos is not asking them to balance two legitimate activities; he is exposing a contradiction at the heart of their religion.

### What makes the worship fraudulent?

Earlier in the chapter, Amos accuses Israel’s powerful people of trampling the poor, taking bribes, and denying justice to the needy at the city gate—the place where legal cases were heard (Amos 5:11–12). Against that background, the festivals, sacrifices, and music are not innocent practices needing a little ethical supplementation. They are the religious life of a community that honors God ceremonially while violating God’s demands socially.

The fraud is the implied claim: **“Our worship demonstrates that we are right with God, even while we wrong our neighbors.”**

It need not mean every worshipper is consciously pretending. People can sincerely enjoy worship and still use it to avoid moral truth. Amos exposes something deeper than insincere feelings: a religious system that can reassure people of their faithfulness without requiring them to confront the suffering they cause or tolerate.

### Why not ask for “better balance”?

Because justice is not a counterweight that makes oppression acceptable. More sacrifices cannot compensate for corrupt courts; more beautiful music cannot cancel exploitation.

A call for balance might leave the basic deception intact: keep the injustice, but add enough charitable or religious activity to offset it. God’s refusal blocks that transaction. The offerings cannot purchase divine acceptance, and the ceremonies cannot make God endorse the community’s account of itself.

The language is deliberately comprehensive: God will not smell the offerings, accept the sacrifices, or hear the songs. Everything the worshippers present as pleasing is refused. Their worship cannot control God’s response.

### What replaces the performance?

“Let judgment run down as waters, and righteousness as a mighty stream.”

Here “judgment” means justice, not merely punishment. The image demands something sustained and public: justice flowing through the community’s ordinary life, rather than appearing as an occasional gesture alongside entrenched wrongdoing. Righteousness must shape courts, economic dealings, and treatment of vulnerable people.

This is not a general rejection of ritual, music, or worship. It is a rejection of their use as substitutes for faithfulness.

The deepest spiritual fraud is **claiming communion with God while resisting God’s claim upon one’s treatment of others**. The corresponding moral fraud is using sacred observance to give injustice the appearance of respectability.

Amos’s point is therefore stronger than “practice what you preach”: **you cannot honor God by ceremonies that help you remain at peace with what God condemns.**
