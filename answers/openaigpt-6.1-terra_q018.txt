Paul places bitterness and forgiveness against each other because they represent opposing ways a community can respond to injury. Bitterness holds and expresses hostility; forgiveness, modeled on God’s forgiveness in Christ, releases the claim to hold a wrong against another.

In Ephesians 4:31, bitterness is the first in a cluster of things to be “put away”: rage, anger, shouting, abusive speech, and malice. These range from inward dispositions to outward behavior. Verse 32 then describes what should characterize the community instead: kindness, tenderness, and forgiveness.

So is bitterness a feeling, a habit, a posture, or something contagious? **The passage suggests it can involve all of these, though its emphasis is on what the community does with it.**

- **A feeling or disposition:** Bitterness can include lingering hurt and resentment. Paul is not necessarily saying that the first experience of pain or anger is itself sinful; earlier he acknowledges anger while warning against letting it lead to sin (4:26).
- **A sustained posture:** In this list, bitterness is more than a passing feeling. It is connected with hostility that can be retained and acted upon.
- **A social influence:** Paul does not explicitly call bitterness contagious here. But the movement from bitterness to shouting and abusive speech shows how inward resentment can affect other people and damage a community.

Forgiveness counters that pattern by offering a different response to wrongdoing: one shaped by kindness and by God’s forgiveness “in Christ” (4:32). Paul does not say that every experience of bitterness comes from unforgiveness, or that forgiveness instantly removes hurt. Nor does forgiving necessarily mean excusing harm, abandoning boundaries, or restoring trust without change.

The contrast is therefore not simply **“stop feeling bad and feel better.”** It is a call to move, individually and together, away from nurtured hostility and toward the gracious treatment of one another that Paul sees in God’s action in Christ.
