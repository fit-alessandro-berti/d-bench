Luke 6:37 is **not** saying, “Moral distinctions do not matter.” In fact, the command to forgive assumes that real wrong has been done. If nothing is truly wrong, there is nothing to forgive. The verse is not abolishing moral judgment; it is condemning a certain *way* of judging: harsh, self-righteous, merciless, finalizing judgment that delights in putting people down.

A helpful way to put it is:

> Jesus is not forbidding discernment. He is forbidding the appetite to condemn.

## 1. “Judge not” is not moral relativism

The wider context of Luke 6 does not teach moral indifference. Jesus speaks of good trees and bad trees, good fruit and bad fruit, wise and foolish builders, people who hear and do his words and people who do not. That requires moral discernment.

So the passage cannot mean:

- “Nothing is sinful.”
- “Do not call evil evil.”
- “Never evaluate anyone’s behavior.”
- “All actions are equally acceptable.”
- “There is no such thing as accountability.”

Rather, Jesus is warning against a spirit of judgment that is:

- self-exempting,
- merciless,
- eager to condemn,
- blind to its own fault,
- quick to finalize a person’s identity,
- unwilling to forgive.

This is why he adds: **“Forgive, and ye shall be forgiven.”** Forgiveness presupposes moral seriousness. It says, “What you did was wrong; it caused a debt; but I will not hold that debt over you forever.”

Moral judgment matters. The question is whether our judgment aims at truth and mercy, or at destruction and superiority.

## 2. Discernment is not the same as condemnation

Discernment says:  
**“This is wrong. This is harmful. This needs to be named, resisted, corrected, or repented of.”**

Condemnation says:  
**“You are beyond hope. You are contemptible. I have the right to finalize you.”**

Discernment can be severe, but it remains open to repentance, healing, and restoration. Condemnation tends to close the door. It treats a person as nothing more than their worst act.

For example:

- Discernment says, “That action was cruel and must be repented of.”
- Condemnation says, “You are worthless and deserve to be crushed.”

- Discernment says, “This teaching is false and dangerous.”
- Condemnation says, “Those who are deceived are contemptible and beyond redemption.”

- Discernment says, “This sin harms you and others; turn from it.”
- Condemnation says, “I take pleasure in exposing and punishing you.”

One seeks truth with sorrow; the other seeks victory with contempt.

## 3. The “appetite to condemn” has a distinctive taste

The phrase “appetite to condemn” is important. It suggests not merely that someone makes a moral judgment, but that they *hunger* for it. They enjoy it. They are eager to find fault. They feel strengthened by identifying who is guilty, who is outside, who is worse, who must be excluded.

That appetite often reveals itself by certain signs:

### It is quick

It rushes to a verdict. It does not wait to understand, to hear, to examine context, or to consider its own blindness.

### It is final

It speaks as though it knows the whole heart, the whole history, and the final state of a person before God.

### It is self-exempting

It sees the other person’s sin vividly but remains strangely blind to its own. This is exactly the point of the “mote” and the “beam” in the surrounding passage.

### It enjoys superiority

It may disguise itself as zeal for holiness, but its emotional fruit is pride, scorn, or grim satisfaction.

### It resists mercy

It treats forgiveness as weakness. It fears that if we show mercy, we will no longer feel morally superior.

### It reduces people to their worst moments

It no longer sees the person as made in God’s image, capable of repentance, or an object of divine patience.

That appetite is dangerous because it can feel righteous while being unmerciful.

## 4. Discernment is necessary, but it must be humble

Moral judgment is still necessary. In fact, without discernment, love becomes sentimental and useless. If we cannot name evil, we cannot protect the vulnerable, call sinners to repentance, pursue justice, or speak the truth.

But biblical discernment has a different posture from condemnation.

Discernment asks:

- “What is true here?”
- “What is good and what is evil?”
- “What harm is being done?”
- “What repentance or repair is needed?”
- “How can this person be restored?”
- “What must I first see in myself?”

The appetite to condemn asks:

- “Who can I blame?”
- “How can I prove I am right?”
- “How can I expose them?”
- “How can I make them pay?”
- “How does their failure confirm my goodness?”

Discernment wants healing. Condemnation wants punishment as its primary satisfaction.

## 5. Forgiveness does not mean pretending wrong did not happen

This is crucial. “Forgive, and ye shall be forgiven” does not mean:

- approve of the wrong,
- ignore the harm,
- pretend the sin was small,
- remove all consequences,
- silence the injured,
- require unsafe reconciliation,
- deny the need for repentance.

Forgiveness means refusing to keep the debt alive forever. It releases the demand for personal vengeance. It gives up the pleasure of holding someone permanently captive to their sin.

In that sense, forgiveness is not the opposite of moral judgment. It is moral judgment transformed by mercy.

## 6. The measure we use will be used on us

Luke 6:37 is closely connected to the verses around it:

> “For with the same measure that ye mete, it shall be measured to you again.”

This means our way of judging matters spiritually. If we judge with merciless severity, we are asking for a merciless standard to be applied to us. If we judge with humility, self-awareness, and willingness to forgive, we place ourselves in the path of mercy.

Jesus is not saying that God’s moral standards are flexible. He is saying that our posture toward others reveals our posture toward God’s mercy.

The person who refuses to forgive reveals that they do not understand the depth of their own need for forgiveness.

## 7. A simple distinction

We can summarize the difference like this:

| Discernment | Appetite to condemn |
|---|---|
| Names sin truthfully | Dwells on sin with relish |
| Begins with self-examination | Begins with self-exemption |
| Hates the wrong | Learns to hate the person |
| Seeks repentance and restoration | Seeks exposure and punishment |
| Grieves over evil | Takes pleasure in finding fault |
| Leaves room for mercy | Hardens into final judgment |
| Says, “This is wrong” | Says, “You are beyond hope” |
| Protects truth with humility | Uses truth as a weapon |
| Can forgive without denying evil | Refuses forgiveness to preserve superiority |

## 8. The deepest difference

The deepest difference is this:

**Discernment sees evil clearly but does not worship the verdict.**  
**The appetite to condemn makes the verdict into an idol.**

Discernment says, “Evil is real, and it must be addressed.”  
Condemnation says, “Evil gives me the right to despise, exclude, and destroy.”

Luke 6:37, therefore, is not teaching that moral judgments never matter. It is teaching that moral judgments are so serious that they must be made with trembling, mercy, and awareness that we too stand in need of forgiveness.

Jesus does not call us to moral blindness. He calls us to clear sight without contempt. He calls us to say, “This is wrong,” without becoming people who delight in saying, “You are doomed.”

That is the difference between **truth guided by mercy** and **truth corrupted by the desire to condemn**.
