# Discernment vs. the Appetite to Condemn

## The verse presupposes moral judgment

Read closely, Luke 6:37 can't mean that moral judgments don't matter. Its third clause is **"forgive."** You can only forgive what you've already recognized as wrong. If wrongdoing didn't matter, forgiveness would be pointless, and you'd just shrug. The command assumes a moral order in which real harm occurs, and then asks for a particular response to it.

The surrounding passage also keeps evaluating:

- **6:39-42:** Remove the log from your own eye *so that you can see clearly* to remove the speck from your brother's. The goal is still to deal with the speck. The problem is hypocrisy and blindness, not seeing.
- **6:43-45:** "Each tree is known by its fruit." That is an instruction to assess character by what it produces.

Elsewhere, Jesus says, "Do not judge by appearances, but judge with right judgment" (John 7:24), and warns against "false prophets" (Matt. 7:15), which requires recognizing them. So the text distinguishes between kinds of judging.

## The word matters: condemnation

Luke escalates: *judge not → condemn not → forgive.* The Greek *katadikazō* means to pronounce a sentence against someone, a final verdict on their standing. The concern is less about noticing that something is wrong than about taking up the role of judge over a person's whole worth, as though you had the full file and the authority to close the case.

## What discernment does

Discernment:

- **Perceives accurately.** It names harm as harm and lies as lies.
- **Stays proportionate.** It sees the act, the pattern, and the context, and it knows the limits of what it knows.
- **Acts prudently.** It sets boundaries, withholds trust where it hasn't been earned, and protects the vulnerable.
- **Aims at repair.** It is open to repentance, growth, and new information.
- **Includes self-scrutiny.** It applies the same standard to oneself.

## What the appetite to condemn does

Condemnation is driven less by concern for truth than by hunger. It often:

- **Feels good.** Outrage can be pleasurable. It gives a sense of superiority, belonging, and innocence by contrast.
- **Presumes to read the heart.** It moves from "that was cruel" to "he is a cruel person, essentially and forever."
- **Wants exclusion or punishment** more than repair, and may be disappointed by repentance.
- **Exempts the judge.** The log stays in its own eye.
- **Closes the case.** It resists new evidence because the verdict is emotionally useful.

## Some diagnostic questions

1. *Would I be glad or disappointed if this person turned out to be less guilty than I thought?*
2. *Would I welcome their repentance, or does it threaten the satisfaction I'm getting?*
3. *Am I holding myself to this standard, or do I have an exemption?*
4. *Do I want the wrong to stop, or do I want the person diminished?*
5. *Do I know the whole story, and do I want to?*

## Practical cases

- **A friend is embezzling.** Discernment says it's wrong, confronts it, and may end the trust or report it. Condemnation says "he's a thief, he's done to me." The first can coexist with compassion, and the second closes the door.
- **An abusive relative.** Forgiveness doesn't require returning to danger. Luke 17:3 pairs the two: "If your brother sins, rebuke him, and if he repents, forgive him." Forgiveness is release from vengeance and contempt, not amnesia or naive reconciliation.
- **A judge or juror.** Some roles require formal judgment. Even there, the ideal is justice without relish and verdicts without contempt.

## The two opposite failures

- **Condemnation:** treating judgment as a weapon and a pleasure.
- **Indifference:** treating "don't judge" as a shield against any accountability. That slogan is itself a moral judgment, often made against those who name wrongs. A verse about mercy becomes a tool for avoiding truth.

## The reciprocity clause

"You shall not be judged... you shall be forgiven" points to a humility about our position. We all live by mercy. Those who condemn fiercely tend to have forgotten how much they rely on it. Taking wrongs seriously, including our own, is what makes mercy meaningful rather than sentimental.

**In short:** moral judgment tells the truth about what happened. Condemnation claims the authority and takes the pleasure of a final verdict on a person. The passage asks us to keep the first and give up the second.
