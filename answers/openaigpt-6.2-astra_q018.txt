Bitterness and forgiveness are set against each other because they are rival ways of carrying a wrong forward. Bitterness lets the injury continue to govern how I see and speak to the person who hurt me; forgiveness refuses to let that injury have the final say. Paul’s contrast is not between *feeling hurt* and *feeling fine*. It is between what resentment and grace each do to life together.

Bitterness can be felt before it is chosen. But “let all bitterness … be put away from you” addresses what people *do* with it: whether they nurse a grievance, rehearse it in speech, or let it settle into ill will. The list in verse 31 does not have to be a sequence, as if bitterness always leads to wrath and then to shouting. But placing bitterness alongside “clamour” and “evil speaking” makes its social reach visible. A hurt that begins with one person can become a way a whole group talks about someone. In that sense, yes, bitterness can be contagious.

The positive commands are likewise communal: “kind **one to another** … forgiving **one another**.” And the reason given is not that the wrong was small or the hurt should already have passed. It is that “God for Christ’s sake hath forgiven you.” For Paul, forgiveness begins with grace already received. One can therefore put away malice while the wound still aches: the point is not to deny the wrong, but to refuse to make it the measure of how one treats another.
