The problem in Matthew 6:1 is **not visibility itself**, but the **craving to be seen**. The phrase “to be seen of them” gives the motive: Jesus is not forbidding all public good, but good done *for the sake of being noticed, admired, and praised*.

So public goodness becomes morally dangerous when it turns a good deed into a performance. The neighbor becomes an audience, God becomes a pretext, and the self becomes the real center. The person is no longer giving alms out of love for God and neighbor; they are trading goodness for human approval. That is why Jesus says they “have no reward of your Father which is in heaven”—their reward is the human praise they sought. As Jesus says elsewhere, “they have received their reward” (Matt. 6:2).

This does not mean public goodness is inherently wrong. In Matthew 5:16 Jesus says, “Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father which is in heaven.” The difference is direction: in 5:16, visibility leads others to glorify **God**; in 6:1, visibility is sought so that **I** am glorified.

So visibility can be an occasion of danger because it feeds vanity, comparison, hypocrisy, and self-worship. But the sin is not being seen; it is **doing good in order to be seen**. Public goodness is faithful when it is ordered toward love, witness, and God’s glory. It is dangerous when it is ordered toward applause.
