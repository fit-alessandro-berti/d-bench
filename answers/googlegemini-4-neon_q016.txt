All five of these are present in the passage, but they do not sit at the same depth. They form layers, moving from surface symptoms down to a single, tragic root.

If you look at the father’s gentle diagnosis in verse 31—*“Son, thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine”*—the elder brother’s deepest problem turns out to be **relational alienation disguised as obedience**: he relates to his father not as a beloved son, but as a resentful slave operating on a ledger of merit.

Here is how the five elements you named peel back to reveal that core issue:

### 1. The Surface Symptoms: Anger and Envy
Anger (*“he was angry, and would not go in”*) and envy (*“thou never gavest me a kid... thou hast killed for him the fatted calf”*) are his immediate emotional reactions. They show what the celebration provokes in him, but they do not yet explain *why* his brother’s survival feels like a personal injury.

### 2. The Moral Framework: Wounded Fairness and Pride in Obedience
Beneath the anger is a rigid moral economy. He says, *“Lo, these many years do I serve thee, neither transgressed I at any time thy commandment.”* 
* **Pride in obedience:** He treats his dutifulness as moral leverage—an unblemished record that puts the father in his debt.
* **Wounded fairness:** If the household runs on wages and desserts, then throwing a feast for a son who wasted his inheritance is genuinely unjust. In a strictly transactional world, the elder brother has a point.

### 3. The Theological Crisis: Discomfort with Mercy Itself
In the broader context of Luke 15, Jesus tells this parable directly to the Pharisees and scribes who were grumbling, *“This man receiveth sinners, and eateth with them”* (Luke 15:2). For someone whose identity is built on being the "good one," unmerited mercy is deeply threatening. If the father gives the robe, the ring, and the fatted calf to someone who did nothing to deserve them, then the elder brother’s years of rule-keeping no longer make him superior or secure. Mercy doesn't just feel unfair to him; it dismantles the entire system he used to measure his worth.

### 4. The Deepest Root: Living Like a Slave in the Father’s House
Look closely at three details in his outburst that expose what lies even beneath his discomfort with mercy:

* **He sees sonship as slavery.** The Greek verb translated *“serve”* (*douleuō*) literally means *to slave for*. He does not say, “I have loved you” or “I have stayed with you.” He says, *“All these years I have slaved for you.”* 
* **He wants the father’s things, not the father himself.** Notice what he actually craves: *“thou never gavest me a kid, that I might make merry with my friends.”* Shockingly, his heart is identical to his younger brother’s. The younger brother wanted the father’s wealth so he could go enjoy himself away from the father; the elder brother stayed home, obeyed the rules, and secretly resented never getting a goat so he could go party with his own friends—also without the father. 
* **He has lost the family heart.** He refuses to call the younger man his brother, referring to him coldly as *“this thy son.”* Because he does not know the father’s love, he cannot share the father’s grief over what was lost or the father’s joy over what was found.

### The Verdict
Among the five options, **discomfort with mercy** and **pride in obedience** come closest to the center—because both stem from the same fatal blindness. 

The elder brother’s deepest problem is that **he believes love must be earned.** Because he has reduced his relationship with his father to a contract, he has spent years living as an orphan inside his own home—completely blind to the truth the father finally speaks over him: *“You are always with me, and everything I have is already yours.”* 

The parable ends with the elder brother still standing outside in the dark while the father pleads with him—revealing that a person can be just as lost through dutiful self-righteousness as through reckless rebellion.
