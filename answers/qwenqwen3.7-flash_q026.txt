This passage connects speech and inner morality through a **metaphor of accumulation and overflow**. Jesus portrays the heart as a moral treasury (`thesauros` in Greek), where virtue or vice is stored over time. When that inner store reaches its `abundance` (`plērōma`, fullness), it naturally spills out through the mouth. Speech, therefore, is not treated as an isolated verbal act but as the **inevitable external projection of a fully saturated internal reality**. You cannot draw good water from a poisoned well, nor can you consistently produce godly speech from a corrupt moral reservoir.

### On Whether Words Are Symptoms, Choices, or Revelations:
The text most strongly frames words as **symptoms** and **revelations**, with **choice present but displaced upstream**.

1. **Symptoms** ✅  
   Jesus treats consistent speech as diagnostic evidence. Just as a fever reveals infection, a pattern of words reveals the heart’s true condition. The rhetorical question (“how can ye, being evil, speak good things?”) assumes that good speech would be impossible to sustain from an evil moral foundation. Words function as vital signs of spiritual health or decay.

2. **Revelations** ✅  
   The passage emphasizes disclosure. The heart’s hidden moral inventory becomes visible through speech. Words don’t manufacture truth; they uncover what is already there. In Jewish wisdom tradition (cf. Prov 4:23; 10:11; Sirach 28:11), talk is consistently portrayed as making inner character public.

3. **Choices** ⚠️ (implicit, not primary)  
   This passage does not frame individual utterances as moment-to-moral decisions. Instead, it highlights **involuntary overflow**. That said, it doesn’t erase human agency; it relocates it. The “treasure” itself is cultivated over time through repeated allegiances, habits, and responses to God. What you choose to feed becomes what you eventually emit. Choice matters in the *formation* of the heart, not necessarily in the *flow* of speech once it runs.

### Synthesis
The passage presents words chiefly as **symptoms and revelations** of a pre-existing moral reality. It’s less about self-control in the moment and more about soul-orientation over time. Jesus’ point is pastoral and convictive: if your speech consistently contradicts your claimed faith, stop policing your tongue and start examining your heart. The remedy isn’t mere behavioral discipline; it’s replenishing the treasure with what is true, noble, and aligned with God’s character.

This fits squarely within biblical anthropology: humans don’t speak from empty vessels, but from what they love, trust, and store up. Speech, in this view, is the audible footprint of the soul’s direction.
