The verse itself answers the question, and it does so with remarkable precision in the Greek. The key phrase is πρὸς τὸ θεαθῆναι αὐτοῖς — "with a view to being seen by them." The construction is *telic*: it describes purpose, not mere circumstance. Jesus does not condemn the fact that alms happen "before men" (ἔμπροσθεν τῶν ἀνθρώπων); he condemns the aim that the seeing should occur. Visibility is the occasion; the craving is the disease.

**The reward logic**

The moral danger is located in the economics of the act: "otherwise ye have no reward of your Father." In the verses that follow (6:2, 5, 16), Jesus sharpens this with the commercial term ἀπέχω — the hypocrites have "received in full" their payment. The picture is of a closed transaction. If the applause of an audience was the price you sought, the applause settles the account. Nothing further is owed. The act is not evil because it was seen; it is *exhausted* by being seen. Its entire spiritual value was converted into social currency at the moment of performance.

**The audience substitution**

This is the deeper corruption. Public goodness done for display replaces God with the crowd as the judge before whom one lives. The needy recipient becomes a prop; the act of charity becomes a stage. What looks like love for neighbor is covertly love of self mediated through the neighbor's admiration. This is why Jesus calls such people "hypocrites" (ὑποκριταί) — literally stage actors. The problem is not that the mask is visible to others, but that the actor has begun to live inside it.

**The tension with Matthew 5:16**

Any reading that condemns visibility itself collapses against Jesus' own command one chapter earlier: "Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father which is in heaven." Goodness *will* be seen, and should be. The difference between 5:16 and 6:1 is entirely one of trajectory — where the glory lands. In 5:16, visibility deflects attention upward to God. In 6:1, visibility is engineered to terminate on the self.

**Why "take heed"?**

The opening warning (προσέχετε) acknowledges that motive is the one thing a person cannot easily audit. Public goodness is dangerous not because eyes are watching, but because being watched is *pleasant*, and pleasure quietly rewrites intention. One can begin with a genuine desire to help and, through repeated exposure to admiration, end as a performer who helps only incidentally. The verse is a warning against that slow, largely unconscious migration.

**Conclusion**

The problem is not visibility but appetite — the craving to be seen, which converts an act meant for God and neighbor into an act of self-worship. Visibility is morally neutral; it becomes dangerous only because it is the medium through which the craving feeds itself.
