# The Mote and the Beam (Matthew 7:3–5)

## Why the Image Is So Sharp

### The absurd disproportion

The entire force of the metaphor rests on a single visual gag: a tiny splinter (*karphos*) lodged in someone else's eye versus a massive timber (*dokos*) sticking out of one's own. A *karphos* was a shaving or chip of wood — nearly invisible. A *dokos* was a structural beam, a heavy rafter. The mental picture is deliberately, almost comically absurd: a man with a log bolted through his own eye socket peering at his neighbor and saying, *"Hold still, let me get that speck out."* The comedy lands instantly because the image requires no explanation.

### Sight language compounds the irony

Notice how thoroughly the passage is built around **seeing**:

- "Why **beholdest** thou" — looking, examining
- "**considerest** not" — perceiving
- "**see** clearly"

The person who most needs to see (the one with the beam) is precisely the one who is most blinded. The beam obscures his own vision, yet he presumes to have a clear enough vantage point to diagnose a problem in someone else's eye. The passage is self-refuting from the outside: you cannot both be blinded and be a competent diagnostician of another's vision. The image thus becomes a visual argument against the very person who makes it.

### The turn to direct address

Jesus begins in third person ("thy brother's eye... thine own eye") and then shifts abruptly to direct accusation: **"Thou hypocrite."** The reader is no longer watching a scene; he is caught inside it. The mode changes from illustration to indictment in a single word.

### The enforced sequence

The word **"first"** (πρῶτον, *prōton*) is the hinge of the entire passage. It establishes an order that is logically binding: self-correction must precede correction of others. The problem isn't seeing the mote in your brother's eye — it's that you see it *while ignoring the beam in your own*.

## How the Passage Criticizes Hypocrisy Without Forbidding Moral Correction

This is the crucial point. The passage could easily be misread as a blanket prohibition against correcting others. But it says the opposite in its final clause:

> *"Then shalt thou **see clearly to cast out** the mote out of thy brother's eye."*

The removal of the mote from the brother's eye is not condemned — it is **the stated goal**. What is condemned is the *condition* and *spirit* in which the correction is attempted.

### 1. The word "first" implies "then"

The structure is sequential, not prohibitive:

| Step | Action |
|------|--------|
| **First** | Remove the beam from your own eye (self-examination, repentance) |
| **Then** | See clearly to remove the mote from your brother's eye (correction) |

If Jesus intended to forbid moral correction entirely, he would have stopped at verse 5a. Instead, he carries the sentence through to its positive conclusion. The passage *assumes* that after proper self-work, the person will go ahead and help his brother.

### 2. The target is the *hypocrite*, not the *corrector*

The word "hypocrite" (ὑποκριτής) names a specific failure: one who applies a standard to others while exempting himself. The criticism is not "don't judge" but "don't judge *hypocritically*." The beam is not merely a different sin — it is the sin of **self-deception and selective blindness**, the refusal to apply the standard inwardly before applying it outwardly.

### 3. The passage sets a *condition*, not a *ban*

The logic is straightforward:

- Genuine correction requires **clear vision**.
- Clear vision requires **the absence of self-deception** (the beam).
- Therefore, self-examination and repentance are **prerequisites** for moral correction, not replacements for it.

This is actually a *higher* standard for correction, not a lower one. It demands that the corrector be in a position of real moral authority — earned through honest self-work — rather than merely occupying a position of apparent authority.

### 4. Jesus elsewhere actively commands moral correction

Matthew 18:15–17 lays out a detailed procedure for confronting a brother who sins: go to him privately, bring witnesses if necessary, escalate to the church. This could not coexist with a blanket prohibition on correction. Similarly, Matthew 7:6 warns not to "give that which is holy unto the dogs" — an instruction that *requires* the capacity for moral discernment and judgment.

---

## The Passage in Summary

The *mote and beam* is not anti-correction. It is **anti-hypocrisy** — specifically, the hypocrisy of correcting others while refusing to undergo the same correction oneself. The image is sharp because it makes that refusal *physically visible*: the log through the eye is so obvious that anyone watching would laugh, yet the man with the log is the only one in the scene who fails to see it. Jesus is not saying "never judge." He is saying: **judge only after you have allowed yourself to be judged first.**
