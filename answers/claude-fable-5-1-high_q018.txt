Ephesians 4:31–32 — Bitterness and Forgiveness

1. Where the two verses sit

Verses 31–32 are the last of a series of paired commands that begins at 4:25. Having said in 4:22–24 that believers have "put off" the old man and "put on" the new, Paul works that out in concrete opposites:

- lying / speaking truth (v. 25)
- anger nursed overnight / anger that does not give the devil a foothold (vv. 26–27)
- stealing / working so as to have something to give (v. 28)
- corrupt talk / talk that builds up and "gives grace" to the hearer (v. 29)

Then v. 30 (do not grieve the Holy Spirit), and then vv. 31–32 as the climax: a six-fold catalogue of what must go, answered by a three-fold description of what replaces it, grounded in what God has done in Christ. So bitterness and forgiveness are set against each other because the whole passage is built on the put-off / put-on pattern. Every vice in this section is paired with the specific virtue that occupies the same space in a person's life. Truth occupies the place lying held; generosity occupies the place theft held; and forgiveness occupies the place bitterness held. The pairing is not rhetorical decoration; it says that bitterness and forgiveness are the same situation handled two opposite ways.

That situation is having been wronged. Nobody is bitter in the abstract; bitterness is always about something someone did. And forgiveness is only meaningful where there is something real to forgive. Both are responses to injury. One keeps the injury alive and lets it work; the other releases it. This is why Paul does not oppose bitterness to "cheerfulness" or "contentment" — that would treat it as a mood. He opposes it to forgiveness because bitterness is unresolved grievance, and the only thing that actually resolves a grievance is forgiving it.

2. The vocabulary of the vice list

The six terms are worth looking at one by one, because their order tells you how Paul understands bitterness.

- pikria ("bitterness"): in ordinary Greek, the sharp taste of gall; in moral usage, a sour, resentful temper. Aristotle (Nicomachean Ethics IV.5) describes the pikroi as people who are "hard to reconcile" and hold their anger a long time, because they keep it down inside rather than letting it out. In the Septuagint and the New Testament the word is tied to the image of a poisonous root: Deuteronomy 29:18 ("a root that beareth gall and wormwood"), picked up in Hebrews 12:15 ("lest any root of bitterness springing up trouble you, and thereby many be defiled"). See also Acts 8:23 ("the gall of bitterness"), Romans 3:14 ("cursing and bitterness"), James 3:14 ("bitter envying").
- thymos ("wrath"): the hot, sudden flare of anger, the outburst.
- orgē ("anger"): the more settled, deliberate anger — the anger that stays.
- kraugē ("clamour"): shouting, the raised voice, quarrelling that others can hear.
- blasphēmia ("evil speaking"): slander, injurious talk about people.
- kakia ("malice"): ill will as such; the phrase "with all malice" (syn pasē kakia) is a summarizing sweep — every form of wanting harm for another.

Read in sequence, this is not a random heap of bad qualities. It moves from the inside outward. Bitterness is the inward, hidden thing; wrath and anger are its emotional temperature; clamour and slander are what happens when it reaches the mouth and other people; malice names the disposition that runs under all of it. Bitterness stands first because it is the spring. Most commentators, ancient and modern, have read the list this way: what begins as a soured heart ends as a poisoned community.

So to the question of what kind of thing bitterness is, the list itself gives a layered answer.

3. Feeling, habit, posture, or contagion?

Feeling. Bitterness certainly has a feeling component, and Paul does not deny it. Note that he does not forbid anger as such: v. 26 says "be ye angry, and sin not." What he forbids is letting the sun go down on it — keeping it. Bitterness is anger that has been kept. It is what the feeling of being wronged turns into when it is stored rather than dealt with. In that sense it starts as a feeling but does not stay one.

Habit. The Greek moral tradition Paul's readers would have known treated pikria as a settled character trait, not a passing emotion — the trait of the person who cannot be reconciled. Paul's grammar points the same way. The imperative "be put away" (arthētō) is aorist, a decisive act, matching the "putting off" of a garment in vv. 22 and 25. You do not tell someone to take off a feeling; you tell them to take off something that has become part of how they dress themselves, a standing way of relating to others.

Chosen posture. This is the level at which the command actually lands. A feeling cannot be commanded, but the refusal to release a grievance can be. Bitterness, in this passage, is the decision (often not consciously made, but a decision nonetheless) to keep holding what someone owes you. That is why its exact opposite is forgiveness. The verb Paul uses for "forgiving" is charizomai — literally to show grace, to give freely, to hand over as a gift. It is the same root as charis, "grace," which he has just used in v. 29 (speech that "gives grace" to the hearer), and the same verb he uses for God's act at the end of v. 32. Forgiveness here is not primarily a feeling of warmth toward the offender; it is an act of release, the cancelling of a claim. Bitterness is the standing refusal to cancel it. One keeps the ledger open; the other closes it.

Contagion. Paul clearly also treats bitterness as something that spreads, and the passage gives three signals of this.

First, the list itself: bitterness becomes clamour and slander, which are by definition social. What was private becomes audible, then becomes gossip and character-damage. The poison travels through speech, which is why the whole section (vv. 25, 29, 31) is so preoccupied with the mouth.

Second, v. 27: anger held overnight "gives place to the devil" — gives him a topos, a foothold, a piece of ground. Retained grievance is presented as an opening through which something hostile to the community gets in. Bitterness is not a closed system inside one person.

Third, v. 30: this behaviour "grieves the Holy Spirit," the Spirit who in this letter is the bond of the body's unity (4:3–4) and its seal (1:13). The larger concern of Ephesians is one new humanity, Jew and Gentile made one body in Christ (2:14–16; 4:4–6, 15–16). Bitterness is a threat to exactly that. Hebrews 12:15 makes the contagion explicit with the same word: a root of bitterness by which "many be defiled." One resentful person can sour a whole fellowship.

So the honest answer is: all four, arranged as a progression. Bitterness begins as a feeling (the natural response to injury), sets into a habit (a sour, unreconcilable temper), is maintained by a posture (the refusal to release the debt), and once it exists it does not stay where it started — it spreads, through the mouth, into the community. Paul addresses it at the level of posture because that is where responsibility lies, and he treats the feeling and the contagion as the upstream and downstream of that posture.

4. Why forgiveness, specifically, is the remedy

Three features of v. 32 explain why forgiveness, not mere self-control, is the answer to bitterness.

"Kind ... tenderhearted." Paul does not ask for the suppression of feeling but its replacement. Eusplanchnos ("tenderhearted") is a visceral word — from splanchna, the inward parts. It is the gut moved with compassion. The bitter person also feels things in the gut; Paul wants the same organ set to a different response. This is not Stoic apatheia; it is a re-tuned affection.

"One another." The reciprocal pronoun (heautois, "to yourselves," i.e., among yourselves) matches the communal scope of the vice list. If bitterness is contagious, forgiveness is the counter-contagion. A community that practices mutual release is one in which grievances cannot take root, because they are constantly being handed over rather than stored.

"Even as God for Christ's sake hath forgiven you." The Greek is kathōs kai ho theos en Christō echarisato hymin — "just as God in Christ graciously forgave you." The comparison does double work. It gives the measure: you forgive as freely as you were forgiven. And it removes the bitter person's implicit claim. Bitterness always carries a conviction that this particular grievance is uniquely justified, that the other person really does owe me. Paul answers by pointing to the reader's own position: you were once "children of wrath" (2:3), and God did not keep the ledger open. To hold a debt against a brother while living on a cancelled debt is the posture Jesus condemns in the unforgiving servant (Matthew 18:23–35), and Paul is making the same point in miniature. The gospel takes away the ground on which bitterness stands.

The passage then flows straight into 5:1–2: "Be ye therefore followers of God, as dear children; and walk in love, as Christ also hath loved us, and hath given himself for us." Forgiveness is the family resemblance. Bitterness, by contrast, is a way of being unlike God while claiming to be his child.

5. Summary

Bitterness and forgiveness are set directly against each other because they are the two possible outcomes of the same event — being wronged — and because the whole passage pairs each vice with the virtue that displaces it. Paul treats bitterness as a feeling that has hardened into a habit, maintained by the posture of refusing to release a grievance, and inherently contagious: it works outward from the heart through speech into the body, gives hostile powers a foothold, and grieves the Spirit who holds the body together. He commands its removal as a decisive act because at the level of posture it is something one can actually do, and he names forgiveness as its replacement because forgiveness is the one act that dissolves a grievance rather than storing it. The standard and the motive are the same: God in Christ has already done to you what you are now told to do to one another.
