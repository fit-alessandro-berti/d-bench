The passage rejects worship outright because the problem is not one of proportion. "Better balance" would imply that worship and justice are two separate goods and that Israel has too much of one and too little of the other. That would leave the worship valid in itself, as a kind of credit on the ledger. Amos denies the credit.

## Why the rejection is total

**The worship is part of the injustice.** Elsewhere Amos describes the worshipers as people who "trample the poor," take bribes, and "turn aside the needy in the gate" (5:11–12), the gate being the local court. The "fat beasts" on the altar come from wealth extracted that way. The offerings are the proceeds of the wrong, presented to God as a gift.

**The worship insulates the worshipers.** Lavish festivals leave people feeling that they are right with God. That feeling is what lets the exploitation go on without a troubled conscience. In this sense the worship is worse than nothing, because it works as an anesthetic.

**It misrepresents God.** These are people who bribe judges, and they are approaching God the same way, as a patron whose favor can be secured with gifts regardless of how they treat others. But for Amos, justice is God's own character. To worship him while crushing the poor is to worship a different god under his name. Accepting it would make God an accomplice.

The language reflects this. God shuts down sense by sense: he will not smell, look, or listen. The possessives matter too: *your* feast days, *your* songs. The worship has become theirs, a self-referential performance.

## The fraud being exposed

The fraud is the use of religion as a substitute for covenant loyalty rather than an expression of it. It is a kind of moral laundering. The worshipers assume that their standing with God can be kept up in the sanctuary, apart from what happens in the marketplace and the court. Their devotion may even be sincerely felt, which is what makes it so dangerous. Just before this passage (5:18–20), they are eagerly awaiting the "day of the Lord," sure it will vindicate them. Amos says it will be darkness, not light.

## The closing image

"Judgment" here is *mishpat*, justice, especially fair dealing in the courts. The "mighty stream" is a perennial stream, as opposed to a wadi that floods in the rainy season and then runs dry. The contrast is with the festivals, which are occasional and seasonal. What God wants is constant, like water that never stops flowing.

Most interpreters do not read Amos as abolishing ritual in principle, and the same critique appears in Isaiah 1, Micah 6, and Psalm 50. But the sharpness is deliberate. A call for balance would have let the hearers keep their main assumption, that the worship counted for something on its own.
