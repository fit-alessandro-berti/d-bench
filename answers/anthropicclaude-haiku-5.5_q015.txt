**Why the image is so sharp**

The image works because it is absurd. A speck of dust in one eye and a rafter in the other is a comic picture, and the audience is meant to laugh before they feel the rebuke. Several features sharpen it:

- **Exaggerated scale.** The Greek words are *karphos* (a dry chip or splinter) and *dokos* (a beam or timber). The contrast is between the smallest possible irritant and a structural log. Hypocrisy, the passage suggests, is not a minor misjudgment. It is a massive blindness that the person cannot even perceive in himself.
- **Concrete, domestic detail.** Jesus draws on the world of workshops and building, where wood and dust were everyday realities. The image is physical and vivid, not abstract, so it sticks.
- **Rhetorical questions that corner the listener.** "Why beholdest thou...?" and "how wilt thou say...?" make the hearer answer to himself. He is led to see the inconsistency from the inside.
- **Direct address.** "Thou hypocrite" drops the third person and speaks to the reader. The word *hypokrites* originally meant an actor, someone playing a part, which fits a person who performs moral seriousness while ignoring his own faults.
- **Ironic self-image.** The hypocrite imagines he is the helper, offering to help his brother, while he is the one who most needs help. The reversal is the sting.

**How it criticizes hypocrisy without forbidding correction**

The passage does not say "never correct anyone." Its own wording assumes correction is good and sometimes necessary:

1. **The final clause grants the permission.** "Then shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote out of thy brother's eye." The goal of the whole passage is clear sight, and removing the mote is the desired outcome. Jesus only specifies the order in which it should happen.

2. **The criticism targets order and motive, not the act.** The problem is the hypocrite who attempts the correction while blind to his own far larger fault. "First" is the key word. Self-examination comes first, not as a replacement for helping others but as a prerequisite for helping well.

3. **Blindness undermines the judgment.** A man with a beam in his eye cannot see accurately, so his correction will be distorted, harsh, or simply wrong. The point is partly practical: moral vision is compromised by unexamined sin. Removing the beam is what makes accurate judgment possible.

4. **The surrounding text supports discernment.** Verses 1–2 warn against judging by a standard you would not accept for yourself ("with what judgment ye judge, ye shall be judged"). Yet verse 6 expects discernment about what is holy and what is not. Christian ethics elsewhere calls for correction within the community (Matthew 18:15) and for restoring a fallen brother "in the spirit of meekness, considering thyself, lest thou also be tempted" (Galatians 6:1). Those passages reinforce the same pattern: correct others, but with humility and self-scrutiny.

**In short:** the image is sharp because it makes the hypocrite look ridiculous, and the passage is restrained because it aims at the sequence and the spirit of correction rather than at correction itself. Examine yourself first, then help your brother, because only then will you see clearly enough to do it well.
