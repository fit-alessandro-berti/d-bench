1 John 4:20 treats love for the visible neighbor as a test of love for the invisible God because the verse is not primarily making a psychological point about “warm feelings,” but a moral and spiritual one about the nature of genuine love.

The verse says that if someone claims, “I love God,” while hating his brother, he is a liar. The logic is sharp: love for God, who is unseen, must show itself in love toward people who are seen. If it does not, the claim is false.

## 1. The visible neighbor is the test because love must become concrete

Love for God can easily become abstract, emotional, or purely verbal. A person may claim to love God because of religious feelings, worship experiences, doctrinal knowledge, or spiritual enthusiasm. But those things can be misleading. They can exist without producing holiness, humility, mercy, or obedience.

The visible neighbor, however, provides a concrete test. You cannot love an unseen God in a way that remains entirely invisible. If God’s love is truly at work in a person, it will show in how that person treats real human beings: family members, fellow believers, neighbors, the poor, the irritating, the difficult, the inconvenient.

John is saying, in effect:

> “Do not tell me only about your feelings toward God. Show me how you treat the people God has placed in your life.”

Love for God is not merely a private inner experience. It must become visible in action.

## 2. The visible neighbor bears God’s image and is loved by God

The verse also works because the “brother” is not a neutral object. The person we are called to love is made in God’s image, known by God, and loved by God. In the broader context of 1 John, “brother” especially refers to fellow believers, but the principle extends to all people as image-bearers of God.

To hate a brother or sister is not merely to have a bad attitude toward another person. It is to reject someone who belongs to God’s moral order, someone whom Christ loved, someone for whom Christ died.

So the test is not arbitrary. God has connected love for Himself with love for those made in His image. Jesus Himself joined the two commandments:

> “You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart… and your neighbor as yourself.”  
> — Matthew 22:37–39

The love of God and the love of neighbor cannot be ultimately separated.

## 3. The verse exposes the self-deception of “spiritual” love without moral fruit

The self-deception exposed here is the belief that one can have a right relationship with God while living in hatred, bitterness, contempt, or lovelessness toward others.

This deception can take several forms.

### a. Mistaking religious feeling for love of God

A person may feel moved in worship, may enjoy spiritual language, may have intense religious experiences, and then assume, “I love God.” But John says that if those experiences do not produce love toward others, they are not proof of genuine love for God.

Love for God is not merely an emotion. It is a disposition of the heart that obeys God and reflects His character.

### b. Loving an idea of God rather than the true God

Someone may claim to love “God,” but the god they love may be a projection of their own preferences: a god who affirms them, defends them, approves their anger, and does not require them to love people they dislike.

But the true God is the God who says, “Love one another.” The true God is love. Therefore, if a person claims to love God but hates his brother, he is probably not loving the true God. He is loving a false image of God that he has made for himself.

### c. Compartmentalizing religion and relationships

The verse exposes the lie that says:

> “My relationship with God is one thing; my relationship with people is another.”

John refuses to allow that separation. In Scripture, the vertical and horizontal dimensions of love belong together. A person cannot claim to be right with God while being cruel, hateful, or cold toward those whom God commands him to love.

This is not merely about outward civility. It concerns the heart. Hatred, contempt, resentment, malice, and refusal to forgive are incompatible with love for God.

### d. Claiming love while practicing hatred

The word “liar” is important. The person is not simply mistaken; he is making a claim that his life contradicts. He says with his mouth, “I love God,” but his hatred says otherwise.

This is self-deception because the person may sincerely believe his claim. He may not realize that his bitterness reveals his spiritual condition. He may hide behind orthodoxy, sincerity, or spiritual intensity. But John says the claim is empty if love is absent.

## 4. The verse is not saying we earn God’s love by loving people

It is important not to misunderstand the verse. John is not saying that love for neighbor earns God’s favor or replaces love for God. He is not teaching salvation by human kindness.

Rather, he is saying that love for neighbor is the evidence of love for God.

The relationship is like this:

- Love for God is the root.
- Love for others is the fruit.
- If there is no fruit, the root is dead or false.

This is similar to James’s argument that faith without works is dead. John is saying that love for God without love for people is dead. It is not genuine love.

## 5. The visible neighbor makes the invisible God visible

There is also a beautiful theological point here. No one has seen God in His full divine glory. But love among human beings makes God visible.

John says just before this:

> “No man hath seen God at any time. If we love one another, God dwelleth in us, and his love is perfected in us.”  
> — 1 John 4:12

God is made visible in the world through the love of His people. When believers love one another, they display the character of the unseen God. But when they hate one another, they distort His witness and deny His character.

So love for the visible neighbor is a test because it is the place where love for the invisible God becomes tangible.

## 6. The deeper deception: hating whom God loves

Perhaps the deepest self-deception exposed by this verse is this: a person thinks he can love God while hating someone God loves.

That is spiritually impossible. If someone belongs to God’s family, bears His image, or is the object of His mercy, then hatred toward that person is an offense against God Himself.

Jesus taught something similar when He identified Himself with the hungry, the thirsty, the stranger, the naked, the sick, and the imprisoned:

> “Inasmuch as ye have done it unto one of the least of these my brethren, ye have done it unto me.”  
> — Matthew 25:40

To love God is to love what God loves. To hate what God loves is to reveal that we do not truly know Him.

## In short

1 John 4:20 treats love for the visible neighbor as a test of love for the invisible God because love for God cannot remain purely private, emotional, or verbal. It must become real in concrete relationships.

The verse exposes the self-deception of claiming to love God while refusing to love those He has made and commands us to love. It warns against false spirituality: the illusion that one can be deeply religious and yet remain bitter, hateful, or unloving.

The verse teaches that genuine love for God is not proved merely by what we say about God, but by how we treat the people He places before us.
