## What makes the image so sharp

**1. The grotesque disproportion.** The Greek words make the contrast extreme: a *karphos* is a tiny chip of straw or dry twig — hardly visible — while a *dokos* is a load-bearing rafter, a construction timber. The comparison is deliberately absurd, almost slapstick: a man with a plank jutting from his face offering to perform delicate extraction of a speck from someone else's eye. The comedy is what makes it unforgettable — and what makes the hypocrite's self-blindness vivid. He isn't minimizing a small flaw of his own; he is oblivious to something enormous.

**2. The materials are the same.** Both objects are wood. Jesus does not say the brother's fault is trivial or imaginary — it is the *same kind* of fault, only comically smaller in the brother and massive in the critic. This is the piercing part. The sensitivity we feel toward another's fault is often the very sensitivity that registers our own; the speck we see is a speck of the same timber that blinds us. The image anticipates what psychology would later call projection.

**3. The organ is the eye.** The eye is the instrument of judgment and perception — and it is precisely the judge's organ that is diseased. The double irony: the man who cannot see offers to correct another's sight, and the very act of peering into his brother's eye is impossible with a beam in his own. The fault-finder's diagnostic instrument is the one thing he has never examined.

**4. The escalating rhetoric.** Verse 3 poses the question, verse 4 dramatizes the absurd scenario ("Let me pull out the mote..."), and verse 5 lands the direct strike: "Thou hypocrite." The Greek *hypokritēs* originally meant a stage actor — someone performing a role. The charge is not "you have faults" but "you are playing a part": performing the physician while being the greater patient.

## Why it criticizes hypocrisy without forbidding correction

**The final clause is the crux.** "And *then* shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote out of thy brother's eye." The removal of the brother's speck remains the goal. Far from forbidding moral correction, the passage *schedules* it: *first* the beam, *then* the speck. The problem is sequence and posture, not the activity itself.

Three features of the corrected corrector follow from this:

- **Clear vision as the precondition.** The Greek (*diablepseis* — to see distinctly) suggests that self-examination is not introspective navel-gazing but preparation. Only the person who has dealt with his own beam has the eyesight — and the steadiness, and the gentleness that delicate eye-work requires — to help someone else.

- **Humility as the precondition.** The one who has extracted his own beam knows what it is to have one. He corrects as a fellow patient who has been treated, not as a superior passing sentence. Compare Matthew 18:15, where correction is explicitly commanded — but as fraternal care ("go and tell him his fault between thee and him alone"), not condemnation.

- **The context confirms this reading.** Verses 1–2 ("judge not... for with what measure ye mete") condemn the *measure* — censorious, self-exempting judgment — not judgment itself. And the very next verse (7:6, "give not that which is holy unto the dogs") *requires* discernment. The whole passage assumes that moral discrimination is necessary; what it forbids is the double standard that scrutinizes the brother while exempting the self.

In short: the image is sharp because it is comic, symmetrical, and anatomically precise — same wood, same organ, absurd scale. And the teaching is precise too: not "never correct," but "never correct with a beam in your eye." The purified eye does not retire from judgment; it finally earns the right to perform it — carefully, humbly, and for the brother's sake rather than one's own performance.
