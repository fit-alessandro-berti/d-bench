Matthew 5:44–45 is one of the most subversive passages in the biblical tradition. It asks us to do precisely what every instinct, every political stratagem, and every evolutionary reflex warns us against: to feed, bless, and pray for the very people who are trying to hurt us. 

To understand why this call to "enemy-love" is so difficult to take seriously—and whether it is sentimental, strategic, or a deeper moral strength—we must first look at the justification the text provides: *"...for he maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good, and sendeth rain on the just and on the unjust."* 

Here is an breakdown of why this concept is so difficult to swallow, followed by an analysis of its true nature.

### Why Enemy-Love is Difficult to Take Seriously

**1. It defies evolutionary and psychological reflexes**
Humanity is hardwired for reciprocal altruism, but also for reciprocal hostility. If someone curses you, betrays you, or threatens your standing, your biological and psychological machinery immediately categorizes them as a threat. To love them—defined here as blessing, doing good to, and praying for them—requires the overriding of deep-seated survival algorithms. It feels difficult because it feels *dangerous*. 

**2. It sounds like a surrender of identity**
We define ourselves largely through opposition. Our identities are forged in the negative space of the "Other." To love an enemy feels like an erasure of the boundary between self and non-self, between protector and persecutor. It suggests a kind of moral dissolution—losing the self in the fog of universal goodwill.

**3. It is easily mistaken for masochism or naiveté**
In modern discourse, "love" is a word that has been drained of rigor. We associate love with soft affection, emotional permissiveness, and sappy positivity (think of the modern heart-emoji). When we hear "Love your enemies," it registers as sentimental mush—something a naive pacifist might say right before being stabbed in the back. We intuitively suspect that enemy-love is just a nice word for moral capitulation.

### Sentimental, Strategic, or Moral Strength?

Given the difficulty, how should we classify this Matthew 5 command? Is it merely sentimental? Is it just a clever strategy? Or is it something else entirely?

#### Not Sentimental (It is not "soft")
Although the word "love" triggers connotations of sentimentality in the modern mind, in the context of Matthew 5, love is not an emotion but an act of will. The text does not say "feel warm feelings for your enemies." It says "do good to them that hate you, and pray for them which... persecute you." Doing good to someone who curses you requires steel, not sentiment. It demands that you take resources—time, prayer, attention, charity—and invest them in a sink-hole of a relationship. Sentimentality would tell you to pity them or offer them sympathy from a safe distance. Matthew 5 commands involvement. It is an act of resistance against the logic of retaliation.

#### Not Merely Strategic (It is not utilitarian)
A cynic might say enemy-love is just a long-term strategy: "Enemies today may be allies tomorrow; love them now so they don't persecute you later." While there is a pragmatic truth to building bridges rather than burning them, Matthew 5 explicitly demands love even when there is no expected return. The quote accepts that the enemy may "despitefully use you, and persecute you"—implying ongoing, unrepentant hostility. To love them anyway is to act regardless of expected outcomes. If enemy-love were merely strategic, it would abandon the lover as soon as the enemy proved incorrigible. Matthew 5 implies you must love them *because* they are incorrigible. It is not a strategy for peace; it is a discipline for the soul.

#### A Deeper Form of Moral Strength
Matthew 5 reveals enemy-love as a profound moral strength, and the key lies in the passage's cosmic justification: the Father in heaven "maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good, and sendeth rain on the just and on the unjust."

This is a theology of impartiality. The Father (whether we read this as God, Nature, or Providence) does not sort the world into lovable charities worthy of sunlight and unworthy enemies to be left in the dark. The sun rises on the virtuous and the wicked alike. To be "the children of your Father which is in heaven" means to adopt the Father’s impartial generosity.

In this light, enemy-love is not a sign of weakness, but the highest test of moral maturity:

- **It requires refusing revenge.** The easiest moral act is to punish the wicked with wickedness—to curse those who curse you. Matthew 5 calls this the smallness of the human heart. To refuse revenge and replace it with charity is to assert one's independence from the logic of vendetta. 
- **It demands a non-arbitrary universe.** Enemy-love says, "Wickedness is not a property of the enemy; wickedness is a property of the act of persecuting." Therefore, doing good to those who persecute you is not validating their evil, but demonstrating that good is unconditional. It is good everywhere, in every direction, regardless of how it is received.
- **It is the moral parallel to Christ's impartial light.** Just as the sun refuses to withhold its light from the unjust, the human being is called to refuse the withholding of love from the persecutor. The moral strength is thus mimicry of the natural order: to treat the unworthy with the same grace as the worthy.

### Conclusion

Enemy-love is difficult to take seriously because it demands the suspension of the very instincts that preserve our ego and our safety. It sounds sentimental because modern language has softened the verb "love," but it is in fact an ascetic discipline. 

When Matthew 5 urges us to bless those who curse us, it is not urging feel-good charity but a radical assertion of sovereignty: *I will not let your persecution govern my heart.* The Father in heaven does not play politics with his sunlight, and so neither should we. To love the enemy is to hold the line of the good against the spreading shadow of the bad, proving that moral strength is measured not by how fiercely we strike back, but by how generously we shine forth.
