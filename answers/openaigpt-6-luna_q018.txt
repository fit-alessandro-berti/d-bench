Bitterness and forgiveness are set against each other because they describe two opposed ways of living with an injury. Bitterness keeps the injury active as a claim against another person; forgiveness responds from the grace one has received.

In verse 31, *bitterness* (“pikria”) appears in a cluster with wrath, anger, shouting, slander, and malice. It is therefore more than a passing feeling of hurt. It can begin in pain, but here it names a resentful disposition that can take shape in words and conduct. The command to “put away” the whole cluster treats it as something a community must not nurture—not as an emotion a person can simply switch off.

Verse 32 gives the contrasting practice: kindness, tenderheartedness, and forgiving. The Greek word translated “forgiving” is related to the word for grace: believers are to extend forgiveness as people who have themselves been forgiven by God in Christ. That does not mean pretending the harm did not happen, excusing it, or instantly restoring trust. It means refusing to make retaliation or the keeping of grievances the governing posture.

The passage also has a communal dimension. Bitterness can spread—not by contagion in a literal sense, but through hostile speech, repeated accusation, and malice, shaping how a whole group treats one another. So the contrast is not simply “feel bitter” versus “feel warm.” It is between a grievance that is cultivated into destructive conduct and a grace-shaped response that seeks the other’s good.
