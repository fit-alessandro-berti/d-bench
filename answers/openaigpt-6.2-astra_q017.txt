Light can be hated because it lets us see and makes us visible. In John, the light is Christ: his coming shows what human deeds are, whether or not the person who did them is ready to acknowledge them. The key phrase is “lest his deeds should be reproved.” What the person fears is not simply being noticed, but having to agree that what the light shows is true.

A person who has humiliated someone can keep calling it “just a joke” as long as the other person’s hurt stays out of view. To listen to that person would mean risking a cherished belief: *I’m not someone who does that.* Darkness need not mean ignorance, then. It can mean keeping just enough distance from the truth to go on as before. One can resent the light precisely because, at some level, one trusts what it would reveal.

Shame makes that resistance more understandable. There is a difference between *I did something cruel* and *if I am seen clearly, cruelty is all anyone will see in me*. The first judgment can lead to an apology; the second makes hiding feel like the only way to remain loved. Yet the passage follows John’s declaration that the Son came not to condemn the world but to save it (3:17). The light does not excuse the deed, but neither is its purpose to humiliate the person who did it.

The final verse therefore offers something other than a contrast between guilty people who hide and innocent people who have nothing to hide. It speaks of one who “doeth truth.” Coming into the light can itself be an act of truth: listening without making excuses, naming the harm, beginning to put it right. And even the good that comes to light is described as “wrought in God,” not displayed as proof of one’s own virtue. The freedom on offer is not the assurance that everything seen in us will be admirable. It is the freedom to stop making concealment a condition of being loved.
