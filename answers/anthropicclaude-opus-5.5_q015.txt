## Why the Image Is So Sharp

**1. It's absurd on purpose.** The Greek words are *karphos*, a speck, splinter, or bit of chaff, and *dokos*, a load-bearing roof beam. Picture a man with a timber jutting from his face leaning in to perform delicate surgery on someone else's eye. The exaggeration makes you laugh, and the laugh disarms you before you realize you're the target. Jesus, raised in a builder's household, reaches for workshop vocabulary: sawdust and lumber.

**2. The two faults are the same material.** Speck and beam are both wood. The difference is size, not kind. The hypocrite isn't condemning some sin foreign to himself; he's spotting a sliver of his own problem in someone else. That hints at projection: we are often most alert to faults that echo our own.

**3. The injury is to the eye itself.** The beam doesn't lodge in a hand or a foot but in the organ of seeing. The flaw damages the very faculty needed to judge. The hypocrite isn't just inconsistent; he's *blind*, and his confidence in his own perception is part of the problem.

**4. The verbs contrast.** "Beholdest" (*blepeis*) versus "considerest" (*katanoeis*): a glance at the brother, versus the careful, attentive examination one refuses to give oneself. We scrutinize others and skim ourselves.

**5. The escalation.** The passage moves from *seeing* (v. 3) to *speaking* ("Let me pull out the mote," v. 4) to *acting* (v. 5). The offer in verse 4 sounds kind, even helpful, which is exactly what makes it galling. Hypocrisy often wears the mask of concern.

**6. The word "hypocrite."** *Hypokritēs* meant a stage actor, someone playing a role. The man with the beam is performing the part of the physician without the qualification for it.

## How It Criticizes Hypocrisy Without Forbidding Correction

The key is the final clause: **"and *then* shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote out of thy brother's eye."**

- **The speck is real.** Jesus never says the brother's problem is imaginary or unimportant. The goal of removing it remains.
- **The command is about order, not abolition.** "*First* … *then*." Self-examination is the prerequisite for helping, not a replacement for it.
- **The aim is clarity.** "See clearly" implies that correction done well requires accurate vision, which only honest self-scrutiny provides.
- **The relationship is "brother."** The frame is family care, not courtroom condemnation. Removing something from an eye requires closeness, gentleness, and a steady hand, which is a fitting picture of how correction should feel.
- **Having had the beam removed changes you.** Someone who has endured painful self-correction approaches another's fault with humility and sympathy rather than superiority.

The surrounding context confirms this reading. Verse 1 ("Judge not") is qualified by verse 2 ("with what measure ye mete, it shall be measured to you again"), which warns against a standard you exempt yourself from rather than against all evaluation. Verse 6 ("give not that which is holy unto the dogs") immediately requires discernment. Elsewhere Jesus commands, "If thy brother shall trespass against thee, go and tell him his fault" (Matt. 18:15), and Paul echoes the mote-and-beam logic: "restore such an one in the spirit of meekness; considering thyself, lest thou also be tempted" (Gal. 6:1).

## In Short

The passage condemns not correction but *self-exempting* correction: judgment that is quick toward others, blind toward oneself, and performed as a role rather than offered as care. Its remedy is not silence but sequence. Look inward first, honestly and painfully, and you become someone who can actually help.
