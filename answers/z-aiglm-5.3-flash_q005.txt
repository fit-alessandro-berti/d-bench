The difficulty, I'd suggest, is not comprehension. "Love your enemies" is perfectly transparent. What's difficult is that we immediately translate it into things it is *not* — because the thing itself, taken at face value and addressed to *us*, is catastrophic to the normal grammar of moral life.

### How we refuse to take it seriously

There are four standard escape routes. **Sentimentalize** it into a warm feeling. **Spiritualize** it into an inner attitude with no behavioral entailments. **Defer** it as an "impossible ideal" — Reinhold Niebuhr's move, the Sermon as a standard meant to convict, not a program to follow. **Generalize** it into love for Humanity, which C.S. Lewis observed is always easier than love for the specific person who wronged you. All four routes share one feature: they remove the cost.

But notice what the verse actually commands. Four verbs: love, *bless* (speak well of), *do good*, *pray for*. Not one of them is a feeling. The Greek uses ongoing forms — keep loving, keep blessing, against those *currently* reviling you. Aquinas defined love as willing the good of the other; on that definition, you can obey this verse in a state of fury. Lewis put it bluntly: don't bother asking whether you love them; act as if you did, and watch what happens. The sentimental objection attacks a verse that doesn't exist. The verse that exists is far harder — a discipline performed against the feelings, trusting the feelings to follow.

### Why it is genuinely hard

Three reasons, I think.

**Reciprocity is our deepest moral default.** Human moral cognition runs on ledgers — fairness, desert, exchange. Enemy-love suspends the exchange. It feels like a category error, like praising someone for refusing to balance their books.

**Resentment is identity work.** The enemy explains who we are; the villain establishes the hero. James Baldwin suggested that people cling to hatred precisely because once it's gone, they must finally face the pain underneath. Loving your enemy means resigning from the office of judge and debt-collector — an office that pays a real psychological salary.

**The justice objection is serious and deserves a serious answer.** Doesn't enemy-love betray the victims? Here the text itself, read with Romans 12, offers a structure: it abolishes *personal* vengeance while not abolishing justice. Paul's commentary on this very passage is "avenge not yourselves... overcome evil with good" — vengeance is relocated to God, which is an act of trust, not leniency. The Amish of Nickel Mines, who attended their children's killer's funeral, carefully distinguished *forgiveness* (releasing the debt) from *pardon* (no consequences) and from *reconciliation* (restored relationship, which requires repentance). And love is not access: you can forgive the abuser and still testify against him. One honest caveat: when preached by the powerful to the powerless, this teaching has often functioned as social control. In the mouths of the wronged — King, ten Boom, the Amish — it has been defiance.

### Sentimental or strategic?

In *effect*, it is often strategic — the civil rights movement, the Truth and Reconciliation Commission, the sheer soft power of Nickel Mines. The prudential fruits are real; King's chiasm holds: darkness cannot drive out darkness; hate cannot drive out hate.

But it cannot be *strategic in foundation*, and this matters. Strategy is conditional. When enemy-love stops working — when it gets you beaten, imprisoned, killed with no visible results, as it sometimes has — the strategist abandons it. Worse, strategic love quietly begins to *use* the enemy, as an occasion for your virtue or your victory. You're back to reciprocity by other means.

The verse grounds the command elsewhere: "that ye may be children of your Father, *for he maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good*." The logic is resemblance, not results. God's own kindness is unconditioned — rain falls on the unjust. The command asks you to practice, in miniature, a generosity God practices in the large, with no guarantee of return. If it worked every time, it would be a technique. Part of its seriousness is that it must be willing to lose.

### The deeper strength — and its test

So: not sentiment, not strategy, but a distinct form of strength — and worth specifying. Not the strength of self-assertion but the strength of *non-reactivity*. The enemy's real power over you is his ability to set your inner agenda — to make you an avenger, which is to say, to make you into himself. Retaliation requires the enemy's cooperation. Enemy-love is the only response to evil that doesn't need the evildoer's consent to remain itself. It absorbs suffering rather than transmitting it — the rarest form of power there is, and the one Bonhoeffer had in mind when he distinguished cheap grace from the real thing.

Nietzsche deserves a fair hearing here: he read enemy-love as *ressentiment* — impotence dressed up as virtue. But his critique is a test, and it has a criterion: has the secret wish for the enemy's destruction actually gone? When Corrie ten Boom stood before a former Ravensbrück guard in 1947 and found she could not forgive him, and prayed instead, "Jesus, give me Your forgiveness for him" — when Amish parents mourned at their daughters' killer's grave — the ressentiment analysis fails. That is not the revenge of the weak. It is a strength the strong rarely have.

### Why it finally cannot be done alone

The deepest reason the command is hard may be reflexive: it presumes you have been on the receiving end of it. "That ye may be children of your Father" — children resemble the Father, and the Father's signature act, in this grammar, is loving *his* enemies: "while we were enemies, we were reconciled" (Rom 5:10). The rain on the unjust includes you. Taking enemy-love seriously means admitting your reserves are insufficient — and admitting that your own standing was that of an enemy who got the rain anyway.

So the triad resolves like this: in comfortable hands it decays into sentimentality; in clever hands it is refitted as strategy; in the hands of the wounded who refuse to hate, it is what the verse actually claims — the family resemblance of a Father whose generosity keeps no ledger.
