The power praised here is **kenotic, cruciform power**: the power of self-giving love. Christ is “in the form of God” and equal with God, yet he does not cling to that equality as a privilege to exploit. He empties himself, takes the form of a servant, enters human likeness, humbles himself, and becomes obedient to death—even the cross. So the passage is not praising weakness or the absence of power. It is praising **power that can renounce domination without ceasing to be great**—authority expressed as service, strength expressed as self-donation, majesty hidden in humility. Its climax is the cross: divine power revealed not as coercive control but as faithful love that bears, serves, and gives itself away.

Humility joined to greatness can feel more unsettling than open domination because open domination is at least easy to recognize. We know what to do with a tyrant: resist, flee, denounce. It leaves our moral categories intact. But a truly great person who humbles himself disrupts those categories. He cannot simply be opposed as an obvious oppressor; his humility disarms us, evokes admiration, and makes a claim on us. It suggests that greatness does not have to be predatory—and that can expose our own hunger for status, control, and recognition.

It is also unsettling because it works from the inside. Open domination uses external force; humble greatness works through conscience, gratitude, shame, admiration, and love. We may even suspect manipulation: power that hides itself in gentleness can feel more dangerous than power that shows itself openly. And with Christ, the unease is sharpest because the passage does not merely ask us to admire him; it says, “Let this mind be in you.” The servant Lord confronts our whole economy of greatness and invites conversion. Open domination can be resisted without changing us; humble greatness makes us feel the need to become different.
