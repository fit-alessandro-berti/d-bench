The sharpness is the point. Amos is not correcting an imbalance inside an otherwise acceptable relationship with God. He is exposing worship that has become a lie.

Israel’s feasts, offerings, and music were not being rejected because liturgy is worthless, or because God prefers ethics to religion in the abstract. They were being rejected because, in that moment, the cult was functioning as a substitute for covenant faithfulness and as cover for its opposite. The people were still bringing what the sacrificial system called acceptable—burnt offerings, grain offerings, fellowship offerings, the “pleasing aroma,” the songs—while crushing the poor, corrupting judgment, and treating the weak as expendable (Amos 2:6–8; 5:10–12). God answers in the cult’s own terms and then voids them: “I will not smell,” “I will not accept,” “I will not hear.” The machinery of approach is still running. Acceptance is refused.

That is why “better balance” would be too soft. Balance language assumes both sides are real and only the proportions are off: a bit more justice alongside the festivals. Amos treats the festivals, as practiced, as already false. Worship that coexists with systemic injustice is not incomplete devotion. It is a performance of nearness to God by people whose common life denies what that nearness requires. The prophet’s verbs are absolute—hate, despise, take away, will not regard—because partial acceptance would bless the fraud.

The fraud has several layers, and they belong together.

**Covenant theater.** Sacrifice and song were meant to express a people already bound to God’s justice. Here they are used to simulate that bond while the bond is being broken. Religious correctness stands in for the righteousness the covenant actually demanded. The worshiper leaves the sanctuary feeling covered; the oppressed leave the gate still robbed.

**Transactional religion.** The offerings are treated as if they obligate God—as if aroma, fat, and melody could purchase favor or cancel what was done in the marketplace and the court. Amos denies the transaction. God is not a creditor who can be paid off in the temple for debts incurred against the neighbor.

**Noise as concealment.** “Take away from me the noise of your songs” is not an attack on music. It is a judgment that the liturgy has become loud enough to drown the cry the prophet keeps naming. Solemn assembly and violated justice are not two separate compartments. The assembly is how Israel tells itself it is still God’s people while refusing the life that would make that claim true.

**False security.** Prosperity and full sanctuaries (Amos 5:18–20, the day of the Lord oracle just before this) bred confidence that God was with them. The oracle says the opposite: the very acts meant to secure divine presence are what God will not receive. The danger is not neglect of worship. It is worship that anesthetizes moral perception.

So the moral and spiritual fraud is this: **using the signs of reconciliation with God to avoid reconciliation with the neighbor, and calling that piety.** Judgment and righteousness are not a second hobby to be added to the feast calendar. “Let justice roll down like waters, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream” sets a continuous ethical flow against intermittent ritual. Until that flow is real, the feast is not a mixed good. It is something God tells them to take away.

Later Scripture does not read Amos as a permanent abolition of worship. It reads him as a standing test of worship: liturgy that legitimizes oppression is not received, however correct its form. The sharpness protects that test from being softened into good manners.
