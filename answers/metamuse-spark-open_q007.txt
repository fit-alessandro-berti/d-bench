This verse is often misread as:

> Nothing is really right or wrong, so never make a moral judgment about anyone.

That's not what Luke 6:37 is saying — and Luke himself makes that clear in the surrounding verses.

Right before this, Jesus says:

> "Be ye therefore merciful, as your Father also is merciful." - Luke 6:36

And right *after* it, he says:

> How can you help your brother remove a speck from his eye while you have a beam in your own? ... Look to the beam first, *then* you will see clearly to help him. - paraphrase of Luke 6:41-42
> 
> A good tree does not bring forth corrupt fruit... Every tree is known by his own fruit. - Luke 6:43-44

So in the same passage Jesus says: 1) be merciful in how you judge, 2) examine yourself first, and 3) you *do* need to learn to see clearly and tell good fruit from bad.

Moral judgment still matters immensely. What he is warning about is something more specific.

Two Greek words help here:

* **krino ("judge")** - to decide, to pass sentence, to put someone in their final box
* **katadikazo ("condemn")** - to sentence, to declare someone guilty and damned

He's not forbidding discernment. He's forbidding a *condemning posture* — taking the Judge's bench for yourself.

### 1. Discernment vs. The Appetite to Condemn

They can look similar from the outside, but their inner logic is opposite:

**Discernment is about clarity for the sake of love.**
The appetite to condemn is about *closure* for the sake of superiority.

A helpful way to distinguish them:

**1. Motive**
* Discernment: "What is true here, so I can act lovingly and responsibly?"
* Appetite to condemn: "How can I prove I am right, they are wrong, and therefore I am above them?"

Discernment wants to *see*. Condemnation wants to *enjoy* seeing someone put down. It's that subtle pleasure in delivering a final verdict on a person.

**2. Posture**
* Discernment starts with self-examination: "Is there a beam in my own eye distorting my vision?"
* Condemnation skips that step. It points outward quickly because it needs an external villain.

That's why Matthew's parallel to this teaching includes "judge not *that ye be not judged. For with what judgment ye judge, ye shall be judged*" (Matt. 7:1-2). It's not karma, it's a principle of the kingdom: the measure you use on others is the measure you are inviting for yourself. If you want a world of merciless verdicts, you will live in one.

**3. Scope**
* Discernment judges *actions, patterns, and fruit*: "This is dishonest. This is harmful. This is not safe for my children. This needs to be corrected."
* Condemnation judges *the ultimate worth and future of a person*: "This is who you *are*. You are worthless, irredeemable, and I am done with you as a human being."

The New Testament actually *requires* the first kind constantly. Parents must discern, judges must judge justly, friends must confront, communities must protect the vulnerable. Paul in the same tradition says in Luke 12:57, "Why do you not judge for yourselves what is right?"

**4. Aim**
* Discernment aims at restoration and protection: truth + mercy. "How can this be healed? How can harm be stopped? How can I help remove the speck *after* I deal with my beam?"
* Condemnation aims at punishment and humiliation: "How can this person be exposed and pay?"

You can see this in forgiveness as Jesus defines it here: "forgive, and ye shall be forgiven." Forgive doesn't mean "pretend it didn't matter." In that culture, to forgive meant to release a debt — to refuse to keep extracting payment, shaming, or controlling through the offense.

It says: This mattered enough to be a real debt, and I am choosing not to make you keep paying me for it forever.

### 2. Why Jesus warns so strongly

The appetite to condemn is dangerous for three reasons:

1. **It pretends to be discernment.** It's easy to feel righteous while being cruel.
2. **It blinds you.** A person hungry to condemn can't see clearly — anger and contempt distort perception. That's the beam.
3. **It traps you.** If your identity is built on being the judge of others, you become terrified of being judged yourself. That creates a community of fear, not mercy.

Modern examples make this vivid: doom-scrolling for people to be outraged at, piling on in comments for the pleasure of a take-down, writing a person off forever because of one category or mistake. That's not discernment — that's appetite.

Discernment can be quiet, sorrowful, humble, and firm at the same time. It can say, "This was wrong, there are consequences, and I still will not despise you."

In short, Luke 6:37 is not saying:

> Moral judgments never matter, so say nothing.

It's saying:

> Moral judgments matter so much that you must not make them like a tyrant. See clearly, start with yourself, judge as one who will also be judged, and let mercy lead — because that's how you have been treated.

The goal isn't to see less, but to see *better* — with eyes cleared by mercy.
