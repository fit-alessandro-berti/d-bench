## The inner contradiction

James exposes a **divided heart that shows up as a divided mouth**. The same person who lifts up praise to "God, even the Father" turns around and curses people "made after the similitude of God."

The contradiction runs deeper than inconsistent behavior. It is theological:

- **You cannot honor the original while degrading the image.** Human beings bear God's likeness (Gen. 1:26–27; 9:6). To curse a person is to show contempt for what God has stamped with His own resemblance. It is like kissing a king's portrait and spitting on the king's child.
- **Worship becomes suspect when it is detached from how we treat people.** John makes the same point: "He that loveth not his brother whom he hath seen, how can he love God whom he hath not seen?" (1 John 4:20). Praise that costs us nothing in the moment is easy. Blessing those who annoy, wrong, or outrank us exposes whether the praise was real.
- **It reveals double-mindedness.** James has already called the divided person *dipsychos*, "double-souled" (1:8). Here the double soul has a double voice.

James then reinforces the point with nature in verses 11–12. A spring doesn't give both sweet and bitter water, and a fig tree doesn't bear olives. Creation is consistent with its nature, and so his conclusion is that if both kinds of speech flow from one person, something is deeply wrong with the source.

## Why speech exposes disorder so quickly

**1. Speech is the least-guarded outflow of the heart.**
Jesus says, "Out of the abundance of the heart his mouth speaketh" (Luke 6:45). We can manage appearances, schedules, and reputations for a long time, but words escape in irritation, fatigue, pride, or fear, when the filter is thinnest.

**2. It is fast, cheap, and constant.**
Most sins need opportunity, planning, or resources. Speech needs only a moment. Because we speak so often, the tongue gives the heart far more chances to show itself than any other part of the body. That is why James says the person who does not stumble in word is "a perfect man, able also to bridle the whole body" (3:2).

**3. Small member, disproportionate effect.**
James compares the tongue to a bit, a rudder, and a spark (3:3–6). A few words can start a feud, destroy a reputation, or wound someone for years. Because speech is the point where private attitude becomes public action, it shows disorder in the heart and spreads it to others.

**4. We compartmentalize, and speech breaks the walls.**
People often keep a "worship voice" and an "everyday voice." The tongue moves between these settings without a pause, so the inconsistency becomes visible: blessing God on Sunday, cursing a driver, a spouse, or an enemy on Monday.

**5. Cursing dehumanizes.**
Contempt, insult, and cursing shrink a person into an obstacle or an enemy. Blessing acknowledges dignity. The two cannot coexist without one of them being false or at least unsettled.

**6. The tongue resists taming.**
"The tongue can no man tame" (3:8). That is not an excuse but a diagnosis. The problem is not mainly a matter of technique. If the tongue can't be mastered by willpower, the heart needs transformation, which is why James turns next to "the wisdom that is from above" (3:13–18).

## "These things ought not so to be"

The verse doesn't say this is rare or unforgivable, but that it is *unfitting*, contrary to what we were made for and what grace is reshaping us into. The remedy is to see people rightly, as image-bearers, and to let reverence for God govern every word we say about and to them. Consistent speech is the fruit of a unified heart.
