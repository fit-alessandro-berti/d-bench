The movement is deliberate: repentance involves both **renouncing evil and learning a new way of life**. “Cease to do evil” is necessary, but it is not sufficient. A person or community can stop committing a particular wrong and still remain indifferent to those who are suffering. Isaiah therefore moves from restraint to positive moral formation.

The word **“learn”** is especially significant. It suggests teachability, cultivation, and practice. Doing good is not assumed to follow automatically from religious profession, ritual worship, or even a sincere decision to stop sinning. A people thoroughly familiar with sacrifice may still need to learn goodness.

The rest of verse 17 defines what that goodness looks like:

- **“Seek judgment”**—actively pursue justice, rather than merely approve of it.
- **“Relieve the oppressed”**—respond to oppression, rather than simply avoid participating in it.
- **“Judge the fatherless”**—secure justice for the orphan, not pronounce condemnation.
- **“Plead for the widow”**—take up the cause of someone easily ignored or exploited.

The orphan and widow represent those with little social protection. They reveal whether power is being used merely for self-preservation or for the protection of others.

So goodness in this passage is **more than restraint, though never less than restraint**. It does not only ask, “What harm have I stopped doing?” but also, “What good have I learned to do, and whose life is more secure because of it?” Isaiah’s own vocabulary is especially that of justice and advocacy; calling this “love” is fitting if love means concretely seeking another’s good rather than simply feeling compassion.

In the wider context, this also explains the opening command to “wash” and “make clean.” The cleansing God seeks cannot be reduced to ceremony. It must appear in a transformed common life. True purity is not merely keeping oneself uncontaminated by evil; it is learning to practice justice toward one’s neighbors.
