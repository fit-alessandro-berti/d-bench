Enemy-love is difficult because it contradicts several very deep human instincts: self-protection, retaliation, loyalty to “our side,” and the sense that love must be earned. If someone curses, hates, or persecutes us, the natural question is not “How can I bless them?” but “Why should I?” Jesus’ command is hard precisely because it removes the enemy’s behavior as the controlling reason for our own moral response.

It is not sentimental.

Jesus does not say, “Have warm feelings toward them” or “Pretend they are not really harmful.” The command is concrete: **love, bless, do good, pray**. Those are actions before they are emotions. Christian love here is not affection, approval, or naïveté. It does not mean excusing evil, abandoning justice, ignoring danger, or allowing abuse. One can pray for an enemy, speak truthfully about their wrongdoing, protect victims, and seek accountability without hatred.

It is also not merely strategic.

“Kill them with kindness” can become a manipulative tactic: be nice so they will change, embarrassed, defeated, or won over. But Jesus does not promise that enemy-love will work in that sense. The ground of the command is theological: God “maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good, and sendeth rain on the just and on the unjust.” God’s generosity is not distributed only according to human worthiness. His people are called to resemble him. The point is not effectiveness but likeness to the Father.

So it is better understood as a deeper form of moral strength.

It is strength because it refuses to be governed by the enemy. Hatred usually means letting the hostile person set the terms of your inner life. Jesus calls for a freedom in which injury does not automatically produce revenge, cursing does not produce cursing, and persecution does not produce spiritual defeat. This is not weakness; it is self-mastery shaped by divine love.

It is also strong because it tells the truth about both parties: the enemy is truly capable of evil, and yet is still a human being before God, not merely an object of revenge. Prayer is especially important here. To pray for an enemy is to place that person before God and to surrender one’s claim to final judgment.

So enemy-love is not sentimental softness or a clever social strategy. It is cruciform moral power: the ability to do good without being ruled by hatred, to seek another’s true good without denying evil, and to imitate the generosity of God toward those who do not deserve it. That is why it is difficult—and why Jesus presents it as a mark of being “the children” of the Father.
