This is not a call to stop thinking. It's a call to stop making yourself the final authority for your thinking.

The image in Proverbs 3:5-6 is deliberate:

> "Trust in the LORD with all thine heart; and lean not unto thine own understanding. In all thy ways acknowledge him, and he shall direct thy paths."

The Hebrew helps here:

* **Trust (*batach*)** doesn't mean a feeling of optimism. It means to lie face-down in confident reliance — like taking refuge. It's relational, covenantal trust in a Person, not trust in a principle.
* **Heart (*lev*)** in Hebrew isn't just emotions. It's the seat of mind, will, and affections together. So "with all thine heart" means whole-person, undivided loyalty — not compartmentalized faith.
* **Lean (*sha'an*)** means to put your full body-weight on something, like leaning on a staff. He isn't saying "don't have understanding." He's saying "don't put all your weight on it as if it can hold you up."

That's why this immediately follows Proverbs' praise of wisdom. Just two chapters earlier: "Get wisdom, get understanding" (4:7). Proverbs 2 begs us to seek discernment like treasure. Proverbs *requires* diligent thinking, planning, listening to counsel, pondering your path.

So the verse cannot mean: reason is bad, study is unspiritual, planning is unfaithful.

### 1. What kind of trust is this?

**Total, exclusive, and practical.**

1. **Wholehearted, not half-hearted:** "with *all* thine heart." Not God *plus* my ego as co-pilot. Not trusting God on Sundays and leaning on my cleverness on Mondays.

2. **Godward, not self-ward:** "lean not unto *thine own* understanding." Your perception is real and useful, but it's finite, biased, and affected by sin. It's a good servant and a terrible god. Trust means treating God's character and Word as more stable than your read of the situation.

3. **All-of-life:** "In *all thy ways* acknowledge him." The word "acknowledge" is *yada* — to know intimately, in experience. Invite Him into doors, budgets, dating, parenting, work emails, anxieties. Not just the "spiritual" parts.

And then the promise: "he shall direct [make straight, *yashar*] thy paths." Not that He will make life effortless, but that He will clear, level, and guide — often through the very wisdom, counsel, and providence you sought in dependence on Him.

In short: autonomy says, "I see, therefore I know." This trust says, "God sees, therefore I can follow even when I don't fully see."

### 2. Humble self-distrust vs. anti-intellectualism

They look similar, but they are opposites:

**Anti-intellectualism says: Don't use your mind.**
It uses "I'm just trusting the Lord" as an excuse for laziness, impulsiveness, or refusal to be corrected. It won't study Scripture carefully, won't listen to wise counsel, won't look at facts. Ironically, that's often still leaning on your *own* understanding — just your feelings or first impressions re-labeled as "faith."

That's not humility. That's abdication. And Proverbs calls it folly.

**Humble self-distrust says: Use your mind, *in submission*.**
It recognizes: I am creaturely (I don't know the future), I am fallible (my reasoning can go wrong), and I am fallen (my desires distort my judgment). Therefore:

* I will think hard *and* pray hard.
* I will do my research *and* test it by Scripture.
* I will have convictions *and* remain teachable.
* I will plan *and* hold my plans loosely before the Lord (see Prov. 16:3, 16:9; James 4:13-15).

Think of it this way: Understanding is like eyesight. Anti-intellectualism gouges out the eyes and calls it faith. Humble self-distrust uses your eyes but admits you need light from outside yourself to see truly — and that light is God.

That's why verse 7 continues the thought: "Be not wise in thine own eyes: fear the LORD, and depart from evil."

Wise faith doesn't fear learning. It fears *self-sufficiency* in learning.
