**It is difficult because it contradicts our strongest instincts for reciprocity, justice, and self-protection, and it is easily dismissed as naive or weak until examined more carefully.**

The teaching is not primarily sentimental. Sentimentality would treat enemy-love as a warm feeling or vague niceness that evaporates under real threat, betrayal, or injustice. Jesus presents it as a command that includes blessing, doing good, and praying for those who actively harm you—actions that can be performed even when feelings of anger or fear remain. It is closer to a disciplined refusal to let the enemy dictate your character.

It is not merely strategic, though it can have strategic effects. Non-retaliation and unexpected goodwill have sometimes de-escalated conflicts, exposed injustice, or converted opponents (historical examples include certain applications by Gandhi or King, or smaller-scale reconciliations). The verse itself, however, grounds the practice in imitation of God: the Father “maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good.” The point is ontological and moral—becoming children who share the Father’s impartial generosity—rather than a calculated tactic for winning. Strategy can be abandoned when it fails; this is presented as a standing orientation.

It is better understood as a demanding form of moral strength. Reciprocity (“eye for an eye,” tit-for-tat) is the default human and even evolutionary setting: it is efficient, feels fair, and protects the in-group. Enemy-love requires overriding that default. It demands:
- Self-mastery over the impulse to return harm in kind.
- A refusal to let the other’s evil define your response (you do not become what you hate).
- Recognition that people are not reducible to their worst acts, while still allowing for justice, boundaries, and protection of the innocent.
- Alignment with a reality larger than immediate payback—here, the character of God who sustains even the undeserving.

This is why it is hard to take seriously in practice. We live in a world of genuine predators, ideological enemies, and zero-sum threats; treating the command as optional piety or “just be nice” makes it look unserious. Critics from Nietzsche onward have called it slave morality or a recipe for being exploited. Yet the alternative—unrelenting enmity—often produces cycles of retaliation that consume the “just” as thoroughly as the “unjust.” The teaching does not require pacifism in every circumstance, denial of evil, or failure to restrain harm; it requires that hatred not be the last word in your own soul.

In short, it is difficult precisely because it is neither cheap emotion nor clever maneuver. It is an attempt to live as if the universe is not ultimately governed by score-settling, which is a high-risk, high-cost claim. Whether one accepts the theological premise or not, the psychological and ethical demand remains: can you will the good of someone who wills you harm without becoming their mirror? That is a form of strength most people find rare and costly, which is why it is so often praised in theory and ignored in practice.
