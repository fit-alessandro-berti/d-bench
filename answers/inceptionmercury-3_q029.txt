Paul is not asking for a harsh, superior, or punitive posture. He is calling for a moral posture of **humble, gentle, restorative care**—rooted in the same grace that has held the corrector upright.

Three things are intentionally held together:

1. **Restoration as the goal**  
   The Greek word translated “restore” (*katartizō*) means to mend, repair, or set something right—like setting a broken bone or mending a torn net. The aim is not punishment, condemnation, or exclusion, but the person’s recovery.

2. **Meekness as the manner**  
   “Meekness” (*prautēs*) is not weakness; it is gentleness under control, strength ordered by love. In Galatians, it is listed as a fruit of the Spirit (Gal 5:23). So correction must be carried out in the Spirit’s way, not in the flesh’s way—without arrogance, anger, or self-righteousness.

3. **Self-suspicion as the protection**  
   “Considering thyself, lest thou also be tempted” is a deliberate act of moral humility. The corrector must remember that they too are fragile, capable of the same failure, and dependent on grace. This prevents the “spiritual” from becoming a judge who stands above the fallen brother, when in fact both stand in the same need of mercy.

So the required moral posture is: **a fellow-sinner, walking in the Spirit, who seeks to mend the broken, speaks with gentleness, and looks in the mirror before pointing the finger.** Restoration, meekness, and self-suspicion belong together because Christian correction is never a project of superiority—it is an act of shared weakness, mutual need, and grace.
