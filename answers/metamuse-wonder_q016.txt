All five are present, but they are not all at the same depth.

- **Anger** is what he feels.
- **Envy** is what he compares.
- **Wounded fairness** is the argument he makes.
- **Pride in obedience** is the identity he is protecting.
- **Discomfort with mercy itself** is the deepest problem.

His pride in obedience has built a moral economy in which mercy looks unjust.

### 1. He lives in his father's house like a slave, not a son

He says:

> “Lo, these many years do I **serve** thee, neither transgressed I at any time thy commandment: and yet thou never gavest me...”

The word translated “serve” is slave-language: *I have been slaving for you.* He is counting years, keeping accounts, and presenting his obedience as evidence in a claim against his father.

He does not say, “Father”—at least no affectionate address is recorded. Nor does he call the returned prodigal his brother. He calls him:

> “**this thy son**”

The father tenderly corrects both distortions:

> “**Son**, thou art ever with me... this **thy brother** was dead, and is alive again.”

The elder brother is physically close to the father but relationally distant from him. He has proximity without intimacy, obedience without joy, and religion without love.

### 2. He believes his obedience should have put his father in his debt

His complaint makes sense if the father's gifts are wages:

- I worked; he wasted.
- I obeyed; he rebelled.
- I stayed; he left.
- Therefore I deserve the celebration, and he does not.

But the father does not operate on wages. The fatted calf was not taken from the elder brother's portion. The father reminds him, “All that I have is thine.” The celebration for the younger brother does not diminish the elder brother's inheritance.

Grace only appears unfair when we assume God owes us His gifts as payment.

That is why the elder son's complaint contains a tragic contradiction: “Thou never gavest me a kid.” Perhaps externally that was true, but the father answers that everything had been available to him all along. He could have rejoiced in the father's abundance, yet he never really believed it was his. He waited for his obedience to earn what sonship had already given him.

### 3. Mercy threatens the identity by which he distinguishes himself

The elder brother needs the story to remain simple:

- He is the good son.
- His brother is the bad son.
- The father's favor proves the difference.

But the father shatters that arrangement. He receives the younger son not after a probationary period, not after repayment, and not with reduced status, but with the robe, ring, shoes, and feast.

Worse, the elder brother embellishes his brother's sin: he says he “devoured thy living **with harlots**.” The earlier account says the younger son wasted his property in reckless living, but the elder brother sharpens the charge. Self-righteousness often needs someone else's sin to appear as dark as possible so that its own record can appear brighter by contrast.

If the prodigal can be restored purely by the father's mercy, then the elder brother cannot use his obedience as currency. And that is unbearable to him—not merely because his brother receives something, but because *the way* his brother receives it makes his whole system unnecessary.

### 4. He wants a celebration, but apart from the father and brother

He says he wanted a kid:

> “that I might make merry **with my friends**”

Notice the separation. He does not long to rejoice with his father. He imagines a private reward enjoyed with his own circle, perhaps excluding both father and brother. Both sons, in different ways, wanted the father's gifts without the father's fellowship.

The younger brother pursued joy far from home.
The elder brother resents that he has not received joy at home—but he also will not enter the joy that is now occurring.

> “He was angry, and **would not go in**.”

The father must come out and plead with him, just as he earlier ran to the younger son. The parable ends without telling us whether the elder brother enters. His exclusion is, at that moment, self-chosen. Nobody has locked him out; the music is playing, the door is open, and his father is begging him to come inside.

His problem is therefore deeper than being upset about an unequal party. He cannot enter a feast whose central joy is that a lost person has been found by grace. To enter, he would have to surrender his record, forgive his brother, and receive his own place as gift rather than wages.

In short:

> **The elder brother is not merely angry that his brother received mercy. He is offended that mercy, rather than his obedience, determines who belongs at the father's table.**

That is why the parable ends outside the house. The younger son was lost in the far country; the elder son is lost at home.
