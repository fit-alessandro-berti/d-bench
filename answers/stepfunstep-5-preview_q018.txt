Paul is treating bitterness as more than a mood. It includes feeling, but in this text it functions mainly as a **settled posture of the heart that becomes habitual and socially contagious**.

### 1. Bitterness is “root-like”
Greek *pikria* means more than momentary irritation. In biblical usage it often names a deep, festering resentment: Deut 29:18 speaks of a “root bringing forth gall and bitterness,” and Heb 12:15 warns of a “root of bitterness” springing up. So Paul is not just saying, “Don’t feel badly.” He is saying, “Do not harbor the kind of inner injury that grows into poison.”

### 2. The grammar makes it an action, not only an emotion
The imperative is *“Let… be put away.”* The same chapter has already used clothing language: believers are to “put off” the old self and “put on” the new self. Bitterness is thus treated like a garment belonging to the “old humanity.” We do not merely observe a feeling inside us; we are commanded to remove it.

### 3. Paul places it against forgiveness because both are ways of handling offense
Bitterness and forgiveness are opposite ways of relating to injury.

- Bitterness keeps the account open. It preserves the injury as an ongoing claim.
- Forgiveness releases the debtor, “even as God for Christ’s sake” has released us.

The key Greek in v. 32 is *charizomenoi*, “forgiving,” related to *charis*, grace. To forgive is to give grace to someone who owes you. Bitterness refuses that grace. That is why the opposition is so absolute: someone receiving God’s grace cannot consistently practice the “keep-the-record-open” ethic toward others.

### 4. It is contagious in Paul’s context
Notice the downward movement in v. 31:

- bitterness — the inner root
- wrath/anger — emotional heat
- clamour — loud conflict
- evil speaking — destructive words
- malice — active ill will

Bitterness is not private. It leaks into speech and damages the church. In the larger argument of Ephesians, this breaks the unity the Spirit gives and grieves the Holy Spirit. “With all malice” suggests Paul is not treating one isolated feeling; he is naming an entire relational toxin.

### So, which category?
The text includes all four dimensions, but its main emphasis is this: bitterness is a **chosen, entrenched posture** that we can confess and uproot, even when it has become a **habit**, and unless resisted it becomes **contagious** in the community. Forgiveness is not simply the absence of bitterness; it is the positive, grace-filled habit that replaces it.
