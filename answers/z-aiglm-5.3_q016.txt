The five options aren't rivals so much as strata. The narrator gives them almost in order, from the outside in — and the father's reply tells us which one is bedrock.

**Anger is where the story starts, not where it ends.** It's real — "he was angry, and would not go in" — but anger is the fever, not the infection. Notice whom it's aimed at: not the brother, but the father. His entire speech is an indictment of the father's bookkeeping ("thou never gavest me... thou hast killed for him"). The brother is merely evidence in the case.

**Envy and wounded fairness are the content of the grievance** — he has been keeping a ledger. Years of service on one side, no kid goat on the other. But the father's answer dissolves the claim: "all that I have is thine." He was never underpaid, because he was never an employee. His own verb betrays him — "these many years do I *serve* thee" (δουλεύω, the word for a slave). He lived as a hireling in a house where he was an heir. Remove the ledger and the fairness-complaint has no ground — which is why it sounds less like justice than like a demand for *ranking*: he doesn't merely want his due; he wants the brother kept a servant. Grace isn't unfair to him; it's simply not comparative, and that is what he can't forgive. Note too his scholarship in his brother's sin: "with harlots" is *his* addition — the narrator never says it. Mercy forgets; resentment remembers, and embellishes.

**Pride in obedience goes deeper still.** "Neither transgressed I at any time thy commandment" — real obedience, but obedience converted into currency, a deposit earning interest, a claim against God. Two ironies undo it in the moment of its utterance. First, the claim is false in the very sentence that makes it: the father is entreating him to come in, and he refuses — he breaks filial obedience precisely while boasting in it. Second, his imagined reward excludes the giver: "that I might make merry *with my friends*." Even his fantasy of celebration doesn't include the father. He wants the father's things more than the father — which is why "thou art ever with me" is both true and tragic: proximity without communion, the field instead of the table.

**But pride, too, is an engine driving at something, and what it protects is this:** if the father forgives a profligate freely — robe, ring, feast, no probation — then obedience never purchased anything, and a lifetime of identity collapses. Hence the deepest of the five is discomfort with mercy itself. Luke says as much in the frame: the parable answers those who murmur that Jesus "receiveth sinners, and eateth with them" (15:2). The elder brother is the murmurers, drawn to scale. And the two preceding parables have fixed heaven's reaction: *joy* over one sinner who repents (15:7, 10). To stand outside this feast is to be out of sympathy with heaven. His quarrel is not with his brother, nor even with the estate's terms, but with the father's own heart — with mercy as the mode of God's love. To him, grace feels like theft.

Yet even "discomfort with mercy" names the wound without naming its cause. The father's one-sentence diagnosis does that: *"Son, thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine."* No rebuke — reaffirmation, in the tender vocative (τέκνον, "child"), repairing in his own grammar what the son had severed ("this *thy* son," "*thy* living" → "Son... this *thy* brother"). The elder brother's deepest problem is not that he lacks, or was cheated, or even that he is proud. It is that **he has never believed the father's love was free — not for his brother, and not for himself.** He kept accounts with a man who kept none; he lived in the house as an employee and so could never enjoy what was already his. Both sons, at the start, want the father's things rather than the father. The younger at least discovered, in the pigsty, that his father's love could not be forfeited, and came home to be astonished by it. The elder gets the same astonovation brought out to him — the father goes out a second time (v. 28, as he ran out in v. 20), keeps on pleading (the imperfect: "intreated"), bears the shame of both sons rather than let either stay lost.

And the parable stops before he answers. That unfinished ending is deliberate: it was aimed at the respectable listeners, and the father's plea still hangs in the air.

So, by your list: anger is the face; envy and fairness are the bookkeeping; pride in obedience is the scaffolding; discomfort with mercy is the allergy. But beneath them all lies a heart that never trusted that "all that I have is thine" was spoken to *it* — a son who never let himself be found by the same love that found his brother. That, finally, is why he is the parable's second prodigal: lost not in the far country, but at the edge of the feast, holding everything and entering nothing.
