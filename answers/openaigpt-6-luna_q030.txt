Paul’s portrait is full of negations because love is being contrasted with the ways ego turns strength into rivalry and control. In a community tempted by status and competition, envy, boasting, irritability, and suspicion make other people into threats or instruments. Love refuses those habits: it does not need to win every comparison, assert itself at every turn, or assume the worst.

But this is not a portrait of weakness or mere niceness. The positive verbs are strenuous: love is patient, kind, bears, hopes, and endures. Its strength lies in self-command and generosity—in being able to remain engaged with another person without dominating, retaliating, or keeping score.

Nor does love mean pretending that evil is good. It “rejoiceth in the truth.” The surrounding lines hold endurance together with truth: love can be trusting without being gullible, and patient without excusing harm. The vision is of a strength that does not depend on self-importance: resilient enough to make room for others, and honest enough to resist what is false.
