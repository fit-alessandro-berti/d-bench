Repentance involves both **“way” and “thoughts”** because returning to God means more than changing visible behavior; it means turning the inner life from which that behavior grows.

- **“Way”** is a person’s course of life—their habits, choices, and actions. Repentance therefore has an outward expression: wrongdoing must be forsaken, not merely regretted.
- **“Thoughts”** includes intentions, plans, judgments, and purposes. A person might stop an outward act while still cherishing it inwardly, justifying it, or imagining how to pursue it again. Repentance reaches those hidden loyalties too.

This does not mean that every passing or unwanted thought is a chosen sin. The emphasis is on the thinking a person **embraces and cultivates**—the inward world that shapes their “way.” Neither outward respectability without inward change nor inward remorse without changed conduct fully expresses the return described here.

Yet the passage’s center is not simply what must be abandoned, but **whom the sinner may return to**: “let him return unto the Lord.” God’s promised response is mercy and abundant pardon. The demand for deep repentance is therefore not a declaration that deeply rooted sin is beyond forgiveness. Quite the opposite: **the mercy offered reaches as deeply as the repentance required—into both what we have done and what we have cherished within.**

“While he may be found” gives the invitation urgency; “he will abundantly pardon” gives the sinner reason to answer it with hope rather than despair.
