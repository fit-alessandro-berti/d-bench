Enemy-love is difficult to take seriously for a reason the verse itself hints at: it refuses to fit into any of the categories we normally use for moral behavior. Our entire moral grammar runs on reciprocity—love for the lovable, punishment for the guilty, cooperation with the cooperative. A command that suspends the reciprocity filter doesn't just seem hard; it seems *incoherent*, like a currency that doesn't exchange for anything. So we do two things with it: we dismiss it as sentiment, or we admire it as tactic. Both moves, I'd argue, are ways of not taking it seriously.

**It is not sentimental—though we sentimentalize it.** The text never asks us to *feel* warmly toward enemies. The verbs are all volitional and concrete: bless, do good, pray. This is *agape*, not *eros*—a settled orientation of the will toward the other's flourishing, not a mood. Sentimental love collapses the moment it goes unreciprocated; agape is *defined* by loving the unreciprocating. The sentimentality is ours, not the text's: we domesticate the command into "niceness" precisely because the real thing is terrifying. As C.S. Lewis observed, everyone finds forgiveness a lovely idea until they have something to forgive.

**It can function strategically—but strategy corrupts it.** History shows the tactic works: Gandhi and King built movements on it, and Proverbs itself notes that feeding your enemy heaps burning coals on his head. But the moment love becomes a means—loving in order to convert, to disarm, to win the moral high ground—it stops being love and becomes manipulation with better manners. And if the strategy fails, the love gets withdrawn, which proves it was never love. Strategy also keeps the self intact: *I* love *you* for *my* ends. The verse grounds enemy-love not in any outcome but in God's own character—"that ye may be children of your Father," whose sun rises on the evil and the good without checking credentials first. God's generosity is not a technique for producing better behavior; it's simply how God runs the world.

**It is a deeper form of strength—stronger than we usually imagine.** Retaliation is cheap; it's reflexive. No one admires a man for hitting back, because he couldn't help it. To absorb a curse and return a blessing—to refuse to let another person's malice determine what you become—requires a power most people never once exercise. Nietzsche dismissed the command as slave morality, but his critique assumes strength means imposing your will; by that logic, renouncing the will to retaliate is the *hardest* act available to a human being, not the easiest. This is what King meant by "strength to love": the capacity to hold one's ground without hatred, to be wounded without being conquered.

But the verse points somewhere deeper than even heroic virtue. Notice the logic: love your enemies *so that* you may be children of your Father. The command isn't primarily a technique for fixing enemies—it's a means of becoming a certain kind of person, one whose love has the same shape as the rain: indiscriminate, unearned, falling on just and unjust alike. And this is the real reason it's so hard to take seriously. Taken seriously, it requires fuel we don't have. It's a command addressed to people who must first be transformed into the kind of people who could obey it. That's uncomfortable. It's much easier to file it under "beautiful but unrealistic" than to admit it's exposing us.

So: not sentimental, not merely strategic, and *strength* only in a sense that inverts our usual definition. Perhaps the difficulty is diagnostic. We call it naive or clever because those categories keep it at a manageable distance. Taken on its own terms, it is the most demanding thing in the moral life: to love the way the sun rises—not because the ground beneath it deserves light, but because that is simply what the sun does.
