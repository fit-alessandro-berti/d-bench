## The Elder Brother's Deepest Problem

Each option you name is present in the passage, but most are symptoms. The deepest problem is that **he does not understand himself as a son.** He relates to his father as a servant working for wages, and once the house is imagined that way, mercy can only look like injustice.

### The symptoms

**Anger** is the first thing the text names ("he was angry, and would not go in"), but anger is a reaction. The question is what it reacts to.

**Envy** is real ("thou never gavest me a kid"), but it is envy of a *reward*, which already shows how he thinks: gifts are payments, and someone else has been overpaid.

**Wounded fairness** deserves sympathy. By any ordinary standard, his complaint has force. Notably, the father never rebukes him or calls him wrong. He "came out, and intreated him," going out to the older son just as he ran out to the younger. But fairness is only the lens through which he reads the situation. It isn't the root.

**Pride in obedience** ("neither transgressed I at any time thy commandment") comes closer. He measures his standing by his record. Yet even this grows out of something deeper.

### The root: a servant in a son's house

The key word is his own: *"these many years do I **serve** thee."* The Greek *douleuō* means to serve as a slave. This is striking, because the younger brother had planned to return saying "make me as one of thy hired servants." The father refused that arrangement for the younger son, and here the elder son has been living in it voluntarily, at home, the whole time.

Several details confirm this:

- **"That I might make merry with my friends."** The celebration he imagines doesn't include his father. He wants the goods of the house, not the life of it. In this he is strikingly like his brother, who also wanted the inheritance more than the father.
- **"This thy son."** He won't say "my brother." He has disowned the relationship, and the father quietly restores it: "this thy **brother**."
- **"Devoured thy living with harlots."** Nothing earlier in the story mentions harlots. He supplies the worst version himself, because for his accounting to work his brother must be as unworthy as possible.
- **The father's answer:** "Son, thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine." The father doesn't argue about merit. He corrects the premise. The elder son was never a wage-earner owed a goat. He already owns everything. He has been poor in his father's house only because he didn't know what he was.

### So is it discomfort with mercy?

That is close, and it is what shows on the surface. But mercy offends only someone who believes the house runs on merit. If your standing rests on your record, then grace for someone with a bad record threatens the whole system you have built your life on. His discomfort with mercy is the logical result of his servant-identity.

That is why the father says "it was **meet**" (*edei*, "it was necessary"). Joy over the returned son isn't an optional indulgence that sets aside justice. In a family, as opposed to a workplace, it is the only fitting response.

### The frame and the open ending

Luke sets these parables in response to the Pharisees and scribes murmuring, "This man receiveth sinners, and eateth with them" (15:1–2). The elder brother is their portrait, drawn with sympathy rather than contempt. The parable then ends without telling us whether he goes in. The younger son "came to himself" in the far country. The elder son's equivalent moment would be realizing he has been lost at home, and the story leaves that door open, with the father standing outside it, pleading.
