**Victory over evil is defined as refusing to be reshaped by it and instead breaking its cycle through active, costly good.**

The passage (quoting Proverbs 25:21–22) does not treat “victory” as destroying the enemy, matching force with force, or even merely surviving. It is: do not let evil overcome *you* (by turning you vengeful, bitter, or like the evildoer), but overcome the evil *itself* by doing good. Feeding a hungry enemy or giving drink to a thirsty one is the concrete illustration. The “coals of fire” image is not primarily a sneaky punishment tactic; in context it describes the searing effect of unexpected kindness—shame, conviction, or disruption of the enemy’s narrative that may lead to repentance. The explicit command that follows is the point: overcome evil *with good*.

This is neither weak nor merely manipulative when read in the surrounding chapter (Romans 12:9–21). Paul has just commanded genuine love, blessing persecutors, associating with the lowly, and leaving vengeance to God. The action is costly and counter-instinctual; it requires self-control and trust rather than retaliation. Weakness would be being overcome—becoming what you hate. Calculated “kindness as psychological warfare” would also miss the preceding call to sincere love and the example of Christ, who practiced this while being executed.

It is genuinely strong because it attacks evil at the root (the cycle of reciprocity and dehumanization) rather than just its symptoms. It follows the logic of the cross: apparent defeat that actually disarms and can redeem. Historical and personal examples (from early Christian responses to persecution onward) show it sometimes converts enemies and sometimes simply prevents the victim from being morally consumed. It does not guarantee the enemy’s change; it guarantees you are not conquered. That is a harder, more durable form of victory than most alternatives. 

Interpretations of the coals vary (shame leading to repentance is the most common reading that fits Paul’s “overcome with good”), but the ethical core does not depend on any single metaphor.
