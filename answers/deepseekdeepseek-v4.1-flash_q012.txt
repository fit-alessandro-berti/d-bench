Jeremiah 17:9–10 is not mainly a stand-alone cynical statement about humans being worthless. In context (vv. 5–8), it contrasts **trusting in man** with **trusting in the LORD**. So the passage is:

- **Pessimistic about self-trust and moral self-sufficiency**: your own heart is not a safe final authority.
- **Realistic about self-deception**: the heart can rationalize, hide, misname motives, and justify sin.
- **Not hopeless**, because v. 10 says the LORD searches the heart and tests the inner person. God is the reliable knower and judge.

The Hebrew word for “heart” includes the mind, will, intentions, and inner life—not just emotions. “Deceitful” carries the idea of crooked or guileful; “desperately wicked” is often translated “desperately sick” or even “incurable.” So the point is not “you can never know anything about motives.” It is “you cannot know them exhaustively or infallibly as God does.” The heart is an unreliable witness, not a nonexistent one.

### How to talk about motives if the heart is unreliable

1. **Speak with humility and provisionality.**  
   Say, “It seems to me…,” “I wonder if…,” “I may be wrong, but…” Avoid, “I know your heart” or “I know exactly why you did that.” Only God searches the heart.

2. **Let God be the final judge.**  
   Pray with Psalm 139:23–24: “Search me, O God, and know my heart.” Paul even says he does not ultimately judge himself; the Lord is the one who judges and will bring hidden motives to light (1 Cor. 4:3–5).

3. **Distinguish actions and fruits from inner motives.**  
   You can say, “That action was hurtful” or “This pattern concerns me.” You can ask, “What were you hoping for?” But you should not pronounce a final verdict on someone’s inner intent. Jesus said you know people by their fruits (Matt. 7:16–20), but fruits reveal patterns, not infallible mind-reading.

4. **For your own motives, assume mixed motives.**  
   Confess that you may be self-deceived. Invite Scripture, prayer, wise friends, and counselors to help you see blind spots. Obey God even when motives are not perfectly pure, and ask Him to purify them. Do not use “my heart is deceitful” as an excuse.

5. **For others, avoid weaponizing the verse.**  
   “Your heart is deceitful” can become gaslighting or control. Jeremiah says **God** searches the heart—not you. In conflict, try: “I don’t know your intent; can you help me understand?” rather than “You did that because you’re selfish.”

6. **Aim at repentance and healing, not endless introspection.**  
   The passage does not call us to navel-gaze forever. It calls us to stop trusting ourselves as final judges and to trust the Lord, who both searches and saves. The same God who diagnoses the heart also promises a new heart and a new covenant (Jer. 31:33; Ezek. 36:26).

So: Jeremiah 17:9–10 is realistic about self-deception and pessimistic about self-trust, but not despairing about human nature. The heart is an unreliable witness; God is a reliable searcher. Talk about motives, then, the way someone who is also being searched by God should talk—humbly, tentatively, prayerfully, and with grace.
