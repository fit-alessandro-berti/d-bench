Isaiah 1:16-17 is a classic prophetic turn from ritual to ethics, and the movement from negative to positive is intentional.

The first imperative is negative and purifying: *Wash you, make you clean; put away the evil of your doings from before mine eyes; cease to do evil.* 

That is the language of repentance as withdrawal. *Chadal ra’*, “cease to do evil,” is a stopping, a clearing away. In the context of Isaiah 1:10-15, Judah is bringing sacrifices while still exploiting the poor. God says the ritual is worthless without moral withdrawal. You have to stop what is offensive before Him.

But stopping is not the goal. The prophet immediately adds *lamad tov*, “learn to do well.” *Lamad* is not “realize” or “decide.” It is the word for disciplined apprenticeship, for learning a trade, a skill. You learn to do well the way a craftsman learns a craft: by instruction, repetition, practice, and failure. Goodness is not a natural default that emerges once evil is removed.

That is why the positive command is then spelled out in concrete social terms, not abstract piety:

* seek *mishpat*, judgment - right order, due process, the restoration of equity;
* relieve the oppressed, *‘anok*;
* judge the fatherless, *yatom*;
* plead for the widow, *almanah*.

These are not private virtues. They are the vulnerable classes in a patronage society who have no voice and no protection. To *judge* the fatherless and *plead* for the widow is active advocacy, costly and public.

The move from “cease to do evil” to “learn to do well” therefore says:

1. **Restraint is necessary but insufficient.** Moral minimalism - don’t oppress, don’t steal, don’t lie - leaves a vacuum. Cessation of evil does not automatically create justice.
2. **Goodness is active, relational, and formative.** It is not the mere absence of sin but the practiced presence of love and justice. It must be learned because it requires skill in discernment - what does seeking judgment actually look like in a particular case? - and habit formation in the community.
3. **Repentance is two movements.** Isaiah’s repentance is *turning away from* and *turning toward*. The first clears the field; the second fills it with what God desires. The washing imagery is inward moral cleansing, but it is completed in outward, embodied care for the neighbor.

In short, Isaiah refuses a goodness defined by what you do *not* do. Goodness is what you have been taught to do, over and over, until justice for the fatherless and widow becomes second nature. It is a practiced form of love.
