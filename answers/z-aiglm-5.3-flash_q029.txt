## The posture: a physician kneeling, not a judge ascending

Paul's choice of verbs determines everything. **"Restore" translates *katartizō* (καταρτίζω)** — the word used for mending torn nets (Matt 4:21), setting a dislocated limb, fitting a bone back into place. Three features of this word fix the posture:

- **It is medical, not judicial.** The restorer comes to a fracture the way a surgeon comes to a broken arm: near enough to touch, concerned with function, aiming at wholeness. A magistrate keeps distance; a healer cannot.
- **It assumes membership.** You restore what belongs to a whole. A net is mended *because* it is a net; a joint is set *because* it is part of a body. The fallen man is treated as a member to be reintegrated, never as a problem to be excised.
- **It is continuous work.** The present imperative (*katartizete*, "keep on restoring") forbids both the once-and-done verdict and the impatient demand for instant recovery. Fractures heal slowly, and the mender must stay.

## Who is authorized, and by what

"Ye which are spiritual" sounds elitist until you check the context. Immediately preceding (5:16, 22–23), Paul has defined spirituality as *walking by the Spirit* and exhibiting the Spirit's fruit — which explicitly includes **meekness (*prautēs*) and self-control**. So the qualification for correcting and the manner of correcting are *the same thing*: only the gentle may handle a wound, because gentleness is the evidence that the Spirit governs them. A correction delivered in harshness falsifies itself, however accurate its diagnosis. Meekness here is not weakness; it is strength refusing to dominate — and since repentance is finally God's gift, not our coercion (2 Tim 2:24–25), vehemence grants nothing that patience would have obtained.

## Why self-suspicion completes the posture

"Considering thyself" (*skopōn seauton* — keep looking at yourself, scrutinizingly) shifts from plural commands to a pointed singular: *"lest *thou* also be tempted."* Each would-be restorer individually receives the reminder. The little word "**also**" carries Paul's whole anthropology: no one holds an exemption from temptation, and the person who presumes immunity is the classic casualty (1 Cor 10:12).

Notice what sits immediately around this verse. Galatians 5:26 had forbidden *vainglory, provoking, envying*; Galatians 6:3 names the disease outright: "if a man think himself to be something, when he is nothing, he deceiveth himself." Correction is precisely the arena where pride dresses itself as piety. Self-suspicion is therefore not neurotic introspection added to the task — it is the hygiene that keeps the task from corrupting the worker.

## Why the three must stand together

They form a single grammar of love, and severing any one distorts the rest:

- **Restoration without meekness** becomes purge and punishment. An angry hand setting a fracture breaks it worse; harshness drives the wounded away from the body, defeating the aim entirely (contrast Heb 12:13: "lest that which is lame be turned out of the way; but let it rather be healed").
- **Restoration without self-suspicion** curdles into condescension — a superior dispensing mercy downward. The difference between restorer and restored is functional, never ontological; the same flesh, the same adversary.
- **Meekness and humility without the recovery-aim** collapse into sentimental paralysis: "Who am I to say anything?" Matthew 7:1–5 shows the balance — Jesus does not forbid removing the speck from a brother's eye; he *orders* it, but only after the beam is dealt with. Self-knowledge does not cancel correction; it cleanses its vision and tempers its touch.
- **All three together** reproduce the pattern of Christ himself, whom Peter describes as committing no sin, neither was guile found in his mouth — the one who bore burdens is the "law of Christ" Paul invokes in the next breath (6:2). Peter is the living case study: overtaken in a fault, restored by his Lord, then commissioned — "*when thou art converted, strengthen thy brethren*" (Luke 22:32).

## The posture in one picture

One hand extended to lift; the other hand pressed against your own chest, feeling your own pulse of weakness; the eyes fixed not on comparing yourself upward against the fallen man, but forward — toward getting him back on his feet beside you. Prompt enough to come near, gentle enough not to wound, sober enough to know you kneel beside him rather than above him.

That is why Paul cannot say "restore" without adding "in the spirit of meekness," and cannot finish that sentence without adding "considering thyself." They are not three duties attached by accident. They are one act viewed from three angles.
