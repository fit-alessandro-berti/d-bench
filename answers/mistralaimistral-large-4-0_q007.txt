The verse is not a charter for moral indifference; it is a surgical strike against a particular spiritual disease: the *appetite* to condemn. The difference lies between **discernment** (the necessary work of the mind to see clearly) and **condemnation** (the craving of the ego to damn).

Here is how to hold the tension:

## 1. Moral judgment is unavoidable; moral *condemnation* is optional
Christianity does not permit us to suspend moral reasoning. Jesus Himself exercised fierce discernment—He called Herod a "fox" (Luke 13:32), exposed the Pharisees as "whitewashed tombs" (Matthew 23:27), and warned, "Beware of false prophets" (Matthew 7:15). The Apostle Paul commanded the Corinthians to "judge those inside the church" (1 Corinthians 5:12) and to "test the spirits" (1 John 4:1).

The Greek is precise. Luke uses **κατακρίνω** (*katakrinō*)—not merely to evaluate (*krinō*), but to **condemn**, to render a final verdict of damnation. The prohibition is against usurping the divine throne, issuing the irrevocable sentence that only God can execute. It is the difference between a physician diagnosing a wound and a prosecutor demanding the death penalty.

## 2. Discernment is humble; condemnation is hungry
**Discernment** (*diakrinō* in Greek) is the faculty of distinguishing between truth and error, good and evil. It is:
- **Provisional**: "This seems wrong, but I may be missing context."
- **Self-implicating**: "Judge not... first take the log out of your own eye" (Matthew 7:3-5). True discernment begins with self-examination.
- **Restorative**: It seeks to heal, warn, or correct (Galatians 6:1).

The **appetite to condemn**, by contrast, is a form of spiritual gluttony. It is the dopamine hit of moral superiority—the rush of feeling righteous while another person feels small. It is:
- **Final**: "This person *is* evil, not merely doing evil."
- **Self-exempting**: It requires no self-examination; it feeds on the hiddenness of one's own faults.
- **Consumptive**: It does not seek the other's restoration but their exclusion.

Discernment asks, "What is the truth here, and how do I love this person?" The appetite to condemn asks, "How can I prove I am right and they are wrong?"

## 3. The difference is love
Jesus’ teaching sits in a chapter about enemy-love (Luke 6:27-36). The logic is: *You will be treated as you treat others* (v. 38). The "appetite to condemn" treats people as objects to be managed or eliminated; discernment treats them as image-bearers to be served.

When you discern, you say: "This behavior is destructive; I must speak the truth in love" (Ephesians 4:15). When you condemn, you say: "This person is disposable; I need them to be the villain so I can be the hero."

## 4. The reciprocal economy
Notice the parallel structure: "Forgive, and ye shall be forgiven." The verse is not about abstaining from moral clarity; it is about **breaking the cycle of retribution**. The appetite to condemn is a demand for justice that we refuse to extend to ourselves. It wants a double standard: mercy for me, wrath for thee.

To "not judge" in the biblical sense is to refuse to play the game of cosmic scorekeeping. It is to recognize that every moral assessment we make of another is a mirror held up to our own fragility. We are all, as Paul says, "trespassers" (Ephesians 2:1). The moment we condemn another, we forget that we stand on the same ground, saved only by grace.

## In practice:
- **Discernment** looks at a situation and says: "This is sin. I must pray, confront, or separate myself, but I do so weeping, knowing I am the chief of sinners."
- **Condemnation** looks at a person and says: "They are sin. I am glad I am not like them."

The first builds the Kingdom; the second builds a shrine to the self.

Moral judgments matter immensely—they are the guardrails of love. But they must be made with the trembling awareness that we are judging from inside the same prison we are trying to help others escape. Discernment is the scalpel; the appetite to condemn is the guillotine. The Christian is called to wield the former with tears, and to crucify the latter.
