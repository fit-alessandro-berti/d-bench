Enemy-love is difficult to take seriously because it can feel like a betrayal of justice. When someone harms you, anger can tell you something true: *That was wrong, and it matters.* The temptation is to take a further step and conclude that, unless you wish the person harm in return, you haven’t taken the wrong seriously enough. Jesus challenges that conclusion, not the judgment that the harm was real.

Notice the verbs: **bless, do good, pray.** None requires affection. Nor is the command chiefly strategic. Kindness may disarm an enemy, but Jesus doesn’t say, “Love them so that they will change.” He points instead to the Father, who sends rain on the just and the unjust alike. The distinction between just and unjust remains; what God refuses to make conditional on it is the gift of rain.

That makes enemy-love a demanding form of moral strength: to seek another person’s good without surrendering your judgment about what they’ve done. You can leave an abusive relationship, tell the truth about the abuse, and seek protection without making the abuser’s suffering your goal. You might pray that they stop, face what they’ve done, and change. That prayer is neither tender sentiment nor a tactic for winning them over. It is a refusal to let their cruelty decide what you will wish for them.
