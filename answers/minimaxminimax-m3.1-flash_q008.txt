The verse treats love of the neighbor as a **necessary sign of love of God**, not as a substitute for it. God cannot be loved abstractly while love is refused in the concrete person closest to us. The command “love your neighbor” is an expression of “love God”: what is claimed as love for the invisible God must become visible in love for the person we can see.

The verse exposes **religious and moral self-deception**—the temptation to preserve a flattering identity (“I am loving, spiritual, and devoted to God”) while exempting oneself from costly love toward particular people. Such a person may say, “I love God,” but hatred of the brother shows that the profession is really pride, self-approval, or a desire for moral reputation rather than love. The contradiction cannot be dismissed by saying that love for God is “inner” and love for neighbor is merely external: genuine love is not a private sentiment; it takes shape in how we treat others.

The neighbor is therefore a **diagnostic test**, not the whole test. Loving the neighbor does not by itself prove love of God, as the verse’s logic also implies; but refusing to love one’s brother exposes a claim to love God as false. The deception is the attempt to **compartmentalize devotion and conduct**: to want God’s approval without God’s command of neighbor-love.
