Enemy-love is difficult to take seriously precisely because we take it *literally* - and when taken literally, it violates almost every other moral intuition we have.

Matthew 5:44-45 is not a Hallmark card. In the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus is not softening the Law, He is radicalizing it:

> You have heard it said, love your neighbor and hate your enemy. But *I* say to you...

He is moving love from a natural, reciprocal emotion to a *discipline*. That's why it feels impossible. It asks for three things we are not built to give easily:

### 1. Why It Feels Unserious

**a) It violates our sense of justice.**
We have a deep, evolved instinct for reciprocity: *do good to those who do good to you, harm those who harm you.* That instinct feels like fairness. To bless those who curse you feels like betrayal - betrayal of yourself, and betrayal of victims. We worry: Does loving my enemy mean excusing what they did?

**b) It violates our sense of tribe.**
Psychologically, we form identity through in-group / out-group. The enemy is useful. He tells us who *we* are. To love the enemy threatens to dissolve the boundary that makes us feel righteous and safe.

**c) It has been misused.**
"Love your enemies" has often been weaponized to tell oppressed people to stay passive, to not resist, to forgive without repentance. When we have seen it used as a tool for keeping power in place, we rightly become cynical about it.

So the modern ear hears it and translates it as either naive or manipulative. Which leads to your three options:

### 2. Is It Sentimental, Strategic, or Strength?

It can look like all three, but only one captures what Jesus is actually describing.

**1. As Sentimentalism: "Just be nice."**
If enemy-love is a feeling - *have warm affection for the person trying to destroy you* - then it is both sentimental and psychologically impossible. Sentiment cannot be commanded. If this is what it means, it is not a morality, it's denial. It asks you to pretend the enmity isn't real.

This is not what the Greek means. The word used is *agapao*, not *phileo* or *eros*. *Phileo* is affection, friendship. *Eros* is desire. *Agape* is *willed* good - to will the good of the other, to act for their ultimate well-being, independent of feeling. You are not commanded to *like* your enemy. You are commanded to *not dehumanize* them.

As C.S. Lewis put it: "Do not waste time bothering whether you 'love' your neighbor; act as if you did."

**2. As Strategy: "Love works better."**
There is a strategic reading, and it is powerful. Martin Luther King Jr. in his sermons on this text argued that nonviolence and enemy-love is the only pragmatism that actually breaks the cycle:

> "Hate multiplies hate, violence multiplies violence... Love is the only force capable of transforming an enemy into a friend."

Game theory supports this. Tit-for-tat retaliation creates endless loops of defection. Unconditional, costly good will is the only move that can reset the game. Gandhi saw the same.

In this view, you love your enemies because it *disarms* them. It is effective.

The problem: If it's *merely* strategic, you will stop doing it when it stops working. And loving enemies often *doesn't* work in the short term. It gets you crucified. Jesus knew that.

**3. As Deeper Moral Strength: "Become like the Father."**

Look at the second half of the verse, which is often ignored. The reason given is not "because it will make them change" or "because you'll feel better."

> *That ye may be the children of your Father which is in heaven: for he maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good.*

The argument is ontological, not instrumental. The sun does not shine *to* make the field worthy; it shines because that is what the sun *is*. Rain does not check if the soil is just before it falls.

Enemy-love, in this framing, is not about the worthiness of the enemy. It is about the *character of the lover*. It is an identity practice.

Nietzsche famously mocked this as weakness, as "slave morality" - the virtue of the powerless who cannot take revenge. But Simone Weil flipped that critique:

> Weakness can only forgive what does not wound it deeply. To truly love the enemy requires more strength than to hate him.

Hate is reactive. It lets the enemy determine who you are and how you act. Enemy-love is *creative*. It says: I will not let your hatred set the terms for my humanity. I will act from abundance, not from injury.

That is why it is the hardest of all virtues. Courage faces danger; justice faces inequity; but enemy-love faces the desire for revenge itself - the most natural and most intoxicating moral feeling we have.

It does not mean:
* Pretending harm didn't happen
* Reconciliation without truth or repentance
* Removing boundaries or allowing abuse to continue
* Abandoning justice. To will someone's good can include willing that they be stopped, judged, and prevented from harming others.

It means refusing to reduce a person to their worst act against you.

### In Short:

* To the cynic, enemy-love looks **sentimental** - too soft to be real.
* To the pragmatist, it looks **strategic** - a clever way to win.
* To the one trying to live it, it reveals itself as **strength** - and the most demanding strength of all, because you have to do good without the guarantee you will be thanked, vindicated, or even safe.

That is why it is so difficult to take seriously. It asks us not to be *nicer* than we are, but to be *freer* than we are - free from the mirror of reciprocity where every curse must be returned with a curse.

> Do you read Jesus here as laying down an impossible ideal to make us aware of our limits, or as describing a possible, practiced discipline - like the way King or Desmond Tutu practiced it - that actually forms a different kind of person over time?
