The negations are doing diagnostic work. Paul is writing to a church full of rivalry: factions claiming allegiance to different leaders (1:12), spiritual one-upmanship over gifts like tongues and prophecy (ch. 12–14), lawsuits between believers (ch. 6), and humiliating divisions at the Lord's Supper (ch. 11). The list of what love *does not* do names the sins that were tearing the community apart. Love is defined partly by refusal because the habits it refuses are the default human ones, and they are the ones that corrode shared life.

The negations also make love concrete. Feelings are hard to verify, but a person can check whether they are keeping score, enjoying someone's failure, or retaliating. Paul treats love less as a sentiment and more as a discipline of restraint.

The vision of strength is the most striking part. Paul's verbs reverse the usual signs of power in his world:

- **Vaunting and puffing up** were the marks of honor culture, where status was displayed and defended. Love does not need to display itself.
- **Being provoked** and **thinking evil** describe the reflex of the person who keeps a ledger of slights and answers every insult. Love does not keep that ledger.
- **Rejoicing in iniquity** is the pleasure of seeing a rival fall, which is a form of secret power-seeking.

What remains is a strength of endurance and self-possession. The key terms are *makrothymei* (suffers long), *hypomenei* (endures), and *stegei* (bears, covers, protects). These suggest staying power under pressure rather than force. Stoic writers admired endurance too, but here it is directed toward other people. The final four verbs, "beareth, believeth, hopeth, endureth," build toward this. Bearing and enduring are the strength to absorb cost without retaliating. "Believeth all things" is not gullibility. It means refusing to presume the worst, which is the positive side of "thinketh no evil." "Hopeth all things" ties this patience to the future, so love is not naive optimism about people but durable trust in God's ultimate reconciliation.

The deepest root of this strength appears in the surrounding chapter. Love can give up pride, envy, and the need to win because the self is already secure. Paul says "now I know in part; but then shall I know even as also I am known" (13:12). A person who is fully known and accepted by God does not need to vaunt, compete, or keep accounts. On this view, the strength Paul proposes is the freedom of someone who no longer needs to prove anything, and so can bear with others.
