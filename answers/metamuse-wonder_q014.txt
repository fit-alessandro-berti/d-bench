This text says **treasure trains the heart**, and only secondarily that it reveals it.

That order matters. Jesus does not begin with “Look inside to discover what you love.” He begins with commands: **“Lay not up… but lay up…”** (vv. 19–20). Then he gives the reason: *“For where your treasure is, there will your heart be also.”* 

The future tense is telling: your heart **will be** where your treasure is placed. Treasure is not merely a symptom of prior devotion; it is a means of forming devotion. If you continually invest resources in something, your affections, fears, hopes, and loyalties gather around that investment.

So the verse works in two directions, but not equally:

- **Revealing:** an honest audit of your money, time, security-seeking, and anxieties will show what you actually trust.
- **Training:** because the heart follows stored treasure, you can redirect the heart by deliberately relocating the treasure.

Jesus is giving a spiritual practice, not just a diagnostic test.

### Possession, attention, love

In biblical language, the **heart** is not just emotion. It is the center of willing, thinking, desiring, and committing. So the chain in Matthew 6 looks like this:

**1. Possession becomes trust.**  
To “lay up” treasure is to decide where your security lies. What you store is what you count on for the future. Earthly treasure is self-kept and self-guarded: *“Lay not up for yourselves treasures upon earth.”* The issue is not simply owning things, but building a private reserve that must finally save you.

**2. Trust captures attention.**  
What you must protect, you must watch. Earthly treasure decays and can be taken — moth, rust, thieves — so it demands vigilance. The next image in the passage is exactly about attention: the eye as lamp (vv. 22–23). What we treasure teaches the eye where to look, what to notice, what to fear losing, what to keep checking on.

**3. Attention deepens into love and service.**  
Repeated attention becomes attachment. What occupies the mind and secures the future slowly becomes master. That is why the passage ends with: *“Ye cannot serve God and mammon”* (v. 24). Treasure moves from possession, to preoccupation, to allegiance. You do not merely have it; eventually, in a functional sense, it has you.

Earthly treasure therefore produces anxiety because the heart has been tied to what can rot or be stolen. The rest of Matthew 6 follows naturally: “Therefore I say unto you, Take no thought [be not anxious]…” (v. 25). Worry is what happens when the heart lives where moths and thieves can reach.

### “Treasures in heaven”

“Treasures in heaven” are not stockpiled by keeping more, but by giving, forgiving, secret prayer, mercy, and righteousness done before the Father rather than for display (Matt. 6:1–18). In other words, treasure is transferred upward by being released outward.

That is the paradox: earthly treasure is stored by grasping; heavenly treasure is stored by entrusting. It is secure not because you guard it better, but because God keeps it.

So Jesus’ logic is almost psychological as well as theological: **you will love what you keep funding; you will fear for what you keep guarding; you will serve what you keep trusting.** Move the treasure, and the heart is schooled to follow.
