Read in context, **Luke 6:37 is not a counsel of moral indifference.** It does not mean “nothing is really right or wrong” or “never make a moral judgment.” It means: **do not relate to people with a condemnatory, merciless spirit.** The verse assumes real guilt and real judgment, and then commands a radically forgiving posture toward guilty people.

## 1. The verse actually intensifies moral realism, not eliminates it

Look at what follows:

> “Judge not… condemn not… **forgive**…”

The Greek word behind “forgive” in Luke 6:37 is literally **“release”** (ἀπολύετε) — as in releasing a debt or setting someone free. But you can only release a debt if a debt really exists.

So the logic of the verse is:

- There are real wrongs to be judged.
- There are real debts that could be condemned.
- But instead of binding people under a verdict, Jesus says: **release them.**

If moral judgments never mattered, this verse would collapse. There would be nothing to condemn, nothing to forgive, and no need for mercy. Forgiveness is not pretending the wrong did not happen; forgiveness names the wrong and then cancels the debt.

## 2. “Judge not” is not the same as “discern nothing”

The Bible elsewhere commands God’s people to make moral judgments:

- **“Judge with righteous judgment”** (John 7:24)
- **“Test everything; hold fast what is good”** (1 Thess 5:21–22)
- **“Beware of false prophets… you will recognize them by their fruits”** (Matt 7:15–20)
- **Church discipline requires judging sin in the community** (Matt 18:15–17; 1 Cor 5)
- **“Have no fellowship with the unfruitful works of darkness, but rather expose them”** (Eph 5:11)

So the issue is not *whether* we judge, but *how* and *why*.

The kind of judgment Jesus forbids is the censorious, self-righteous, unforgiving kind — the appetite to condemn. The kind of judgment Scripture commands is humble, truthful, restorative discernment.

## 3. Discernment vs. the appetite to condemn

| **Discernment** | **Appetite to condemn** |
|---|---|
| Recognizes that an act is wrong | Relishes declaring a person guilty |
| Aims at truth, healing, protection, restoration | Aims at shaming, punishing, excluding |
| Begins with self-examination (“first take the log out of your own eye”) | Is blind to its own faults (Rom 2:1) |
| Speaks reluctantly, prayerfully, sorrowfully | Speaks eagerly, harshly, even gleefully |
| Uses facts to heal | Uses facts as ammunition |
| Leaves room for repentance | Wants a final verdict now |
| Leaves ultimate judgment to God (Rom 12:19; James 4:12) | Usurps God’s seat |

A simple way to say it:

> **Discernment is diagnostic; condemnation is sentencing.**

A good doctor diagnoses a disease in order to heal. An executioner diagnoses guilt in order to destroy. Both may see the same facts, but their spirit and goal are opposite.

Discernment says:  
**“This is sin. It is destroying you. Let me help you turn from it.”**

The appetite to condemn says:  
**“You are disgusting. You deserve to be cast out. I am glad you are exposed.”**

The first may be painful but is aimed at redemption. The second may even use true words, but is aimed at ruin.

## 4. Jesus shows the difference perfectly

In John 8, the woman caught in adultery is brought to Jesus. The religious leaders want to condemn her. Jesus does two things at once:

1. **He does not deny the moral reality:**  
   “Go and sin no more.”

2. **He refuses to condemn:**  
   “Neither do I condemn you.”

That is Luke 6:37 in action. Jesus takes sin seriously, but He does not satisfy the crowd’s appetite to condemn. In fact, at the cross He takes the condemnation upon Himself so that sinners can be released.

This is why the verse cannot mean “moral judgments never matter.” Jesus forgives a real sinner for a real sin. He just refuses to let the final word be condemnation.

## 5. The opposite of “condemn” is “forgive,” not “ignore”

This is crucial:

> The biblical opposite of “condemn” is not “pretend nothing is wrong.”  
> The biblical opposite of “condemn” is **“forgive / release.”**

Forgiveness is one of the most morally serious acts in the universe. It says:

- A real wrong was done.
- A real debt exists.
- I will not make you pay for it.
- I release you, because I have been released by God.

That is why “forgive, and you shall be forgiven” is not a cheap bargain. It describes the character of the Kingdom: **those who have been forgiven much become forgiving people** (Matt 18:21–35). If we refuse to release others, we show we have not really grasped how much we have been released from.

## 6. How to hold it together

The Christian calling is not to choose between “judging everything” and “judging nothing.” It is to hold **truth and mercy** together:

- Judge actions, teachings, and fruit — yes.
- Name sin clearly — yes.
- Practice discernment — yes.
- But do it with humility, tears, gentleness, and a willingness to forgive.
- Restore the sinner “in a spirit of gentleness,” watching yourself (Gal 6:1).
- Speak “the truth in love” (Eph 4:15).
- Leave vengeance and final judgment to God.

## In short

“Judge not” is not the abolition of moral judgment. It is the **baptism of moral judgment in mercy**.

- **Moral indifference** says: “Nothing is wrong.”
- **The appetite to condemn** says: “You are wrong, and I will destroy you.”
- **Christian discernment** says: “This is wrong, and I will grieve, tell you the truth, call you to repentance, and forgive you — because I am a forgiven sinner too.”

That is the narrow way Luke 6:37 calls us to: **clear eyes about sin, and a merciful heart toward sinners.**
