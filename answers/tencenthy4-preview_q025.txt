The short answer: **the danger is not that the good deed is visible, but that the doer makes visibility the point.** The KJV wording already hints at this:

> “…do not your alms **before men**, **to be seen of them**…”

“Before men” tells us *where* the act happens; “to be seen of them” tells us *why* it happens. In the Greek, that second phrase is a purpose clause:  
**πρὸς τὸ θεαθῆναι αὐτοῖς** — “in order to be gazed at by them.” The verb carries an almost theatrical sense: to be made a spectacle. So Jesus is forbidding the practice of doing righteousness **for the audience**.

*(A small textual note: the KJV’s “alms” follows a Greek reading, “ἐλεημοσύνην”; many early manuscripts read “righteousness,” “δικαιοσύνην.” The literal scope is a bit broader in the older reading, but the moral principle is the same, and the verses that follow apply it to almsgiving, prayer, and fasting.)*

### Visibility itself is not the sin

Jesus does not ban public good works outright. In fact, earlier in the same sermon He commands them:

> “Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father which is in heaven” (Matt. 5:16).

There, visibility is good—because the purpose is God’s glory, not the doer’s.

Nor do the commands about secrecy in 6:3–4, 6, 17–18 require that no human being ever know. “Let not thy left hand know what thy right hand doeth” is vivid, hyperbolic language against ostentation and self-congratulation. The early church took up public collections (Acts 4:34–37; 2 Cor. 8–9); the widow’s offering was seen by Jesus (Mark 12:41–44); and Jesus himself healed openly. The issue is not that people see the good, but that the heart performs for them.

### Why the craving to be seen is dangerous

When the aim is “to be seen of them,” several things go wrong:

1. **The end is disordered.**  
   The deed is no longer done *coram Deo*—before the face of God, out of love for Him and neighbor—but is curved back onto the self. The person helped becomes a prop for my image.

2. **It becomes hypocrisy.**  
   Jesus calls such people “hypocrites” (6:2, 5, 16). In Greek, *hypokritēs* means “actor.” Outwardly the work looks righteous; inwardly the heart is far from God (cf. Matt. 23:25–28).

3. **It exchanges God’s glory for human glory.**  
   This is the root idolatry: “They loved the praise of men more than the praise of God” (John 12:43; cf. John 5:44; Rom. 2:29). Reputation becomes the reward.

4. **It forfeits the Father’s reward.**  
   Jesus does not forbid reward as such; He forbids settling for the wrong payer. The hypocrites “have received their reward” in full—human applause (6:2, 5, 16). Therefore, “ye have no reward of your Father.” The act may still help the poor, but as an act of discipleship it stores up no treasure in heaven (6:19–21).

### So what makes public goodness dangerous?

Public goodness is morally dangerous **when the crowd’s gaze is the reason for the act**. It is dangerous because it launders pride in the currency of virtue: it looks like love, mercy, devotion, or generosity, while inwardly feeding vainglory, self-deception, and a slavery to human approval.

### The heart-test

The question is not first, “Did anyone see?” but:

- “Whose approval was I seeking?”
- “Would I have done this if no one ever knew?”
- “Does the visibility lead others to glorify God (Matt. 5:16), or to admire me?”

If the latter, the trumpet is sounding even if no literal trumpet is blown.

### Summary

The problem in Matthew 6:1 is **not visibility itself, but the craving to be seen**—doing righteousness *in order to be seen by men*. Visibility is the occasion; the desire for human glory is the sin. Jesus calls His disciples to do good before the Father “which seeth in secret,” content with His reward, and to let every good work—public or private—be aimed at God’s glory rather than their own.
