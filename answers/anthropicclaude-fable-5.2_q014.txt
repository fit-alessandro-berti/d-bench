**Both, but the text leans toward training.**

The logic runs from treasure to heart, not the other way. Jesus doesn't say "where your heart is, there you will put your treasure," which would be the purely diagnostic claim. He says the heart *will be* (future tense) where the treasure is. And the verse begins with "for": it gives the reason for the commands before it. Be careful where you store up treasure, *because* your heart will follow it there.

That is why the command is aimed at the treasure rather than the heart. You cannot directly will yourself to love something, but you can decide where to put your money, time, and effort. Jesus commands the thing within reach and promises the heart will follow. Luke's parallel (12:33–34) makes this concrete: "Sell that ye have, and give alms... for where your treasure is, there will your heart be also."

The revealing sense is still there, though as a consequence. If hearts follow treasure, then you can locate your heart by looking at what you guard, what you worry about, and what you could not bear to lose.

**How possession, attention, and love become tied together**

The surrounding passage traces a sequence:

1. **Treasure (vv. 19–21): possession.** What you store up, you must protect. Moth, rust, and thieves mean that earthly treasure demands vigilance. Investment creates concern.

2. **The eye (vv. 22–23): attention.** The next image is the eye as the lamp of the body. What you have invested in, you watch. A "single" eye is undivided attention, and an "evil eye" is an idiom for stinginess, so the eye saying is still about money.

3. **Two masters (v. 24): love.** "He will hate the one, and love the other... Ye cannot serve God and mammon." What began as a possession ends as a master. The language has moved from owning to serving.

So what you store, you watch; what you watch, you come to love; and what you love, you serve. The possessor ends up possessed.

**The heart inherits the fate of its treasure**

A heart lodged in perishable things becomes vulnerable to everything that threatens them. That is why the passage moves on to anxiety: "Therefore I say unto you, take no thought for your life..." (v. 25). Anxiety is what a heart feels when its treasure is exposed. Treasure in heaven is not just a better investment but a secure place for the heart to live.

Jesus is not saying "have no treasure." He assumes everyone treasures something and that every heart will end up somewhere. The only question is where, and he treats it as something you can act on.
