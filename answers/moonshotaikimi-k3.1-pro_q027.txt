Most precisely, the Pharisee is condemned for **self-trusting, self-exalting righteousness**, which expresses itself in comparison and contempt. Luke supplies the diagnosis before the parable and its principle afterward:

- It concerns people **“trusting in themselves that they were righteous”** and **“despising others”** (v. 9).
- It concludes: **“Every one that exalteth himself shall be abased; and he that humbleth himself shall be exalted”** (v. 14).

### What is—and is not—wrong with the Pharisee?

**Not false doctrine, at least not explicitly.** No particular heresy is identified. His prayer even begins by thanking God. If his practical self-reliance amounts to bad theology, that is fair to say, but Luke’s focus is on his stance before God, not on a doctrinal proposition.

**Not his virtues as such.** Nothing in the parable suggests that fasting, tithing, or avoiding extortion and adultery is bad—or even that he is lying about his conduct. That makes the warning sharper: genuinely good practices can become materials for self-justification.

**Pride is the root, but of a particular kind.** He does not merely feel pleased with himself. He treats his religious and moral record as sufficient grounds for confidence before God. He presents his righteousness, but no need; his prayer contains thanksgiving and self-description, yet no petition for mercy.

**Comparison is his instrument.** His measure is “other men,” especially “this publican.” Relative moral superiority becomes evidence of absolute spiritual security. The problem is not noticing differences between people; it is using those differences to establish his own righteousness.

**Contempt is the inevitable fruit.** He cannot merely be grateful without also degrading his neighbor. His neighbor’s sin becomes part of his own religious résumé.

**“Self-display” is only partly accurate.** Luke does not specifically say, as Matthew 6 does, that he is performing for spectators. He may be entirely sincere. More exactly, he turns virtue into **self-accrediting capital**: his goodness becomes the basis on which he trusts himself and disdains others.

### Why is the tax collector’s posture truer?

His distance, downcast eyes, and breast-beating are not techniques that earn justification. They outwardly express the same thing his prayer expresses inwardly: **he comes without a defense**.

He makes no comparison, offers no achievements, and does not even claim that his humility deserves reward. He simply says, in effect: *This is what I am—a sinner—and mercy must come from God.* His self-assessment is spiritually truer because he does not use another person’s worse reputation to escape the truth about himself.

“Justified” concerns God’s verdict—being acquitted or accepted by God—not a prize for having the most convincing posture. The tax collector is not justified because feeling wretched is meritorious, any more than the Pharisee would be justified for fasting. His hope is placed in **God’s mercy**, not in the quality of either his virtues or his emotions.

The parable therefore does not excuse the tax collector’s sins or teach that moral transformation is unimportant. It teaches that neither religious achievement nor religious humility can put God in one’s debt.

The final warning is subtle: **“God, I thank thee that I am not self-righteous like that Pharisee” would reproduce the Pharisee’s sin exactly.** One man comes to God with a case for himself; the other comes with a plea for mercy.
