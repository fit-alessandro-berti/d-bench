Light is hated because it makes visible what a person wants to keep hidden. In John’s Gospel, the light is not just information or moral insight; it comes into the world in Jesus. His presence makes it harder for people to tell themselves whatever story they need in order to leave their lives unchanged. Notice the order in verse 19: people love darkness *because* their deeds are evil. The passage is not mainly about people who cannot see. It is about people who have something to lose by seeing clearly.

Exposure threatens more than reputation. I may fear that others will discover what I have done, but I may also fear having to admit it to myself. In the dark, I can call resentment discernment, or cruelty honesty. Shame can make that concealment feel necessary: “What I did was wrong” becomes “If anyone sees what I did, that is all they will see in me.” When being known feels like being reduced to your worst deed, even someone who offers to help you face it can feel like an enemy.

But John does not end with exposure as humiliation. The contrast is not between people who have something to hide and people who have never done wrong. It is between those who hide and those who “doeth truth.” That phrase makes truth a way of living, not just a set of claims to believe. And the person who comes to the light does so not to advertise their virtue, but so that what they have done may be seen to be “wrought in God.” The light reveals not only what needs to be confessed, but also what God has been doing that the person cannot take credit for.

That is why the light can be both feared and welcomed. It takes away the shelter of a false account of ourselves, but it also offers the possibility of being known without having to keep that account intact.
