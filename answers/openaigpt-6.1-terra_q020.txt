Bitterness is described as a **root** because a root can be hidden while it grows, yet eventually produce visible fruit. The image suggests that spiritual danger may begin beneath the surface—in a grievance, a settled distrust, or a turning away from God’s grace—before it becomes apparent in words and actions.

The picture also suggests that the damage may be **delayed**. Something left unattended can deepen over time and later “spring up” as conflict, alienation, or conduct that disturbs the community. The warning is therefore to look diligently, not merely to react once the damage is obvious.

Finally, a root can affect more than the place where it begins. One person’s resentment can spread through accusation, distrust, and divided loyalties, so that “many” are harmed. The passage calls the whole community to pursue peace and holiness and to watch over one another.

The image also echoes **Deuteronomy 29:18**, where a “root” producing bitter fruit refers to turning away from God. So Hebrews’ warning is broader than personal resentment alone: it concerns a harmful departure from God’s grace that can take hold within a community. It does not mean that every hurt or expression of anger is sinful; it warns against what is allowed to grow into destructive estrangement.
