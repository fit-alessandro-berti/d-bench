His deepest problem is not simply anger or envy. Those are the surface symptoms. The root is a prideful misreading of sonship: he has turned obedience into a credential.

What he says is plainly felt and partly true: he has served, he has kept commandments, and he has not received the joy he wanted. But that wounded fairness is quickly organized into a ledger: service “should” produce recognition; the returning brother’s welcome is evidence of favoritism; the fatted calf is unearned surplus. So anger becomes an accusation, envy becomes an accounting, and pride becomes self-justification.

At the deepest level, the elder brother is uncomfortable being loved rather than being “paid” for love. He wants a father who rewards obedience, not a father who welcomes the one who failed. He cannot enter the house because the mercy shown to the younger brother undoes his identity. In that sense, the discomfort with mercy itself is the heart of the problem—expressed through pride in obedience.

So the short answer is: **anger and envy are symptoms; wounded fairness is the feeling; pride in obedience is the moral root; discomfort with mercy is the existential form.** He needs not a better accounting of service but a new identity: sonship as gift, not wage.
