## The Verb Settles It: This Is Formation, Not Just Diagnosis

The most common reading treats the aphorism as a diagnostic tool: *look at someone's bank statement and you'll see their heart*. That's true as far as it goes, but the Greek doesn't quite say that. The clause reads **ὅπου γάρ ἐστιν ὁ θησαυρός σου, ἐκεῖ ἔσται καὶ ἡ καρδία σου** — "for where your treasure is, *there your heart will also be*." The future tense (ἔσται, *estai*) points forward, not merely inward. If Jesus meant only "your treasure reveals where your heart already is," the present tense would have served: "there your heart *is*."

The future tense makes the saying a *consequence*, not just a description. And that matters because the verse is attached to an imperative: "Lay not up... but lay up." The command assumes you can choose where your treasure goes *before* your heart has fully followed. The aphorism then explains why that choice is so consequential: **the heart follows the treasure**. You cannot simply direct your heart by willpower; but you can direct your treasure, and the heart will trail after it like a compass needle settling on north.

So the answer is: it says both, but with the emphasis on *training*. The heart is revealed by treasure (you can read your own condition by auditing your investments), but the deeper claim is formative — treasure is a lever that moves the heart.

## The Chain: Possession → Attention → Love

Notice how the imagery of verses 19–20 makes the mechanism visible. Earthly treasure is described by its *liabilities*: moth, rust, thieves. What do these have in common? They demand **vigilance**. A hoard must be watched. The owner of treasure on earth is constitutionally anxious — checking for corrosion, guarding against burglary (the Greek for "break through," *diorussō*, literally means to dig through — an image of thieves tunneling through the mud-brick walls of a Palestinian house). Possession obligates attention. You cannot own something fragile without your mind returning to it.

And attention, sustained over time, ripens into affection. This is a psychological law that Jesus states as a spiritual one: **what you attend to, you come to love; what you love, you attend to still more.** The loop is self-reinforcing. Possession captures attention; attention deepens attachment; attachment drives further acquisition and further guarding. The heart (καρδία, *kardia* — in Hebraic thought the seat not of emotion primarily but of will, desire, and commitment) migrates toward whatever it must keep safe.

This is why the command is not "stop loving money" — an impossible direct order — but "relocate your treasure." Redirect the investment, and the heart is dragged along by the mechanics of attention. Heavenly treasure — generosity, mercy given, devotion to God, whatever is given away rather than kept — has the opposite profile: no moth, no rust, no thieves, and therefore *no anxiety required to guard it*. It frees attention rather than consuming it.

## The Context Confirms the Reading

The saying doesn't float alone. It sits at the exact center of a discourse about anxiety and mastery:

- **6:1–18**: Alms, prayer, fasting — practices that relocate treasure toward God.
- **6:19–21**: Treasure → heart.
- **6:22–23**: The eye as the lamp of the body — the "single" (ἁπλοῦς) eye versus the "evil" eye, in Jewish idiom generosity versus stinginess. Here it's explicitly about *how perception shapes the whole self*: if the light in you is darkness, how great the darkness.
- **6:24**: No one can serve two masters — you cannot serve God and mammon.
- **6:25–34**: "Do not be anxious" — the payoff of treasure rightly placed.

The structure moves inward: treasure determines the heart; the eye determines the body; the master determines the whole person. And the thread running through all of it is **attention**. Earthly treasure seizes attention and converts it into worry ("what shall we eat, what shall we drink"); heavenly treasure releases attention for the kingdom ("seek first the kingdom").

## The Practical Logic

The verse, read as formation rather than mere diagnosis, gives a strikingly practical spiritual strategy: you cannot command your heart, but you can command your money — and the heart will follow. Generosity is therefore not the fruit of a sanctified heart so much as one of its *causes*. Where you put your treasure is where you are teaching your heart to live.
