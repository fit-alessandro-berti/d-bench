In Galatians 6:1, Paul sets out a distinctively Christian moral posture for correcting another believer. It is not the posture of a judge, a therapist, or a self-appointed enforcer, but of a careful, humble, and loving fellow-sinner.

**The required moral posture is:**

- **Meekness / gentleness** – “in the spirit of meekness.” Paul does not say “with firmness” or “with authority” or “with righteous indignation.” He says *meekness*. In Greek, *prautēs* is the same quality Jesus says of Himself: “I am gentle and lowly in heart” (Matthew 11:29). It is strength under control, not weakness. It is the power to correct someone without crushing them.
- **Self-suspicion / self-watchfulness** – “considering thyself, lest thou also be tempted.” The one correcting must look at himself while he looks at the other. He must not see himself as standing safely apart from sin. Paul literally says, “Keep looking at yourself,” *skopōn*, like a sentinel watching his own heart. This temperament is vigilant, not arrogant.
- **Restoration as the goal** – “restore such an one.” The Greek word *katartizō* means to set a broken bone, to mend a net, to put back in order. The point is *healing and repair*, not retribution. The one in fault is not an enemy to be crushed, but a brother to be restored.
- **Familial solidarity** – “Brethren.” The faulted person is still family. The correction happens inside a family relation, not from an outside moral high ground.

**Why are restoration, meekness, and self-suspicion kept together?**

Paul puts them together because Christian correction is a spiritually dangerous act. If you separate them, one of two things goes wrong:

- **Restoration without meekness becomes harshness.** If the goal is to “fix” someone but the attitude is not gentle, the person will often be driven away, not healed. The sin may be addressed, but the brother may be lost.
- **Meekness without self-suspicion becomes false pity.** If you are gentle but think you are safe from sin, your gentleness can condescend from above. It can become the gentle touch of someone who thinks, “Poor sinner, I am not like you.”
- **Self-suspicion without restoration becomes fearful withdrawal.** If all you do is watch yourself, you may avoid correcting anyone because you are afraid of falling into temptation yourself. Paul doesn’t say, “Don’t correct; stay safe.” He says, “Correct, but do it with humility.”

So these three are woven together: restoration is the *goal*, meekness is the *manner*, and self-suspicion is the *protection*. The one correcting must remember that he is not unreachable by sin. His own sinful capacity is alive, and the very act of correcting someone can tempt him to pride, harshness, self-righteousness, or even the same sin in a new form. Therefore he must hold the broken brother in one hand and his own frailty in the other.

This is the moral posture of the gospel: we confront sin, but we do it as beggars telling other beggars where to find bread. We are not better than the fallen brother; we are only, by grace, more or less aware of our own need. That awareness is what makes correction redemptive instead of destructive.
