

The parable itself gives the controlling interpretation in Luke’s introduction and conclusion:

- **Introduction:** “He spoke this parable to those who **trusted in themselves that they were righteous** and **despised others**” — Luke 18:9  
- **Conclusion:** “Everyone who exalts himself will be humbled, but the one who humbles himself will be exalted” — Luke 18:14  

So the Pharisee is not condemned for one isolated vice in a simple list. Rather, **the root condemnation is self-righteous pride before God**, and that pride reveals itself in comparison, contempt, and the use of virtue as self-display. False doctrine may be implied, but it is not primarily a matter of wrong propositions; it is a false way of standing before God.

## 1. The Pharisee’s fundamental error: he trusted in himself

The text says Jesus addressed this parable to people “which trusted in themselves that they were righteous.” That is the central diagnosis.

The Pharisee’s problem is not first that he fasts or tithes. Fasting and tithing are not condemned. His problem is that he treats those acts as the basis of his standing before God. He comes into the temple not as a sinner seeking mercy, but as a righteous man presenting credentials.

His prayer is essentially:

> “God, I thank you that I am not like other people — and especially not like this tax collector. I fast. I tithe.”

But notice what is missing:

- He does not confess sin.
- He does not ask for mercy.
- He does not ask forgiveness.
- He does not ask God for help.
- He does not express love for his neighbor.
- He does not depend on God’s grace.

His religion is externally correct but spiritually hollow. He has moral discipline, but he lacks repentance. He has religious performance, but he lacks mercy.

So the decisive condemnation is this: **he makes himself the ground of his own righteousness.**

He thinks he stands before God as creditor, not debtor. He thinks God owes him recognition because of his religious achievements.

That is the heart of self-righteousness.

## 2. Pride is the root; comparison and contempt are the fruit

The Pharisee’s pride immediately expresses itself through comparison.

He says:

> “I am not as other men are, extortioners, unjust, adulterers, or even as this publican.”

He does not measure himself primarily by God’s holiness. He measures himself by other people. His righteousness is comparative. He is not saying, “God, have mercy on me in light of your holiness.” He is saying, “God, look how much better I am than them.”

This comparative righteousness leads directly to contempt.

Luke says the audience “despised others.” The Pharisee demonstrates this when he singles out the tax collector:

> “… or even as this publican.”

He does not merely thank God that he avoids evil; he thanks God that he is not like the man standing nearby. He uses the tax collector as a dark background against which to display his own light.

So comparison and contempt are not separate problems. They are symptoms of the deeper disease. The Pharisee has turned religion into a mirror. He looks at the tax collector and sees someone inferior. He looks at himself and sees someone superior. He looks at God and uses God as the audience for his self-congratulation.

## 3. He turns virtue into self-display

The Pharisee’s virtues may be real. There is no reason to think he is lying about fasting or tithing. In fact, that is part of the danger. His difficulty is not hypocrisy in the sense of pretending to do religious acts he does not do. His difficulty is that he uses genuine religious acts for a false purpose.

He says:

> “I fast twice in the week, I give tithes of all that I possess.”

Fasting and tithing are acts of devotion. But here they become acts of self-exaltation. He does not present them as gifts from grace; he presents them as achievements. He does not say, “God, you have helped me to obey.” He says, essentially, “God, look at what I have done.”

His virtue becomes a platform for pride.

This is the “use of virtue as self-display.” But it is more than display before other people. In this parable, the Pharisee seems to be alone with God, so his self-display is ultimately before God. He is performing righteousness in the divine courtroom. He is asking God to ratify his self-image.

Therefore, the Pharisee is condemned not because virtue is bad, but because he has made virtue into an idol of self. His obedience no longer directs him toward God in gratitude; it directs him toward himself in admiration.

## 4. Is false doctrine involved?

Yes, but not primarily as formal doctrinal error.

The Pharisee probably has correct theology in many outward respects. He believes in God. He prays. He thanks God. He values Torah. He practices fasting and tithing. He likely belongs to a movement known for strict obedience to God’s law.

But his practical theology is deeply false. He behaves as though:

- God owes him acceptance because of religious performance.
- Righteousness is earned by moral comparison.
- Other people can be despised because they lack his level of observance.
- He does not need mercy.
- He can stand before God on the basis of his own merit.

That is a false doctrine of justification in practice, even if it is not stated as a theological system.

The parable exposes the lie at the center of self-righteous religion: that a person can make himself acceptable to God by being better than others.

The Pharisee’s doctrine of God is also distorted. He treats God as a judge who grades comparative performance, rather than as the holy and merciful Lord who receives contrite sinners. He does not say, “God, be merciful to me.” He says, “God, I thank you that I am superior.”

So the condemnation includes a false theology, but the focus is on the posture produced by that theology: **self-exaltation before God and contempt toward neighbor.**

## 5. The tax collector’s posture is truer because it is honest

The tax collector stands in contrast at every point:

> “And the publican, standing afar off, would not lift up so much as his eyes unto heaven, but smote upon his breast, saying, God be merciful to me a sinner.”

His posture is spiritually truer because it corresponds to reality before God.

### He stands “afar off”

He does not come near with confidence based on merit. He recognizes his unworthiness. He does not presume on access. He knows that if he is to stand before God, it must be by mercy, not achievement.

This is not mere low self-esteem. It is theological honesty. He knows what he is.

### He will not lift his eyes to heaven

His eyes are downcast because he is ashamed. He is not putting on a humble pose. His shame is real. He feels the weight of his sin.

In the ancient world, lifting the eyes to heaven could be associated with confidence, prayer, and bold appeal to God. The tax collector lacks that confidence. He has no righteous record to present. He cannot look upward on the basis of works.

### He beats his breast

This is a gesture of grief, anguish, and repentance. He is not calmly evaluating his spiritual condition. He is broken over it. His sin is not a small defect to be managed; it is a wound to be healed.

### He says, “God be merciful to me a sinner”

The Greek phrase is striking: he asks God to be “merciful” or “propitiated” toward him. The word can carry the sense of “let your mercy cover me” or “be atoned toward me.” He is appealing not to his record but to God’s mercy.

He does not say, “God, thank you that I am not like others.”

He does not say, “God, I’ll do better.”

He does not say, “God, compare me favorably with the Pharisee.”

He says, “God, be merciful to me — the sinner.”

The phrase “a sinner” could almost be translated “the sinner.” He does not merely admit that he has committed sins. He identifies himself as a sinner before God. He takes full responsibility for his condition.

He makes no comparison with the Pharisee. He does not say he is worse than the Pharisee, though he may imply it. His only comparison is between himself and God. And before God, he knows he has no claim.

That is why his posture is spiritually truer. He sees himself rightly. He sees God rightly. He sees his need rightly.

## 6. The tax collector is justified because he receives mercy, not because humility earns it

Jesus says:

> “I tell you, this man went down to his house justified rather than the other.”

The tax collector does not go home justified because humility is a superior religious technique. Humility does not save him as a new kind of merit. If humility became a work, it would merely create another Pharisee.

He is justified because he finally approaches God in the only way sinners can truly approach God: by mercy.

The Pharisee asks for nothing because he thinks he deserves everything.  
The tax collector asks for mercy because he knows he deserves nothing.

The Pharisee exalts himself.  
The tax collector humbles himself.

The Pharisee makes himself righteous by comparison.  
The tax collector is made righteous by divine verdict.

Jesus’ conclusion makes this explicit:

> “Everyone who exalts himself will be humbled, but the one who humbles himself will be exalted.”

The Pharisee’s self-exaltation brings him down. The tax collector’s humility opens him to God’s justifying grace.

## 7. The deepest problem: he loves his own righteousness more than God

The Pharisee’s prayer sounds like gratitude, but it is not true gratitude. True gratitude to God produces humility. If the Pharisee really believed that God was the source of his righteousness, he would not use that righteousness to despise others. He would be grateful, not contemptuous.

Instead, he loves his own religious image more than he loves God or neighbor. He does not ask God to bless the tax collector. He does not intercede for him. He does not pity him. He uses him.

In this sense, the Pharisee breaks both great commandments in his prayer. He fails to love God with all his heart, because he makes God a witness to his own glory. He fails to love his neighbor, because he despises the man standing in the temple with him.

That is the moral depth of the parable. The Pharisee is not condemned merely for being proud in a vague sense. He is condemned because his religion has become an engine of self-exaltation and neighbor-despising.

## 8. So which is the exact condemnation?

The most precise answer is:

**The Pharisee is condemned for self-righteous pride before God, expressed through comparison, contempt, and the use of virtue as self-display.**

If we separate the terms:

- **False doctrine?** Yes, indirectly. He holds a false theology of merit and self-justification, though not necessarily as formal heresy.
- **Pride?** Yes. This is the root sin. He exalts himself before God.
- **Comparison?** Yes. This is the mechanism of his pride. He measures himself against others instead of standing before God.
- **Contempt?** Yes. This is the explicit moral fruit. He despises the tax collector and other sinners.
- **Use of virtue as self-display?** Yes. This is the form his pride takes. He turns fasting and tithing into credentials for self-exaltation.

But the controlling theme is Luke 18:14: **he exalted himself**.

He did not merely have a bad thought. He adopted a false stance before God. He came to pray, but his prayer was about himself, by himself, and for himself.

The tax collector, by contrast, came with empty hands. He had no religious résumé, no moral comparison, no claim to superiority. He had only sin and need. And that made him the true worshiper.

## Short answer

The Pharisee is condemned because he trusted in his own righteousness and despised others. His pride made him compare himself contemptuously with the tax collector and turn genuine virtue into self-exaltation. The tax collector’s posture is spiritually truer because he stands before God with honesty, shame, and total dependence on mercy. He does not justify himself; God justifies him.
