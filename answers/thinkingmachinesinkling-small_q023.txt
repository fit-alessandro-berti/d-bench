In Isaiah 55:6–7, the command to **forsake “his way”** and **“his thoughts”** is not redundant; it is deliberately comprehensive. Together they map repentance as an **interior-to-exterior conversion**, revealing that sin is never just a behavior—but a system of imagination and intention that produces behavior.

### 1. “Way” = Outward Conduct
“The way” (*derekh*) is the observable pattern of life—actions, associations, habits, and social morality. To forsake it means repentance must show up in tangible change: you do not merely feel sorry, you stop walking the path. Outward conduct must be abandoned because God is not only judge of private feeling but of public walking (cf. Isa. 55:7, “return unto the Lord”).

### 2. “Thoughts” = Inward Imagination
“The thoughts” (*machashavot*) are the hidden architecture of sin—the plans, reasoning, justifications, and imaginative frameworks that make the wicked path seem reasonable or necessary. The unrighteous man is told to forsake not only what he does but **what he tells himself about it**: the rationalizations, the envy, the self-exaltation that fuel the way. Repentance therefore reaches the imagination; it demands that the inner “map” be redrawn.

### Why Both?
Because **conduct flows from cognition**. You can change a behavior temporarily while still holding the corrupt logic that generated it; God calls for the root and the fruit to be abandoned together. The pairing implies:
- **Sin is total.** It infects both body and mind.
- **Repentance is total.** It is not cosmetic reform (changing the “way” while clinging to the “thoughts”) but a turning of the whole self—heart, mind, and life.

### The Implication for Mercy
The verse moves from command to promise: *“Let him return… and he will have mercy… for he will abundantly pardon.”* The possibility of mercy is grounded precisely in this **wholeness of turning**:
- Because God is “near” (v. 6), He does not require a distant, perfect self—only a sincere return.
- But He requires a **complete return**: the sinner must leave both the visible road and the invisible reasoning behind it.
- The result is not thin forgiveness but **abundant pardon** (*yarbeh*—to multiply, overflow). God can pardon abundantly because He is not merely excusing an external failure; He is receiving a person who has turned away from both the deed and its inner logic. Mercy is possible because repentance is honest and total—forsaking the “way” without the “thoughts” is partial; forsaking both opens the door to full restoration.

**In short:** Isaiah demands that repentance be **integrated**—external conduct and internal imagination must both be surrendered. The promise of mercy is then proportionate: God pardons deeply because He receives the whole sinner, turned from both action and the imagination that drove it.
