## Short answer

Both readings are available, but they aren't equally available *here*. The verse can function diagnostically — your ledger doesn't lie about you — yet in its actual position in the argument it is doing formative work: **treasure trains the heart.** The diagnostic sense is the presupposition; the formative sense is the point.

## Why the logic runs that direction

Notice the shape of the whole unit. Verses 19–20 are imperatives: *stop stockpiling here; start stockpiling there.* Verse 21 begins with γάρ, "for" — it is the **reason the command makes sense**. If v. 21 only meant "your valuables indicate where your affections already are," the command would spin in place: *relocate your treasure, because your treasure shows where your heart is.* That grounds nothing. It has traction only if the deposit is a lever on the heart: put it there, and the heart will come.

The decisive detail is that **the heart is never commanded.** Jesus does not say "love God" or "set your heart above." The heart appears only in the indicative and the future: *there your heart will be.* The only thing placed under the will is the transaction. This is an anatomy of moral leverage. Affection is not directly voluntary — you cannot will yourself to love something by fiat — but you can will a transfer of goods. So the imperative aims at the one point you can move, and the heart is named as consequence.

ἔσται, "will be," fits this: there is a lag between deposit and arrival. You can invest ahead of your desire. The disciple who feels nothing for God is told to give anyway, and the feeling is downstream. That lag is not hypocrisy; it is how formation works.

Honesty requires noting the counter-case: ἔσται can be a future of assertion ("there your heart will be *found* to be"), and proverbs often state correlations without direction — "where the carcass is, there the eagles gather" does not mean eagles cause carcasses. So the tense alone doesn't settle it. The imperative-plus-γάρ structure does most of the work.

## How possession becomes attention becomes love

Matthew supplies the chain himself, in order:

**Possession (19–21) → attention (22–23) → mastery and love (24) → anxiety (25–34).**

The hinge is *corruptibility*, and here is the passage's central irony. Moth, "eating" (βρῶσις), thieves — the reason earthly treasure is worthless is precisely the reason it captures the heart. **What can be lost must be watched.** Fragile treasure generates vigilance: you check it, guard it, insure it, lie awake over it. Secure treasure needs no attention; insecure treasure monopolizes it. So heaven's incorruptibility is not merely a better rate of return — it is the condition of undivided attention. The rot that makes hoarded wealth a bad investment is the same rot that makes it an efficient heart-capture device.

Then the eye. Immediately after treasure comes "the lamp of the body is the eye," and the contrast is ἁπλοῦς — single, undivided — against πονηρός, evil. In Hebrew idiom the "good eye" is the generous one and the "evil eye" the grudging (Prov 22:9; cf. Matt 20:15, "is your eye evil because I am good?"). The organ of *attention* and the organ of *generosity* are the same organ. What you look at lights your interior; a grudging look darkens the whole inner life, and "how great is that darkness" — a darkness that is not mere error but an incapacity to see worth at all.

Then the terminus: two masters, and the vocabulary finally becomes love and hate, cleaving and despising. Mammon is not neutral stuff; it is personified as a rival lord that claims service. So the chain closes: storing → watching → serving → loving. **Attention is the mechanism by which possession converts into love**, because what you attend to becomes real to you, and only what is real to you can be loved. Weil's line that attention is the rarest and purest form of generosity, and Murdoch's account of love as the difficult discovery that something other than oneself is real, are essentially commentary on this sequence. Augustine's *pondus meum amor meus* — my weight is my love, I am carried wherever I am carried — is almost a paraphrase of v. 21: treasure is where you have put your weight, and weight determines where you come to rest.

## How heavenly deposits are actually made

The context tells you, and it makes the formative reading nearly unavoidable. The immediately preceding section is almsgiving, prayer, fasting done in secret, with the Father who sees in secret as the one who repays. Elsewhere: "sell what you have and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven" (Matt 19:21); Sirach's "store up almsgiving in your treasury"; Tobit's "you will be laying up a good treasure for yourself."

So the deposit above is made by **divestment below.** You put the thing out of your hands and into God's keeping, and the heart follows the thing. That is not a metaphor for an inner attitude; it is a technique. You cannot argue yourself into loving the poor, but you can hand them money, and the affection migrates toward what you have invested in.

Note also that hoarding is itself a slow consumption. Grain withdrawn from use rots, garments get eaten, silver corrodes. The storing is the destroying. Which suggests the heart becomes like its treasure: guarded, corroded, watched, dark.

## What survives of the diagnostic reading

Everything, once the loop has run. Because the relation is circular — you treasure what you love, and you come to love what you treasure — an audit of your valuables remains the most reliable available report on your heart, more reliable than your professions. In a context (6:1–18) that has just exposed the treasure of reputation, that sting is intended. You may say you love God; check the ledger.

The two readings differ mainly in *tense and use*: looking backward, treasure reveals; looking forward, it forms. The passage's double edge is that the heart is **mobile** — hopeful, because you are not stuck with the affections you currently have; alarming, because the migration happens without your deciding it, and "will be" means you may arrive somewhere you never chose to go.
