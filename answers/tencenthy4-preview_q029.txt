Paul is describing a distinctly Christian **posture of fraternal, restorative humility**. The one who corrects is not to act like a judge pronouncing sentence, nor like a rival scoring a point, but like a spiritual physician or an older brother helping someone who has stumbled.

The moral posture can be summed up in three linked attitudes:

1. **Restoration as the goal**  
   The word “restore” (*katartizō*) means to mend, set right, or put back into proper order—like setting a dislocated joint or repairing a torn net. The person has been “overtaken in a fault” (*paraptōma*, a false step or trespass), suggesting he was caught by surprise or seized by weakness rather than necessarily acting with calculated malice. The aim is therefore **healing and reinstatement**, not humiliation or punishment.

2. **Meekness as the manner**  
   “In the spirit of meekness” (*en pneumati prautētos*) describes gentleness produced by the Spirit. Meekness is not weakness or softness about sin; it is strength held under God’s control. It excludes harshness, contempt, gossip, and the desire to dominate. The spiritual person speaks the truth, but speaks it with the gentleness that is the fruit of the Spirit (Gal. 5:23).

3. **Self-suspicion as the inner guard**  
   “Considering thyself, lest thou also be tempted” commands a continual self-examination. The corrector must keep an eye on himself while he is correcting, remembering that he is not beyond the reach of temptation. This is the biblical cure for spiritual pride.

---

## Why are these three kept together?

They protect one another, and each corrects a different corruption of discipline.

### 1. Restoration without meekness becomes cruelty
If I am eager only to “set the person straight,” I can wound rather than heal. Harsh correction may crush the brother, drive him away, or turn discipline into a power play. Meekness ensures that the treatment fits the goal: the brother is to be **mended**, not mangled.

### 2. Meekness without restoration becomes cowardice or indifference
Gentleness that never names the fault or seeks real change is not love; it is sentimentality or avoidance. True meekness cares enough to say, “This is sin, and it must be repaired.” Restoration supplies the backbone that keeps gentleness from becoming mere niceness.

### 3. Both without self-suspicion become pride and hypocrisy
This is the hinge of the verse. The most dangerous posture in correction is the Pharisee’s: “I thank God I am not like this man” (Luke 18:11). Paul cuts that off by making the corrector look in the mirror: **“you also may be tempted.”** Remembering my own vulnerability does two things:

* It produces empathy: I see myself in the offender’s place.
* It produces dependence: I do not correct in my own strength or righteousness, but as one beggar telling another where bread is found.

Self-suspicion does not paralyze correction; it **purifies** it. The one who knows he could fall is the only one safe to help someone stand.

---

## The context confirms the point

Just before this verse Paul warns against “vain glory,” provoking one another, and envying one another (Gal. 5:26). Just after it he says to “bear ye one another’s burdens, and so fulfil the law of Christ” (6:2), and then warns, “if a man think himself to be something, when he is nothing, he deceiveth himself” (6:3).

So the truly “spiritual” person is not the one with the sharpest eye for others’ faults, but the one who:

* names the fault honestly,
* seeks the brother’s healing,
* speaks with gentle, Spirit-wrought humility,
* and never forgets his own need of grace.

In short, Galatians 6:1 requires the posture of the cross: **truth about the sin, grace toward the sinner, and humility about oneself.** That is how restoration, meekness, and self-suspicion belong together, and it is what makes Christian correction an act of love rather than an act of pride.
