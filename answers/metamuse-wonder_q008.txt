John makes love for the visible brother the test because love for the invisible God is too easy to *claim* and too hard to *verify* — even to oneself.

### 1. The invisible God can be loved in imagination

A person can feel sincere religious devotion toward a God he has never seen: in worship, in doctrine, in private prayer, in emotion. That God makes no immediate, awkward demands. He does not interrupt, disagree, need money, need forgiveness, smell bad, vote differently, or wound our pride.

The brother does.

The brother "whom he hath seen" stands in front of us with concrete needs and concrete faults. He requires patience, generosity, mercy, time, and humility. So he reveals whether our "love" is a real disposition of the heart or merely a religious feeling we enjoy.

John's logic is a kind of exposure test: *If love does not survive contact with a real person, it was not love for the real God either.*

### 2. The brother bears the God we claim to love

Behind the argument is the biblical conviction that human beings are made in God's image, and, in 1 John's immediate context, fellow believers are children of the same Father. To despise the child while professing devotion to the Father is incoherent.

It is like claiming to love an artist while trampling his work, or claiming loyalty to a king while abusing his son.

More deeply, John has already said: "No man hath seen God at any time. If we love one another, God dwelleth in us" (4:12). God remains invisible, but He makes His presence known precisely in a community where His love becomes embodied. The neighbor is not a distraction from loving God; he is one of the chief places where love for God becomes real.

### 3. The self-deception exposed

The verse exposes a compartmentalized spirituality.

The man John describes has separated religion into two rooms:

- In one room: God, worship, belief, spiritual language — "I love God."
- In the other room: actual people, with resentment, contempt, avoidance, or indifference — "but I cannot stand him."

He assumes the first room proves his heart is right, even while the second room contradicts it.

That is why John calls him not merely inconsistent but "a liar." The lie is first of all to himself. He mistakes:

- **religious emotion** for love,
- **correct belief** for obedience,
- **private devotion** for divine charity,
- **love of an idealized God** for love of the true God.

He may even justify the hatred: *My brother is difficult, wrong, unworthy, hurtful; God is perfect, so of course I love God but not him.* John reverses it: the very difficulty of the brother is the point. Divine love, *agape*, is not admiration for the lovable. It is the self-giving love God showed in Christ toward the undeserving (4:9–11). If that love has truly been received, it begins to reproduce itself toward others. "If God so loved us, we ought also to love one another" (4:11).

So hatred of the brother does not merely show a failure in manners. In John's theology it shows that the claimed source — being "born of God" (4:7) — is absent. A spring cannot claim to be full while producing no water.

### 4. Not earning God's love, but proving its reality

John is not saying we purchase God's acceptance by being kind to people. He is giving a diagnostic.

Love for God is invisible. Love for neighbor is visible. Therefore the visible becomes the evidence of the invisible. As he says elsewhere: "Let us not love in word, neither in tongue; but in deed and in truth" (3:18).

The self-deception is exposed because the neighbor cannot be edited. We can imagine a God who always agrees with us and overlooks our contempt. But the brother stands before us as God's appointed reality-check.

In short: John treats the seen brother as the test because a love that never becomes embodied toward persons is not yet love of God — it is love of one's own religious self-image.
