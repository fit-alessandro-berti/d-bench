**The image is sharp because of its extreme, almost comic contrast and physical impossibility: a tiny speck (mote) vs. a massive timber (beam/plank) both lodged in an eye.** The beam would make seeing *anything* impossible, let alone performing delicate surgery on someone else’s eye. This visual hyperbole instantly exposes the absurdity of the hypocrite—someone whose own glaring fault blinds them while they fuss over a minor one in another. It is memorable, visceral, and hard to dodge.

The passage criticizes *hypocritical* judgment, not moral correction itself. It does not say “never notice or address a brother’s fault.” It says: first remove the beam from your own eye *so that you can see clearly* to help with the speck. The sequence is explicit—self-examination and repentance come first; only then is one in a position to offer correction without hypocrisy. The goal remains helping the other person (“cast out the mote”), but from a place of humility and clear sight rather than superiority. In short, it forbids the log-eyed critic, not the act of correction after the log is gone.

This is consistent with the surrounding Sermon on the Mount material on judgment (do not judge hypocritically or by a double standard) while still allowing discernment and fraternal correction once one’s own life has been examined.
