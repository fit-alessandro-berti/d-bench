Bitterness and forgiveness are placed together because they represent **opposing ways of carrying an injury into a relationship**: bitterness keeps the wrong active as a source of hostility; forgiveness interrupts that hostility with grace.

But Paul’s contrast is broader than a private feeling versus a private decision. The whole passage concerns how a community learns to live together.

### 1. Bitterness is a feeling that can become a settled posture

The word translated “bitterness” (*pikria*) evokes sharpness or acridity and, figuratively, bitter resentment. Here it is surrounded by wrath, anger, shouting (“clamour”), slander (“evil speaking”), and malice. That company suggests something more than the immediate pain of being wronged: **hurt becoming a durable disposition toward others**.

It is worth distinguishing those things. Earlier Paul says, “Be ye angry, and sin not” (4:26). He therefore does not simply identify every experience of anger with sin. Nor does this passage say that feeling wounded is itself bitterness.

The concern is what anger and injury become when they govern our speech, intentions, and treatment of people.

### 2. It is personal, but also socially contagious

The list moves among inward dispositions, outward speech, and ill will:

- **Bitterness, wrath, anger:** the hostile inner climate.
- **Clamour and evil speaking:** hostility expressed publicly.
- **Malice:** the disposition to harm or act with ill will.

These are not necessarily stages in a rigid sequence. Together, however, they describe how resentment can become a community’s atmosphere. One person’s grievance can shape conversations, recruit allies, damage reputations, and teach everyone to expect the worst of one another.

So “contagious” is an apt description of its effects, though not a technical definition of the word. The repeated **“one another”** in verse 32 makes the communal concern explicit.

### 3. Forgiveness is its opposite because it changes how we treat the offender

Paul does not merely say, “Stop being bitter.” He supplies a positive alternative:

> “Be ye kind one to another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another.”

Kindness replaces harmful conduct; tenderheartedness replaces hardness toward another’s vulnerability; forgiveness replaces the continuing use of a wrong as grounds for hostility.

The verb translated “forgiving” (*charizomai*) is related to the language of grace: forgiving is a gracious act, not simply the disappearance of an unpleasant emotion. That helps explain why forgiveness can begin even while grief, anger, or painful memories remain.

**Forgiveness is not pretending that nothing happened.** Nor does this verse by itself require immediate trust, restored access, or freedom from consequences. Those are different questions from whether resentment will rule one’s conduct.

### 4. God’s forgiveness provides the pattern

“Even as God for Christ’s sake hath forgiven you” makes divine grace the ground and model of this new life. Paul is not saying, “Become unhurt.” He is saying, in effect, **“Let the grace you have received shape the way you respond to those who hurt you.”**

So bitterness here is best understood as **resentment capable of hardening into a habit, a posture, and a shared culture**. Forgiveness stands against it because forgiveness refuses to let the wrong become the governing principle of life together.
