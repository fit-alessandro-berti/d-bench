Luke himself gives the diagnosis before the parable begins:

> “...certain which **trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and despised others**” (v. 9).

Those are the two intertwined problems: **self-trust** and **contempt**. Pride is the root; comparison is how it operates; virtue used as display is how it speaks; contempt for others is its inevitable fruit.

False doctrine plays a part, but not mainly at the level of his statements. The Pharisee is probably telling the truth: he may really fast, tithe, and avoid extortion, injustice, and adultery. Those are good things. His error is what he believes those things *do* — that they establish his righteousness before God and entitle him to be accepted.

### The Pharisee: gratitude that is really self-congratulation

He begins, “God, I thank thee,” but his prayer contains:

- **No confession**
- **No petition**
- **No need**
- **No mercy asked for, because none is thought necessary**

God becomes the audience for his résumé. Even his gratitude is comparative: “I thank thee, that I am **not as other men are**... or even as this publican.”

That means his identity requires someone beneath him. Righteousness, for him, is not primarily a right relation to God and neighbor; it is distance from worse people. If your goodness depends on being “not like him,” you must keep despising him in order to keep feeling righteous. Contempt is therefore not an accidental bad attitude added to his piety — it is built into the way he constructs his piety.

His virtues are real, but they have been converted into credentials. He does not bring them to God in thanksgiving and service; he brings them as evidence in his own defense.

### The tax collector: prayer without a résumé

The publican does the opposite at every point:

- He stands “afar off” — aware he cannot presume on nearness.
- He will not lift his eyes to heaven — shame rather than self-presentation.
- He beats his breast — grief located at the heart.
- He says only, “God be merciful to me a sinner.”

The Greek is even stronger: “to me, **the** sinner.” He is not comparing himself to the Pharisee. He is not saying, “At least I’m honest about it,” which would make humility itself into a new résumé. He makes no claim at all except his need.

“Be merciful” (*hilastheti*) also carries the language of atonement: *make propitiation for me*. He locates his hope entirely outside himself — in God’s merciful provision, not in his record, his remorse, or even his humility. His posture is spiritually truer because it matches reality: before God he is a recipient, not a creditor.

That is why Jesus’ verdict is so shocking: “This man went down to his house **justified** rather than the other.” The respectable man leaves with only the righteousness he brought in — his own. The disreputable man leaves with a righteousness given by God.

So the parable does not teach that discipline is bad and failure is good. Fasting and tithing are not condemned; **self-justification is**. The Pharisee is condemned not because he is virtuous, but because he uses virtue to avoid grace — and to look down on the very people grace would teach him to love.

The tax collector is not justified because self-abasement is a more effective performance. He is justified because, finally, his hands are empty — and therefore open.
