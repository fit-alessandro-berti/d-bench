**The clue is in the first thing he says.**

"Lo, these many years do I *serve* thee." The verb is δουλεύω — the labor of a slave. He has spent his life in his father's house, and in his own account of it he has been an employee. Everything else follows from that: the anger, the envy, the arithmetic, the pride in his clean record, the recoil from mercy. These are not five candidate problems. They are five symptoms of one condition — he does not know his father, and therefore does not know himself as a son.

Consider what this does to him. If your standing rests on performance, then it must be measured, and measurement requires comparison, and comparison means that another man's good fortune is subtracted from your own. So the fatted calf is not merely galling; it is *expropriation*. Notice how precisely he does the accounting: not a calf but "a kid" — he has been keeping a running tab on what he is owed, down to the livestock. Notice too that he says the prodigal devoured "*thy* living," even though the father had already divided the estate; he is aware that the celebration is being funded out of what remains, which is his. His grievance is not abstract. He can cost it out.

So, of the options: **envy and wounded fairness are the surface**, and they are logically necessary consequences of the slave-mind. **Pride in obedience** is nearer the center — "neither transgressed I at any time thy commandment" is a claim to a flawless record, and it stands in exact contrast to his brother's "I have sinned." One son comes home with a confession; the other comes in from the field with a résumé. But even pride is not quite the floor. **Discomfort with mercy** is closest of all, because mercy is what exposes him. If the father receives a son who has earned nothing, then earning was never the basis of anything, and the whole structure the elder brother has built himself out of — the years, the compliance, the unbroken commandment-keeping — turns out not to be the foundation of his sonship but a misunderstanding of it. Grace does not merely fail to reward him; it dissolves the currency he has been saving in. That is why he cannot bear it. Mercy to his brother is the annihilation of his own self-conception.

But it is worth being honest about the grievance, because Jesus is. The elder son *did* work. His labor really was unacknowledged; there was no party for the faithful years. The father does not contradict him. He does not say, "You are mistaken, I gave you plenty." He says something far more devastating and far kinder: "Son, thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine." *You could have had the kid at any hour of any of those many years.* The goat was never withheld; it was never asked for. A man holding out an invoice cannot receive a gift; his hand is full. The elder brother's misery was real, and it was entirely self-inflicted — not by wickedness but by a wrong reading of his father's character. He was starving in a house with the storerooms open.

And there is a detail easy to skip past. What did he want the kid *for*? "That I might make merry with my *friends*." Not with his father. The joy he imagined for himself was a joy elsewhere, in other company. His brother took his portion to a far country; he kept his body in the field and sent his heart. Both sons wanted the estate rather than the man who owned it. The difference is that one son's version of this looked like dissipation and the other's looked like virtue — which is why the second is the more dangerous, since it does not feel like sin. It feels like justice.

Then the pronouns. He says "this *thy* son"; he will not say *my brother*. The father immediately puts it back: "this *thy* brother." The refusal of brotherhood is simply the social shape of the refusal of sonship — if you will not be a son, you cannot have brothers, only rivals and standards. And the father's final word changes the category altogether: "he was dead, and is alive again; was lost, and is found." *It was meet* — it was *necessary*. You cannot run a fairness calculation on a resurrection. Merit is not the frame; life and death are. The elder brother is arguing about wages at a funeral that turned into a homecoming.

Where does it leave him? Outside. The younger son left and came in; the elder stayed and would not enter. He excludes himself from a feast to which he has been personally entreated by a father who came out to him in the dark, exactly as he had run down the road that morning. The parable does not tell us whether he went in — and that silence is aimed at the men in Luke 15:2 who murmured that this man receives sinners, and at anyone else who has served long and well and found the mercy of God faintly insulting. The door is open. The music is audible from the yard. Nothing prevents him except the ledger he is still holding.
