**Victory is refusing to let evil set the terms—and actively defeating it by good.**

Romans 12:20–21 (quoting Proverbs 25:21–22) defines victory in the last line: do not be overcome *by* evil; overcome evil *with* good. The preceding verses make the contrast sharp. Repaying evil for evil means evil has already won in you (12:17, 19). Kindness to an enemy—feeding the hungry, giving drink to the thirsty—is the opposite move. You stay free of the cycle, you do real good, and you leave final justice with God (“Vengeance is mine; I will repay”).

That is not weakness. Retaliation is often the easier, more instinctive response. Refusing it takes self-command, and doing concrete good to someone who has harmed you costs something. The passage treats this as strength under a different standard: you are not conquered by what was done to you, and you do not become an agent of the same evil.

It is also not best read as manipulation. “Heaping coals of fire” is a hard image (shame, burning conviction, or an ancient figure for remorse that can lead to repentance). Paul does not present it as a tactic for scoring a psychological win or making the other person suffer for your satisfaction. The command is to give actual food and drink; the outcome is tied to God’s repayment, not to your cleverness. The governing aim in the paragraph is peace where possible, honest dealing, and the defeat of evil by good—not the engineering of guilt. If someone used kindness only as a weapon to humiliate, they would have left the passage behind.

So the victory this text names is moral and practical: evil does not get the last word in your actions, and good is treated as the stronger power. It is demanding precisely because it is not passive and not vengeful.
