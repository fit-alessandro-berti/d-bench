Luke 6:37 is not saying that moral reality dissolves, that cruelty and kindness are equivalent, or that we should become morally indifferent. It is saying something much more specific and much more difficult: **do not let moral judgment become condemnation; do not turn discernment into a hunger for someone’s ruin.**

The difference is between **seeing rightly** and **judging in a condemning spirit**.

## 1. If moral judgments never mattered, Jesus’s words would collapse

Luke 6:37 is immediately embedded in a moral world:

> “Judge not… condemn not… forgive…”

Forgiveness would mean nothing if there were no wrongs to forgive. Mercy would be empty if there were no offenses to release. Discernment would be pointless if there were no true and false, righteous and unrighteous, fruit-bearing and fruitless.

Jesus is not teaching moral apathy. He himself calls for moral perception elsewhere:

> “Do not judge by appearances, but judge with right judgment.”  
> — John 7:24

> “You will know them by their fruits.”  
> — Matthew 7:16

> “Test the spirits.”  
> — 1 John 4:1

> “Abhor what is evil; hold fast to what is good.”  
> — Romans 12:9

So the command “Judge not” cannot mean “Do not distinguish good from evil.” It means: **do not usurp God’s final judgment; do not use moral knowledge as a weapon against your neighbor.**

## 2. “Judge not” forbids a posture, not all perception

The Greek word for “judge” here, *krinete*, is broad: it can mean to evaluate, decide, assess, or pass judgment. But in this context it is not merely saying, “Do not think.” It is warning against a certain kind of judging: **the self-righteous, punitive, final-verdict style of judgment.**

Jesus makes this clear by adding:

> “Condemn not.”

To condemn is not simply to identify evil. To condemn is to declare someone’s destruction, to seal them off from mercy, to treat them as if their guilt exhausts their identity. Condemnation says, “This person is finished.” Discernment says, “This act is wrong, but the person remains accountable to God and open to repentance.”

There is a difference between:

> “That was unjust.”  
and  
> “You are unjust, worthless, and beyond hope.”

The first may be moral judgment. The second moves toward condemnation.

## 3. Discernment sees the act; the appetite to condemns the person

Discernment is the capacity to perceive moral reality clearly: what is true, what is harmful, what is loving, what is deceptive, what needs correction, what needs protection.

The appetite to condemn is not merely sharp moral perception. It is a heart orientation. It is a desire to exercise judgment in a way that excludes, punishes, dominates, or feels morally superior. Discernment is concerned with truth and love. The appetite to condemn is concerned with guilt and power.

A simple contrast:

| Discernment | Appetite to condemn |
|---|---|
| “Something wrong has happened.” | “This person is irredeemable.” |
| “This act must be corrected.” | “This person must be destroyed.” |
| “How can I love and protect the neighbor?” | “How can I punish or exile them?” |
| “I see my own need for mercy too.” | “I see myself as already justified.” |
| “I name sin because I believe repentance is possible.” | “I name sin because I enjoy the verdict.” |
| “Leave final judgment to God.” | “I must pronounce and enforce finality.” |
| Aims at restoration. | Aims at ruin. |

That last line matters. Jesus’s command is not against moral seriousness; it is against moral seriousness without mercy.

## 4. The “appetite” is the key word

You said: “the appetite to condemn.” That is an excellent phrase.

An appetite is not just a thought. It is a hunger. It is a craving. One can have a hungry need to condemn. It can become a way to feel safe, superior, righteous, clean, or powerful. When someone has wronged us, the appetite to condemn may disguise itself as justice, but its root is often resentment: a desire to make the offender pay, to diminish them, to make them feel as small as they made us feel.

Discernment says: “That was evil.”  
The appetite to condemn says: “Let me taste their guilt; let me relish their disgrace; let me make their shame confirm my goodness.”

This is why Jesus adds the warning:

> “With the measure you use it will be measured back to you.”  
> — Luke 6:38

That means: **be careful what kind of judgment you practice, because you are also a judge and also guilty.** If you habitually deal in condemnation, you will likely receive condemnation rather than mercy. The measure you use for others becomes the atmosphere of your own soul.

## 5. Moral judgments matter, but human judgments are provisional

Luke 6:37 does not abolish moral judgment. It limits it.

We can judge that violence is evil. We can judge that theft is wrong. We can judge that abuse must stop. We can judge that injustice must be corrected. We can judge that someone’s behavior is dangerous and must be restrained. We can even judge that public officials, leaders, and institutions need moral accountability.

But we are not God. We do not see the whole heart. We do not see the full story. We do not know the final state of a soul. We do not possess the right to make our verdicts absolute, irrevocable, and unforgiving.

So the difference is this:

- **Moral judgment says:** “This is wrong.”
- **Condemnation says:** “You are damned.”
- **Discernment says:** “This sin must be named, repented of, and resisted.”
- **The appetite to condemn says:** “Let me use this sin to crush you.”
- **Forgiveness says:** “Even though you are guilty, you are not outside the possibility of mercy.”

That is why “forgive, and ye shall be forgiven” is not cheap sentimentality. Forgiveness does not mean pretending evil was good. It means releasing the debt from our own vengeful control, while still entrusting justice to God. Forgiveness opposes the appetite to condemn without abandoning the moral seriousness of judgment.

## 6. The context of Luke 6 proves this is not moral indifference

Jesus continues in Luke 6 by speaking of the speck and the log:

> “How can you say to your brother, ‘Brother, let me take out the speck that is in your eye,’ when you yourself do not see the log that is in your own eye?”  
> — Luke 6:42

Notice: Jesus does not say, “You cannot remove the speck.” He assumes the speck exists. He assumes there is something to see. The problem is not that the other person has a speck; the problem is that the judge is blind to his own condition.

So moral judgment is real. The issue is humility. We may see the speck, but we must first see the log. We may call evil evil, but we must not pretend we are exempt from the same need for mercy.

Then Jesus says:

> “For no good tree bears bad fruit, nor again does a bad tree bear good fruit.”  
> — Luke 6:43

That is moral discernment. He expects people to evaluate fruit: teachings, characters, habits, lives. The command “judge not” does not mean “do not evaluate fruit.” It means “do not evaluate fruit in order to condemn persons without mercy.”

## 7. Discernment is for love; condemnation abuses love’s language

The deepest distinction may be this:

**Discernment is an act of love. The appetite to condemns the person while claiming the authority of love.**

A physician identifies disease because healing is possible. A parent corrects a child because the child matters. A witness names injustice because the victim matters. A community disciplines wrongdoing because the common good matters. That is not condemnation in the forbidden sense; that is moral perception joined with love.

The appetite to condemn, by contrast, may still speak religious or moral language, but its energy is hostile. It wants someone cut off. It wants someone shamed. It wants someone proven worthless. It may even say, “I am doing this for truth,” while secretly feeding on another’s guilt.

That is why Jesus connects judgment with forgiveness:

> “Forgive, and ye shall be forgiven.”

Forgiveness reveals whether our moral judgment was ever truly seeking the good of the neighbor. If we cannot forgive, if we cannot release the debt, if we cannot hope for repentance, if we cannot apply the same mercy to ourselves, then our “moral judgment” was perhaps never love at all.

## 8. So what does Luke 6:37 forbid?

It forbids several things:

### a. Usurping God’s role
We are not the final Judge. We are finite, implicated, and dependent on mercy ourselves.

### b. Declaring someone irredeemable
No person may be reduced to their worst sin. The thief on the cross repents. Saul becomes Paul. The Prodigal returns. To condemn someone absolutely is to deny the possibility of repentance and grace.

### c. Using moral knowledge as contempt
Knowing what is wrong is not a license to look down on the wrongdoer. Discernment should produce grief, responsibility, correction, and mercy—not disgust and domination.

### d. Pretending our judgments are innocent
If we condemn others, we become guilty of the same unforgiving spirit that destroys us.

### e. Refusing the measure of mercy
If we judge without forgiveness, we invite judgment without forgiveness.

## 9. So the difference is not “no moral judgment,” but “no condemning judgment”

Luke 6:37 is not saying:

- Moral distinctions are meaningless.
- Justice is unnecessary.
- Sin does not matter.
- Leaders need not be evaluated.
- Abuse is not real.
- Evil need not be named.
- Forgiveness is just pretending someone did nothing wrong.

It is saying:

- Do not make your moral judgments into weapons of contempt.
- Do not pronounce final verdicts on persons.
- Do not enjoy guilt as a way to feel superior.
- Do not close off the possibility of repentance and mercy.
- Do not forget your own need for forgiveness.
- Let discernment be joined to humility, justice, and love.

The moral judgment matters. The heart with which you judge matters even more.

## 10. The practical test

You can ask:

**Am I discerning, or am I condemning?**

- Does my moral assessment aim at truth and love?
- Does it protect the vulnerable without dehumanizing the offender?
- Can I still pray for the person I am judging?
- Am I willing to see my own faults by the same standard?
- Do I leave room for repentance?
- Do I desire restoration more than punishment?
- Does this judgment soften me toward God, or make me proud?
- Does it make me more merciful, or more merciless?

If moral judgment leads to greater love, repentance, humility, protection, and mercy, it is likely discernment. If it leads to satisfaction in someone’s disgrace, certainty in your own innocence, and hopelessness about the other person, it is likely the appetite to condemn.

## In short

Luke 6:37 does not erase moral judgment. It redeems it.

It says: **Do not treat moral insight as a right to destroy people. Do not confuse perceiving evil with becoming an executioner of souls. Name wrong, seek justice, correct error, protect the innocent—but do not condemn. Forgive. Leave final judgment to God.**

Discernment says: “I see sin, and I love the sinner enough to hope for change.”  
The appetite to condemn says: “I see sin, and I hope they are finished.”
