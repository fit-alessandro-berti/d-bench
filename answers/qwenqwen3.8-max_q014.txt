Short answer: **it says something more than “treasure reveals the heart.” It says treasure *locates* the heart — and therefore both reveals and trains it.**

The key sentence is:

> “For where your treasure is, there will your heart be also.”

Notice the direction. Jesus does **not** say, “Where your heart is, there your treasure will be.” He reverses it. The heart follows the treasure. That reversal is important.

So the text is not merely diagnostic — “your treasure shows what you love.” It is also formative — “what you treat as treasure will shape what you love.” Treasure functions both as a **mirror** and as a **school**.

---

## 1. Treasure reveals the heart

Treasure reveals the heart because what we store, protect, accumulate, and invest shows us what we value.

If you want to know where someone’s heart is, look at their treasure. Not merely their money, but what they spend themselves on: time, energy, imagination, security, status, pleasure, future planning, reputation, control, comfort, family, power, religious performance, approval.

Treasure is a practical confession. It says, “This matters. This is worth securing. This is what I am living for.”

So in that sense, treasure reveals the heart. It exposes the soul’s priorities.

But the verse goes deeper.

---

## 2. Treasure also trains the heart

The passage is not only describing the heart; it is commanding action:

> “Lay not up for yourselves treasures upon earth…”  
> “But lay up for yourselves treasures in heaven…”

That command assumes that our choices about treasure affect our hearts. If treasure only revealed the heart, the command would be strange. Why tell people to store up treasure in heaven if it makes no difference to the heart?

But Jesus says: put your treasure here, and your heart will go there. Put it there, and your heart will go there.

That means treasure has a formative power. It trains affection. It shapes desire. It disciplines love.

What we repeatedly invest in, we become attached to. What we secure, we trust. What we guard, we love. What we imagine as our future good, our heart moves toward.

So treasure is not only evidence of the heart; it is a means by which the heart is educated.

---

## 3. The deeper logic: the heart follows value

In biblical language, the “heart” is not merely emotion. It is the center of the person: thought, desire, intention, loyalty, affection, will. It is where love resides. It is the seat of allegiance.

“Treasure” is whatever a person treats as durable wealth, ultimate security, or highest good.

So when Jesus says, “Where your treasure is, there your heart will be also,” he is saying:

> Your heart will attach itself to whatever you treat as your lasting good.

Treasure creates gravity. The heart is drawn toward it.

This is why the passage is so serious. It is not mainly about money, though money is one of the clearest forms of treasure. It is about what the soul is anchored to.

Earthly treasure says:  
“Security is here. Status is here. Comfort is here. Power is here. Approval is here. Future control is here.”

And the heart responds:  
“Then I will cling to this. I will fear losing this. I will serve this. I will love this.”

Heavenly treasure says:  
“God is my good. His approval is my reward. His kingdom is my future. Love, mercy, righteousness, prayer, generosity, faithfulness — these are what last.”

And the heart learns to love what God loves.

---

## 4. Possession, attention, and love are tied together

The passage connects possession, attention, and love through the word “where.”

### Possession: treasure is what you store

“Lay up” means to accumulate, store, invest, secure. It is not merely owning things. It is making provision for yourself in what you regard as lasting.

The problem is not simply having possessions. The problem is making possessions the place where the soul seeks life.

Jesus warns that earthly treasure is vulnerable:

> “where moth and rust doth corrupt, and where thieves break through and steal”

Earthly treasure is perishable. It can decay. It can be taken. It can fail. It cannot ultimately protect the heart.

That matters spiritually because whatever you tie your heart to becomes the source of your peace — or your anxiety.

If your treasure is fragile, your heart becomes fragile.  
If your treasure can be stolen, your heart becomes anxious.  
If your treasure decays, your hope decays with it.  
If your treasure depends on human approval, your heart becomes enslaved to opinion.  
If your treasure is wealth, your heart becomes dependent on wealth.

So possession is not neutral. Possession becomes a spiritual school. It teaches the heart what to trust.

---

### Attention: treasure fixes the gaze

The repeated word “where” is crucial.

> “Where your treasure is…”  
> “there will your heart be also.”

Treasure gives the heart a location. It directs attention.

Whatever we treasure, we watch. We calculate it. We protect it. We imagine it. We return to it mentally. We plan around it. We worry about it. We take pleasure in it.

Treasure creates a map of attention.

If treasure is on earth, the heart’s attention becomes earthly:  
acquisition, preservation, comparison, fear of loss, desire for more, reputation, control, comfort.

If treasure is in heaven, the heart’s attention becomes heavenly:  
God, righteousness, mercy, prayer, generosity, humility, truth, love, eternal reward.

This is why the following verses about the eye fit so well:

> “The light of the body is the eye…”

The eye is the organ of attention. What we look at fills us. Treasure draws the eye, and the eye shapes the whole person.

So attention is the bridge between treasure and love. What we treasure captures our attention; sustained attention forms affection.

---

### Love: treasure becomes the heart’s beloved

Love is where possession and attention become personal allegiance.

The heart does not simply observe treasure. It clings to it. It delights in it. It trusts it. It serves it.

That is why Jesus later says:

> “No man can serve two masters…”  
> “Ye cannot serve God and mammon.”

Treasure can become a master. Wealth can become a rival god. Not because money is evil in itself, but because the heart turns it into an object of trust, devotion, and fear.

Love, in this passage, is not mainly sentimental affection. It is loyalty. It is devotion. It is the orientation of the whole self.

So when Jesus says, “there will your heart be also,” he means:

> Your love will be there.  
> Your trust will be there.  
> Your desire will be there.  
> Your imagination will be there.  
> Your allegiance will be there.  
> Your anxiety will be there.  
> Your hope will be there.

Treasure does not merely occupy space. It occupies the heart.

---

## 5. The cycle: love chooses treasure, and treasure trains love

There is a cycle here.

The heart first chooses what it values. It says, “This is worth having. This is worth securing.” Then it stores treasure accordingly.

But once that treasure is stored, it begins to act on the heart. It draws attention. It demands protection. It invites imagination. It cultivates dependence. It forms affection.

So the movement is:

**Desire chooses treasure → treasure captures attention → attention shapes love → love deepens desire.**

That is why Jesus gives a command. He is telling us to interrupt the wrong cycle and begin a right one.

Do not store treasures on earth, because that will train your heart to love perishable things.

Store treasures in heaven, because that will train your heart to love God and what lasts.

---

## 6. Heavenly treasure is not merely future reward

“Treasure in heaven” should not be reduced to a crude idea of earning points for the afterlife. In Matthew’s Gospel, heavenly treasure is connected with life under God’s rule: generosity, righteousness, prayer, mercy, dependence on the Father, seeking the kingdom.

Earlier in Matthew 6, Jesus speaks of almsgiving, prayer, and fasting done before the Father rather than for human approval. Those are treasures with the Father. Later, Jesus tells the rich young man:

> “If thou wilt be perfect, go and sell that thou hast, and give to the poor, and thou shalt have treasure in heaven…”

So heavenly treasure is often expressed through concrete love: giving to the poor, seeking righteousness, trusting God, serving others, living for God’s approval rather than human applause.

It is not that God is bribed by our actions. It is that these actions reorient the heart. They loosen the grip of mammon. They teach the soul to find its good in God.

---

## 7. So which is it: revelation or training?

Both — but with an important emphasis.

Treasure **reveals** the heart because your treasure shows what you currently value.

Treasure **trains** the heart because the verse says the heart *will be* where the treasure is. The heart follows.

If I may put it simply:

> Treasure tells you what you love, and treasure teaches you what to love.

Or even more sharply:

> Treasure is not only a symptom of the heart; it is a school for the heart.

The text does not present the heart as fixed and independent, simply displaying itself through external choices. Nor does it present treasure as morally neutral. Jesus treats treasure as spiritually formative. What you store, you serve. What you serve, you love. What you love, you become attached to. And what you are attached to, your heart inhabits.

---

## Final summary

Matthew 6:19–21 ties possession, attention, and love together like this:

- **Possession** is what we store, secure, and invest in as our good.  
- **Attention** is drawn to that treasure; we watch, guard, imagine, and calculate it.  
- **Love** is formed as the heart attaches itself to that object of trust and desire.

Therefore, treasure is not merely external. It becomes the geography of the heart.

Earthly treasure pulls the heart toward what perishes, producing anxiety, greed, fear, and divided loyalty.

Heavenly treasure pulls the heart toward what endures, producing trust, generosity, love for God, and love for what God loves.

So the text says: **choose your treasure carefully, because your treasure is forming your heart.**
