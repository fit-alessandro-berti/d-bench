Galatians 6:1 — The Moral Posture of Correction

I. What the verse actually commands

Paul is addressing a situation the church constantly faces: a member has been "overtaken in a fault" — the Greek (prolambano / paraptoma) suggests someone surprised or caught off guard by sin, a stumble rather than settled defiance. The instruction is not "expose," "punish," or "expel," but "restore" (katartizo) — a word used elsewhere for mending nets and setting broken bones. The goal of correction is repair: the person is to be returned to wholeness and to fellowship, the way a dislocated joint is set back in place — firmly, but with care, because the thing being handled is injured and can be injured further.

Three elements are bound together in the verse: (1) restoration as the aim, (2) meekness as the manner, and (3) self-suspicion ("considering thyself, lest thou also be tempted") as the inner condition of the corrector. The question is why Paul refuses to let these come apart.

II. The moral posture required

The posture is that of a fellow patient, not a judge; a physician who knows he carries the same disease. Several features define it:

1. Correction as an act of service to the fallen, not a performance of the corrector's virtue. Paul assigns the task to "ye which are spiritual" — but the mark of being spiritual, in the immediate context of Galatians 5, is the fruit of the Spirit: love, gentleness, meekness, self-control. So the verse contains a built-in test: the person qualified to correct is precisely the person who will do it gently. Anyone eager to rebuke harshly has, by that very eagerness, disqualified himself. The desire to correct without meekness is itself evidence of the flesh — pride, rivalry, "vainglory" (5:26, the verse immediately preceding).

2. Meekness as strength under restraint, not weakness. Meekness (prautes) is not timidity or moral indifference. The verse presupposes that the fault is real and must be named — restoration is impossible without truth. Meekness governs how the truth is carried: without contempt, without relish, without using the other's sin as a pedestal. It is the temper of someone who takes sin seriously but takes no pleasure in having found it in someone else.

3. Self-suspicion as the safeguard of the whole operation. "Considering thyself, lest thou also be tempted" — note the shift from plural ("ye which are spiritual") to singular ("thyself"). Paul makes it personal and inescapable: each corrector, individually, must keep one eye on his own heart while dealing with another's failure. The corrector stands under the same law of moral gravity as the corrected. Today's restorer may be tomorrow's stumbler.

III. Why the three must be kept together

Because each element, isolated from the others, becomes corrupt:

Restoration without meekness becomes coercion. If I aim at fixing you but approach you with harshness, I will not restore you; I will humiliate you, drive you into defensiveness or despair, and likely sever you from the community rather than mend you into it. Broken bones are not set with hammers.

Meekness without restoration becomes indulgence. If I am gentle but have abandoned the goal of restoring you to what is right, my gentleness is mere niceness — a refusal to love you enough to tell the truth. The verse does not permit tolerance-as-avoidance; it demands the harder work of gentle truth-telling.

Restoration and meekness without self-suspicion become condescension. This is the subtlest failure. I may aim at your good and speak softly, yet inwardly stand above you — the healthy tending the sick, the clean handling the dirty. Paul's "lest thou also be tempted" destroys that altitude. It names two dangers at once: first, that I am capable of the very sin I am correcting; second, that the act of correction carries its own temptations — self-righteousness, gossip, the pleasure of another's fall (what we would now call schadenfreude), spiritual pride. The moment I correct you while forgetting my own corruptibility, correction itself becomes my sin. The physician catches the patient's disease precisely through the treatment.

Self-suspicion, in turn, is what generates and sustains the meekness. Gentleness that is merely a technique or a tone can be faked; gentleness that flows from the honest recognition "this could be me — this may yet be me" cannot help being real. And it is what keeps restoration as the goal: the one who knows he may soon need restoring himself will treat the fallen as he hopes to be treated. The verse is, in effect, the golden rule applied to moral failure.

IV. The deeper logic

Underneath the verse lies a picture of moral community in which no one occupies a permanently elevated position. Sin is not the property of a class of bad people to be managed by a class of good people; it is a common vulnerability. "Spirituality" in Paul's sense confers responsibility, not immunity. Hence the next verse: "Bear ye one another's burdens, and so fulfil the law of Christ" (6:2). Correction is a species of burden-bearing — I take up the weight of your failure alongside you — and it is immediately followed by the warning against self-deception: "if a man think himself to be something, when he is nothing, he deceiveth himself" (6:3).

The moral posture required, then, can be summarized as: truth in the hands of humility, aimed at healing. Firm about the fault, tender toward the person, honest about oneself. Restoration supplies the purpose, meekness the manner, and self-suspicion the protection — and only together do they make correction an act of love rather than an act of power.
