James is exposing a double-edged loyalty in the same person. The one mouth “therewith” blesses God the Father and curses men “made after the similitude of God.” That is, a speaker can direct upward reverence toward the divine source while simultaneously denying the image of God in ordinary people. The contradiction is not merely that the words sound out of order; it is that the agent’s moral hierarchy is out of order. God is honored, but the creatures made like God are treated as expendable, inferior, or beneath respect. The mouth that proclaims the incomparable God therefore becomes the instrument of a double stance: awe for the archetype and contempt for the image.

The “same mouth” matters. This is not an accident of pronunciation or a bad slip of the tongue. It is the same person, in the same act, at the same time. The blessing and cursing are not separated by time or by two different motives; they issue together, so the inconsistency cannot be quarantined as a private embarrassment. James’s point is that the tongue is where the interior disposition becomes audible and visible. In a sense, it is the first thing that leaks.

That is why speech reveals moral disorder quickly. Speech is a low-threshold channel between heart and world: a small act with large evaluative force. It is also socially constitutive; to bless someone is to include them, to honor their likeness, to say they are worth the grace of welcome. To curse them is to mark them as outside the circle of worth. One indivisible gesture therefore discloses whether the person’s desire for God has been translated into respect for creatures created in God’s likeness. The tongue also cannot hide this clearly because it is public, immediate, and hard to disown. In James’s anthropology, the tongue is both a small member and a fire that touches the whole person—so it behaves less like an isolated organ and more like an index card of the soul.

So the passage does not say that speech is the cause of the disorder; it says that speech is the point where the disorder becomes unmistakable. The contradiction appears as soon as the person speaks, before self-justification can catch up, because a single utterance is already an act of ranking—who is worthy of grace, and who is not. In James’s terms, the tongue is not merely a channel for expressing an already settled self; it is the first place that self becomes audible, shameful, and therefore correctable.

In short, the inner contradiction is this: the speaker names God as the one worthy of praise while refusing to recognize human beings as God’s image in real life. Speech makes that contradiction visible quickly because it turns inward loyalty into an outer moral verdict, in a single, hard-to-spin moment.
