In Isaiah 55:6–7, the pairing of **“way”** (*derek*) and **“thoughts”** (*maḥăšāḇôt*) uses Hebrew poetic parallelism to describe repentance as an act of the whole person. Rather than treating behavior and inner life as separate compartments, the prophet links them together—and immediately connects them to verses 8–9 (*“For my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways”*). 

This pairing carries three major implications for outward conduct, inward imagination, and the nature of divine mercy.

---

### 1. Outward Conduct: Repentance Must Be Tangible (“His Way”)
In biblical Hebrew, “way” refers to a literal road, and metaphorically to a person’s habitual pattern of life, ethics, and choices. 
* **More than regret:** Calling the wicked to forsake their “way” implies that repentance cannot remain purely emotional or theoretical. Feeling bad about wrongdoing without altering one’s trajectory is not true *šûḇ* (“returning”).
* **Structural change:** Because a “way” is an established path, forsaking it means breaking with ingrained habits, unjust practices, and destructive loyalties. Outward conduct matters because internal beliefs inevitably take physical, social shape.

### 2. Inward Imagination: Repentance Must Reach the Root (“His Thoughts”)
“Thoughts” here does not just mean passing ideas; it denotes plans, schemes, values, and the underlying logic by which a person interprets reality.
* **More than outward compliance:** Just as inward sorrow without outward change is incomplete, outward conformity without inward renewal is mere performance. A person can stop committing an external act while still clinging to the pride, resentment, or self-serving imagination that produced it.
* **Reshaping desires and assumptions:** To forsake one’s “thoughts” is to surrender the mental frameworks—cynicism, self-justification, idolatrous trust in human power—that make the wrong “way” seem reasonable in the first place. 

### 3. The Possibility of Mercy: Clearing the Way for God’s Abundance
Crucially, Isaiah does not present forsaking one’s ways and thoughts as a grueling prerequisite by which humans *earn* God’s favor. Instead, the structure of the passage reveals a profound link between repentance and mercy:

* **Mercy is already near:** Verse 6 urges listeners to seek the Lord *“while he may be found… while he is near.”* God’s willingness to forgive precedes human action; repentance is turning around to receive what is already being offered.
* **Abundant pardon exceeds human logic:** The text promises not bare tolerance, but that God will *“abundantly pardon”* (literally, *multiply to forgive*). Immediately afterward, verses 8–9 explain *why* this is believable: God’s thoughts and ways are as high above human thoughts and ways as the heavens are above the earth.
* **Forsaking human despair:** In human reckoning, deep guilt often seems unforgivable, or forgiveness seems purely transactional. Therefore, one of the very “thoughts” the unrighteous must forsake is the projection of human stinginess or vengeance onto God. Turning from one’s own limited imagination makes room to trust a mercy that operates on a radically higher plane.

### Summary
Repentance in Isaiah 55:6–7 requires both **“way”** and **“thoughts”** because human brokenness is both behavioral and imaginative. Outward conduct must change so repentance is real; inward imagination must change so repentance is sincere. Ultimately, letting go of both clears away self-reliance and cynical assumptions, opening a person to a God whose capacity to pardon far surpasses human calculation.
