What makes this image so sharp is that it is deliberately, almost comically impossible.

The Greek word translated “mote” is *karphos*—a tiny splinter, a speck of straw or sawdust. The word translated “beam” is *dokos*—a heavy timber, a log, a construction beam. Jesus is not making a subtle comparison. He is asking you to picture a man with a wooden plank sticking out of his eye, solemnly telling his brother, “Hold still, let me get that little speck out of your eye.”

The absurdity is the point. It exposes how self-deception works: our own faults are huge, but we don’t notice them; someone else’s faults are tiny, and we see them with perfect, obsessive clarity. The image is unforgettable because it is so disproportionate. It makes hypocrisy look ridiculous, not just wrong.

But the passage does not forbid moral correction. In fact, it assumes correction is meant to happen.

Look at the final sentence:

> “Thou hypocrite, first cast out the beam out of thine own eye; and then shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote out of thy brother’s eye.”

The word “then” is crucial. Jesus does not say, “Never correct your brother.” He says, “First remove your own beam, and then you can see clearly to remove your brother’s speck.”

So the problem is not correction itself. The problem is correction done by a hypocrite—someone who sees another’s flaw but refuses to see his own. Jesus harshly calls such a person a hypocrite, from the Greek word for an actor wearing a mask. It’s an unmasking. But after the unmasking, there is a restoration: once you have dealt with your own sin, you are actually able to help your brother, because your vision has been cleared.

This is the sharpness of the passage: it combines brutal honesty with a hopeful purpose. It refuses to let anyone use self-righteousness as a tool of judgment. But it also refuses to let anyone use humility as an excuse for never speaking a truthful word to a brother. The order is everything: first the beam, then the mote. First self-examination, then sibling help. First the inward work, then the outward correction.
