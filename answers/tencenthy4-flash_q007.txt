The short answer is that Jesus is not saying, “Moral categories don’t exist” or “Nothing is really right or wrong.” He is saying, “Don’t take God’s place as the final, punitive Judge of another person.” The verse is a command about **posture and heart**, not a suspension of **moral reasoning**.

To understand the difference, it helps to look at the immediate context, the broader teaching of Scripture, and the contrast between two very different ways of "seeing" sin: **discernment** and the **appetite to condemn**.

---

## 1. The Context: Mercy, Not Moral Relativism

Luke 6:37 sits in the middle of Jesus’ "Sermon on the Plain" (Luke 6:17–49), a passage saturated with radical mercy:
*   Love your enemies (6:27)
*   Give to everyone who asks (6:30)
*   Be merciful, just as your Father is merciful (6:36)

When Jesus says, "Judge not... condemn not... forgive," he is describing the **texture of Kingdom life**. The people of God are to be characterized by a forgiving, merciful spirit because that is how God has treated them. 

If Jesus meant "moral distinctions never matter," the rest of his teaching would make no sense. In the very next chapter (Luke 7), he commends a centurion, praises John the Baptist, and confronts the Pharisees for rejecting God’s purpose. In John 7:24, he says plainly, **"Do not judge by appearances, but judge with right judgment."** He isn’t against judging; he is against *wrong* judging.

---

## 2. Discernment vs. The Appetite to Condemn

The difference lies in **what you are doing with your moral perception** and **why you are doing it.**

### Discernment (Seeing clearly, with humility)
Discernment is the God-given capacity to distinguish truth from error, fruit from bad fruit, and wisdom from folly. Jesus assumed his followers would exercise it:
*   *"Beware of false prophets... you will recognize them by their fruits"* (Matthew 7:15–20).
*   *"Test the spirits to see whether they are from God"* (1 John 4:1).
*   *"The spiritual person judges all things"* (1 Corinthians 2:15).

Discernment has several marks:
1.  **It begins with self-examination.** In Luke 6:41–42, Jesus says, "Why do you see the speck that is in your brother’s eye, but do not notice the log that is in your own eye?" He isn’t saying the speck isn’t there; he’s saying you can’t see clearly until you deal with your own sin first.
2.  **It aims at restoration.** When Paul tells the Corinthians to judge the sinner in their midst (1 Corinthians 5), the goal is "that his spirit may be saved." In Galatians 6:1, the command is to restore a fallen brother "gently, and watch yourself."
3.  **It is anchored in truth and love.** Ephesians 4:15 calls us to "speak the truth in love"—truth without love is brutality; love without truth is sentimentality.

### The Appetite to Condemn (Self-righteous verdict)
Condemnation is different. It is not just recognizing a fault; it is **pronouncing a final verdict** and adopting a posture of superiority. It is the "appetite" Jesus warns against.

This appetite looks like:
1.  **Self-exaltation.** The Pharisee in Luke 18:11–12 thanking God he was "not like other men." The act of judging becomes a way of proving our own goodness.
2.  **A desire to punish or exclude.** It is eager to write people off, to label them, and to see them suffer consequences. It delights in being "right" more than it delights in seeing a sinner restored.
3.  **Usurping God’s role.** Ultimately, condemnation forgets that God alone is the righteous Judge who sees the heart and knows the end from the beginning (Romans 12:19; 1 Corinthians 4:5). When we condemn, we act as if we have God’s exhaustive knowledge and God’s authority to punish.

Jesus reserves his harshest words for the religious leaders of his day precisely because they had perfected the art of moral labeling while neglecting "justice and mercy and faithfulness" (Matthew 23:23). They had a ravenous appetite to condemn, but no appetite to forgive.

---

## 3. Why Moral Judgments Still Matter

If we say "moral judgments never matter," we actually undermine the very ground on which Jesus stands. 

*   **Sin matters.** Jesus died because sin is real and destructive. If moral judgments didn't matter, the cross would be unnecessary.
*   **Holiness matters.** Jesus called people to "go and sin no more" (John 8:11). He didn't say, "Your sin doesn't matter, don't worry about it."
*   **Justice matters.** The Bible is full of God’s judgments against injustice. To say "judgments never matter" is to say that God’s own moral character doesn't matter.

The point is not that we should stop making moral evaluations; the point is that our evaluations must be governed by **mercy, humility, and a desire for redemption.**

---

## 4. The Link Between Forgiveness and Judgment

Notice the structure of Luke 6:37: 
> *Judge not... condemn not... **forgive**, and ye shall be forgiven.*

The antidote to condemnation isn't moral indifference; it is **forgiveness**. Forgiveness only makes sense if a wrong was actually committed. You can’t forgive someone for something that "doesn't matter." So Jesus is saying: *Yes, you will see sin. Yes, you will recognize wrong. But instead of holding it against them, extend the mercy God extended to you.*

James 2:13 puts it starkly: **"Mercy triumphs over judgment."** This doesn't mean judgment (in the sense of God's justice) doesn't exist; it means that in the life of the believer, mercy is the dominant, defining reality. We have been forgiven an unpayable debt, so we are freed from the need to collect pennies from others.

---

## Summary

Saying "moral judgments never matter" is a recipe for moral chaos and spiritual laziness. Jesus never taught that. 

What he taught was that **the act of judging others is a dangerous spiritual occupation** when it is divorced from self-awareness and mercy. 

*   **Discernment** says: "That is wrong, and I will speak the truth about it in love, hoping for your restoration, while remembering that I am a sinner too."
*   **Condemnation** says: "That is wrong, and it proves I am better than you. You deserve to be cut off."

The first reflects the heart of Christ, who came "not to condemn the world, but to save the world" (John 3:17)—even while he called sin what it was. The second reflects the heart of the Pharisee. 

The call of Luke 6:37 is to keep our moral eyes wide open, but our hearts soft and our hands open—leaving the final verdict to God, and committing ourselves to the hard, holy work of mercy.
