

In Matthew 12:34–35 Jesus connects speech with the “hidden moral stockpiles” of the heart by presenting the mouth as an outlet from the inner person.

The key images are:

- **heart**: not merely “feelings,” but the center of will, desire, moral identity, and character;
- **abundance**: what the heart is full of tends to overflow;
- **treasure**: stored-up moral resources—values, loves, fears, resentments, virtues, habits;
- **bringeth forth**: speech as the visible fruit or product of what is stored inside.

So the passage says that words are not just clever performances, random outbursts, or surface habits. They are morally loaded expressions that draw on what a person has accumulated in the heart.

The answer to the question is: **all three—words are symptoms, choices, and revelations—though with different emphases.**

### 1. Words as symptoms

In one sense, words function like diagnostic signs. Jesus does not say, “The mouth independently creates good or evil,” but rather:

> “out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaketh.”

The mouth speaks what the heart is full of. If a person’s speech is repeatedly bitter, defensive, self-exalting, fearful, slanderous, or cynical, that speech functions as a symptom of something stored deeper in the person. It reveals the kind of moral environment inside the heart.

This is why Jesus can call the Pharisees a “generation of vipers” and ask how those who are evil can speak good things. Their words, in context, are evidence of the character behind them. The poison is not merely in the words; the words show the poison.

But “symptom” should not be taken as meaning that words are completely involuntary. They are symptoms, not the whole disease. The disease is the condition of the heart; the words are visible evidence of it.

### 2. Words as revelations

The passage also presents speech as revelation. A good person “out of the good treasure of the heart bringeth forth good things.” An evil person “out of the evil treasure bringeth forth evil things.”

The verb “bringeth forth” suggests disclosure. What is hidden in the heart is made visible through speech. The “treasure” is not a neutral collection of words or phrases; it is a moral inventory: what a person loves, fears, admires, resents, trusts, and treasures. Speech reveals that inventory.

This is reinforced by what follows in the broader context, Matthew 12:36–37, where Jesus says that people will give account for every word, and that by words they will be justified or condemned. Words disclose moral reality, and they expose the speaker to divine judgment.

So words are not only private internal symptoms; they are public revelations of character.

### 3. Words as choices

At the same time, the passage does not reduce speech to a mechanical overflow from the heart. The verbs are active: the mouth “speaketh,” the man “bringeth forth.” The person is still speaking, choosing, and being responsible.

Jesus is not saying, “The words happened to you.” He is saying that the words come from your heart, but they are still yours. In the larger context, he assigns moral responsibility for words. Therefore speech is also a matter of choice.

But this is a qualified choice. One does not choose words in a moral vacuum. One chooses from the resources already stored in the heart. A person formed by love, gratitude, humility, and truth has a different moral vocabulary available than one formed by pride, resentment, fear, and self-justification.

So the passage does not mean that words are arbitrary choices unrelated to character. Rather, it means that a person’s choices reveal what the person has made of himself or herself. The heart supplies the material; the mouth selects and voices it.

### Conclusion

The passage best fits an integrated view:

- **Words are symptoms** because they reveal the inner condition of the heart.
- **Words are revelations** because they disclose to others, and ultimately to God, what is hidden in the moral treasure of the person.
- **Words are choices** because the speaker is responsible for what is spoken, and Jesus assigns judgment based on words.

But the three ideas are not separate. In Matthew 12:34–35, speech is a chosen expression that reveals the heart and functions as a symptom of its moral contents. The deeper point is that lasting change in speech requires change in the heart’s treasure. One may control outward words for a time, but the character of one’s speech will ultimately follow the character of what one loves, fears, and stores inside.
