## The kind of power praised

The power here is **voluntary, self-giving power**, not power that is displayed or seized. Three features stand out.

- **It is not grasping.** "Thought it not robbery to be equal with God" (Greek *harpagmos*) suggests that equality with God was not something to exploit or clutch. Christ's status is treated as a position from which to give, not a possession to protect.
- **It is chosen.** "He made himself of no reputation" and "he humbled himself." No one strips him of anything. His descent is the act of someone fully in command of himself.
- **It defines greatness by direction, not height.** The movement is downward: form of God, then servant, then human, then obedient, then death, then the cross. Each step deepens the self-giving. The passage implies that the highest power is the freedom to give yourself away without losing yourself.

Paul frames this as a model: "Let this mind be in you." It is meant to shape how Christians treat one another, especially those with status (see vv. 3-4).

## Why humble greatness can be more unsettling than domination

1. **Domination is legible.** We know how to respond to it with fear, resentment, resistance, or flattery. A powerful being who serves doesn't fit those responses, and our usual strategies stop working.

2. **It removes our excuses.** If the most powerful figure refuses to dominate, then our own grasping, which we often justify as necessary or natural, looks like a choice. The hymn exposes that.

3. **It reverses our measure of worth.** Most of us quietly assume that greatness means being served, protected, and acknowledged. Seeing greatness in a servant suggests we have been admiring the wrong things.

4. **It cannot be controlled by fear or bargaining.** A tyrant can be appeased or opposed. Someone who holds power without needing to use it for self-advancement is unpredictable and cannot be manipulated with threats or rewards.

5. **It makes a claim on us.** Domination demands submission, which is external. Humble greatness invites imitation, which is internal and more costly. It asks for a change of mind, not just compliance.

6. **The cross exposes the violence of power.** When the one who could dominate accepts humiliation and execution, the people and systems that inflict it are judged by what they do. Vulnerability becomes a mirror.

7. **It can look suspicious.** We know that humility can be a tactic, as in false modesty or soft manipulation. Genuine humility joined to real power is rare enough that we may distrust it, and that distrust says something about us.

## A caution

This text has often been misused to tell the powerless to accept their place. The hymn isn't about the humiliated being told to stay down. It is about someone with real power choosing to give it up for others. The humility it praises is a *choice made from strength*, not a condition imposed by weakness. That is why it is unsettling: it is not submission to power, but power that reveals what power was for.
