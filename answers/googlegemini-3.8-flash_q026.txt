In Matthew 12:34–35, Jesus establishes a direct, causal relationship between internal character and external speech. By employing the metaphors of an "abundance" (an overflowing vessel) and a "treasure" (a storehouse or deposit), He exposes speech not as mere arbitrary noise, but as the inevitable spillover of one’s accumulated inner life. 

The passage ultimately indicates that words are **all three: symptoms, revelations, and choices**, working together in a unified moral framework.

---

### 1. The Connection: The Heart as an Accumulated Stockpile
Jesus uses two key Greek concepts to describe the heart’s moral stockpile:
* **The "Treasure" (*thesauros*):** A treasury, vault, or storeroom. The heart is not neutral or empty; it is a repository. Day by day, a person deposits affections, resentments, desires, and habits into this vault.
* **The "Abundance" (*perisseuma*):** That which is left over, a surplus, or an overflow. 

The connection between the heart and speech is **hydraulic**. A person can suppress their internal inventory for only so long. Eventually, the capacity of the vessel is breached, and whatever fills the heart overflows through the lips. A storehouse filled with grain can only yield grain; a storehouse filled with rot cannot produce life. Therefore, speech is the organic currency drawn from the heart's treasury.

---

### 2. Words as Symptoms, Revelations, and Choices

Rather than these categories being mutually exclusive, the text relies on all three to make its theological and moral point:

#### A. Words as Symptoms (The Diagnostic Dimension)
In verse 34, Jesus asks, *“How can ye, being evil, speak good things?”* Here, He posits an organic impossibility: corrupt nature cannot produce healthy fruit (mirroring Matthew 12:33). 
* In this sense, words function as **symptoms** of a spiritual condition, much like a fever is a symptom of an infection. 
* Speech is largely involuntary over the long run. While a hypocrite can manufacture polite, pious words temporarily, under pressure or over time, the natural "sickness" or "health" of the heart will leak out. The words diagnose the state of the soul.

#### B. Words as Revelations (The Epistemological Dimension)
The heart is hidden; it cannot be seen with the human eye. The Pharisees presented themselves as immaculate keepers of the Law, masking their inner cruelty and pride behind ceremonial purity. 
* Jesus notes that speech serves as a **revelation**—an unmasking of the hidden inventory. 
* By calling the miracles of the Holy Spirit the work of Beelzebub (the immediate context of Matthew 12), the Pharisees' mouths betrayed them. Their speech pulled back the curtain on the "evil treasure" they had kept concealed beneath their robes. Words take what is private and make it public.

#### C. Words as Choices (The Moral/Volitional Dimension)
While Jesus emphasizes the deterministic overflow of the heart, He does not absolve the speaker of moral responsibility. In verse 35, the man *“bringeth forth”* (*ekballei*—casts out, produces) from his treasure. Furthermore, in the verses immediately following (vv. 36–37), Jesus warns that humans will give an account for "every idle word" on the day of judgment.
* Words are **choices**, but the locus of choice is two-fold:
  1. **The immediate choice:** Deciding to speak or hold one's tongue, and choosing how to direct one's language.
  2. **The preparatory choice:** The daily, habitual choices that built the "stockpile" in the first place. You choose what you meditate on, what you love, and what you resent. When the mouth speaks, it executes the verdict of a thousand prior moral choices.

---

### Conclusion
The genius of Jesus’ metaphor is that it binds these three elements into a single progression: 

Because we **choose** what we store in the heart over time, our words inevitably become the **symptoms** of our internal health and the final, undeniable **revelations** of our true character before God and the world.
