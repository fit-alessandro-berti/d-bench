Matthew 12:34–35 connects speech and the heart by presenting the heart as a hidden storehouse and the mouth as the place where that storehouse overflows into public view.

Jesus says:

> “Out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaketh.”

That is the key connection. Speech is not merely external noise; it is the audible overflow of the inner person. The “heart” here is not simply emotion in the modern sense. In biblical language, the heart is the center of thought, desire, intention, loyalty, and moral orientation. So when Jesus speaks of “good treasure” and “evil treasure,” He is describing hidden moral stockpiles: what a person has accumulated inwardly through desire, habit, allegiance, and repeated moral direction.

The metaphor is rich. A treasury is not created in a single moment. It is stored over time. Likewise, the heart gathers what it loves, what it rehearses, what it trusts, what it admires. Speech then draws from that reserve. A person does not merely speak from a passing impulse; he speaks from what has been deposited within.

So the passage strongly suggests that words are **symptoms**, **choices**, and **revelations**—all three—but with a particular emphasis.

---

## 1. Words are symptoms of the heart

Jesus says, “How can ye, being evil, speak good things?” This is diagnostic language. He is not merely addressing a single careless sentence. He is saying that their speech reveals a deeper condition.

The imagery of “vipers” reinforces this. Vipers were associated with venom and deadly harm. Jesus is saying, in effect, “How can a brood of vipers produce wholesome, life-giving speech when the inner nature is poisoned?” Their words are symptoms of a moral condition.

This is like a spring revealing the nature of its source. If the spring is bitter, the water will be bitter. The water is not the cause of the bitterness; it is evidence of it. In the same way, words often expose what is already present in the heart.

That is why this passage is so searching. It treats speech as morally diagnostic. What comes out of the mouth often shows what is filling the heart. Anger, slander, pride, lust, deceit, envy, cruelty—these are not usually isolated verbal accidents. They are signs of something deeper.

So yes, words are **symptoms**. They indicate the condition of the inner person.

---

## 2. Words are also choices for which we are responsible

However, Jesus does not treat words as mere involuntary symptoms, as if we are passive victims of our hearts. In the immediate context, He warns that people will give an account for their words:

> “But I say unto you, That every idle word that men shall speak, they shall give account thereof in the day of judgment. For by thy words thou shalt be justified, and by thy words thou shalt be condemned.”  
> — Matthew 12:36–37

This is crucial. If words were only symptoms and not responsible acts, judgment would make no sense. Jesus holds people accountable for what they say. Their words are not merely evidence of corruption; they are acts of moral agency.

The Pharisees did not just “reveal” a bad heart accidentally. They chose to speak against Christ. They attributed the work of the Holy Spirit to Satan. That was a deliberate verbal act. It was sinful speech, and they were responsible for it.

So the passage does not teach a kind of moral determinism where the heart mechanically forces speech and the person is merely a passive channel. Rather, it shows that the heart shapes speech deeply, but the person still speaks. The words are truly theirs.

This is an important balance. Scripture often holds together what we might be tempted to separate:

- The heart is the source of sin, yet the person is still responsible.
- Sinful words flow from a sinful condition, yet they are also chosen acts.
- A corrupt nature inclines the person, but it does not excuse the act.

So yes, words are **choices**. They are not just things that happen to us; they are things we do.

---

## 3. Words are revelations of hidden treasure

The third category may be the most central in this passage. Words reveal.

Jesus says:

> “A good man out of the good treasure of the heart bringeth forth good things: and an evil man out of the evil treasure bringeth forth evil things.”

The word “treasure” suggests something stored up, hidden, valuable, and accumulated. The heart has a treasury. What is inside eventually comes out. Speech makes the hidden visible—or rather, audible.

This is why words can be so revealing. A person may maintain outward composure, but sudden speech, pressure, conflict, temptation, or crisis often exposes what is stored within. What someone says when provoked, offended, afraid, or powerful often tells us what they truly treasure.

In Matthew 12, the Pharisees’ words reveal their hearts. They have seen the Spirit’s power at work in Jesus, yet their speech betrays hostility, spiritual blindness, and slander. Their accusation is not a small mistake; it is a disclosure of inner opposition to God.

So words are **revelations**. They unveil the moral contents of the heart. They tell us what a person treasures, what he loves, what he fears, what he worships, and what direction his soul is facing.

---

## How the three ideas fit together

The best way to put it is this:

> Words are chosen revelations—voluntary acts that flow from and disclose the hidden moral condition of the heart.

They are **symptoms** because they show what is inside.  
They are **choices** because the person actively speaks and is accountable.  
They are **revelations** because they make hidden treasure public.

The passage emphasizes especially the revelatory and symptomatic nature of speech. The mouth speaks from what fills the heart. But the surrounding context insists that words are also morally accountable acts.

So the answer is not “one or the other.” It is all three, integrated.

---

## The deeper point: speech cannot be permanently improved without heart-change

One of the most powerful implications of Matthew 12:34–35 is that mere outward control of speech is insufficient.

A person may learn to avoid certain words, moderate his tone, or speak more politely. But Jesus is not giving a lesson in etiquette. He is teaching that the problem of speech is ultimately a problem of the heart. If the treasure is evil, the speech will be corrupted at the root, even if it is outwardly refined. If the treasure is good, the speech will bear the fruit of goodness.

This means that truly good speech is not mainly a matter of vocabulary management. It is a matter of having a heart filled with good treasure: truth, love, humility, mercy, reverence, justice, and the fear of God.

In biblical terms, that points to the need for inner renewal. The heart must be changed, cleansed, filled, and reoriented toward God. Then the mouth will speak from a different abundance.

---

## In short

Matthew 12:34–35 teaches that speech is the overflow of the heart. Words are not trivial. They are drawn from hidden moral stockpiles. They reveal what is stored within, they expose the condition of the soul, and they remain responsible acts for which people will give account.

So yes: words are **symptoms**, **choices**, and **revelations**.

But if we had to state the central emphasis of this passage in one sentence, it would be:

> **Words are the audible overflow of the heart.**
