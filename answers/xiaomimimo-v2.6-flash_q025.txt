## Moral Danger in Matthew 6:1

The verse itself carefully distinguishes between these two possibilities, and the distinction matters.

### The key text: "to be seen of them"

The Greek phrase **πρὸς τὸ θεαθῆναι αὐτοῖς** ("in order to be seen by them") introduces a **purposive clause**. This is grammatically decisive: it is not describing visibility but *motivation* — the deliberate orientation of one's action toward the audience.

This means:

- **Visibility is not the problem.** You cannot give alms entirely unseen — money must reach hands, food must reach tables. Secret giving to strangers is also a practical impossibility. Jesus never condemns the *fact* of being seen.
- **The craving to be seen is the problem.** The danger lies in the **intentional crafting** of one's goodness as a performance — curating the scene so that observers will draw the right conclusions about the giver.

### Why this craving is morally corrosive

1. **It reorders the ends of the action.** The act of charity, which has its proper end in the welfare of the recipient, is subtly *hijacked* by a second end — the donor's reputation. The recipient becomes instrumental.

2. **It creates an inverted reward economy.** Jesus says "ye have **no reward** of your Father." This is not arbitrary punishment. The reward *is* the interior disposition of sincerity itself. A person seeking praise has already collected their payment in the currency of social approval — so there is nothing further to receive from God. The self has already spent itself on itself.

3. **It corrupts the sincerity of the act at its root.** The same external behavior (giving alms publicly) is morally *opposite* depending on the heart's orientation. This is why Jesus can later permit public acts of righteousness (Matt. 5:16 — "let your light shine") while forbidding performative ones here. The external structure is identical; the internal structure determines the moral category.

### The deeper principle

Jesus is drawing a line between **two loves** operating in the same act:

- Love of the neighbor (which genuine almsgiving embodies)
- Love of one's own image (which the performance secretly serves)

When the second love disguises itself as the first, the act is not merely lacking virtue — it is *counterfeiting* it. That is what makes it dangerous: not that it is outward, but that it is **self-worship dressed in the clothing of generosity**.

So to answer directly: **it is the craving to be seen, not visibility itself**, that the verse identifies as the moral hazard — and it identifies it precisely by the purposive construction of its language.
