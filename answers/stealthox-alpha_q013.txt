## Why total rejection rather than "better balance"

The sharpest thing about this passage is that God does not say, "Your worship is lopsided—add more justice to it." He says, "I hate it. Take it away." That severity is deliberate, because the problem Amos diagnoses is not a *deficiency* in worship but a *category error* about what worship is.

In the covenantal framework Amos assumes, sacrifice and festival were never freestanding goods that could be weighed against ethics on the same scale. They were *expressions* of a relationship—a whole-life loyalty to YHWH whose visible content was mishpat (just judgment) and tsedaqah (righteous dealing), especially toward the vulnerable. Ritual was the celebration of that order. So when the ethical core is ripped out, the ritual doesn't become incomplete worship. It stops being worship at all and becomes something else: noise, stench, an offense. Asking God to "balance" it would imply the worship is fundamentally sound and merely needs supplementing—which would ratify the very fiction Amos came to destroy.

Notice how the rejection is total and sensory: God refuses to *smell* the offerings (using the cult's own technical vocabulary for acceptance—the "pleasing aroma" of Leviticus and Numbers, now cancelled), refuses to *look* upon them, refuses to *hear* the music. Every channel of liturgical encounter is closed. There is even a bitter echo of Amos 1:2, where "the LORD roars from Zion"—now the people's songs are mere *hamon*, din and tumult. Their loudest devotion registers to God as static.

## The fraud being exposed

Amos has just described the machinery behind the festivals (5:10–12): the righteous pushed aside at the gate, bribes taken, the poor crushed, grain extracted as tribute. Against that backdrop, several distinct frauds come into focus:

- **Laundering injustice through liturgy.** The wealth financing the burnt offerings and fat beasts traces back to exploitation. Worship becomes the final stage in a supply chain of oppression—the place where stolen goods are converted into piety. The festival doesn't merely coexist with the injustice; it *celebrates* the prosperity it produced.

- **Transactional substitution.** The underlying assumption is that sacrifice is payment—that obligation to God and neighbor can be discharged in kind, that enough bulls can offset withheld justice. This treats God as a creditor to be bought off rather than a covenant partner to be obeyed. It's the same fraud Isaiah exposes ("to what purpose is the multitude of your sacrifices?", Isa 1:11) and Micah answers ("what does the LORD require of you but to do justly…?", Mic 6:8).

- **Manufactured assurance.** Verse 18 reveals the function the worship actually served: they *long* for the Day of the LORD, confident their religiosity guarantees their standing. The cult operates as anesthesia—it converts warning signs into feelings of blessing and silences the conscience that ought to be screaming. This is the deepest layer of the fraud: not deceiving God, which is impossible, but constructing an elaborate apparatus of self-deception.

- **Aesthetic substitution.** The songs and viols produce the *sensation* of God's nearness—an emotional experience of the sacred that substitutes for the reality of it. Feeling worship is easier than practicing justice, and the feeling can be scheduled, performed, and repeated.

It's worth noting the possessives: "your feasts," "your assemblies," "your songs." Amos is not abolishing sacrifice in principle; elsewhere the prophets assume the cult is legitimate within a faithful covenant. What God rejects is this worship, as constituted—as a system currently serving the worshipers' need for absolution without repentance. And the following verses (5:25–27) suggest the rot runs deeper than the present moment: even the wilderness offering is called into question. External continuity of ritual, across centuries, turns out to certify nothing.

## The counter-image: a river, not a festival

Verse 24 answers with an image chosen with precision. Festivals are episodic—they punctuate the calendar, surge and subside. But "let judgment run down as waters, and righteousness as a mighty stream" invokes a *ne'eman*—a perennial, never-failing flow, in contrast to the desert wadi that floods violently one day and is bone-dry the next. Their religion is a wadi: intense, dramatic, seasonal, and ultimately empty. What God wants is hydrology, not spectacle—justice flowing continuously through ordinary channels: the courtroom at the gate, the marketplace scales, the treatment of the debtor and the hired hand.

That is also why the passage cannot simply ask for balance. A stream and a festival are different kinds of things. You don't correct a counterfeit bill by adding a few authentic features; you name it counterfeit. The violence of Amos's language—"I hate, I despise"—is diagnostic, even merciful in function: only a rejection this total can break the spell of a system designed to make its participants feel blessed precisely while they are under judgment.
