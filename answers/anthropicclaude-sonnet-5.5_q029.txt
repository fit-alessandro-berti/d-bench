## The posture: humble, gentle, and aimed at mending

Paul describes correction as something done by a person who is **spiritual**, **gentle**, and **aware of their own weakness**, with a goal of **restoration** rather than vindication.

**1. "Ye which are spiritual."** In context, this means people walking by the Spirit (5:16-25), not a self-appointed elite. The fruit of the Spirit includes *gentleness* (5:23), the same Greek word behind "meekness" here. Paul is saying that if you are truly spiritual, it will show in how you correct. Harshness disqualifies the person who claims the title.

**2. "Restore."** The Greek *katartizō* is used for mending nets and setting bones. The image is surgical and healing, not punitive. The aim is the person's recovery, not their shaming, and not the corrector's sense of being right.

**3. "Overtaken in a fault."** The word suggests being caught or surprised by a slip, not cold-blooded rebellion. That shapes the response, which should be that of someone helping a stumbler back up.

**4. "Considering thyself."** Paul shifts from the plural "ye" to the singular "thou." Each person must look inward personally. "Lest thou also be tempted" reminds the corrector that the offender's sin is one they too are capable of.

## Why the three belong together

Each guards against a distortion of the others.

- **Restoration without meekness** turns into punishment. You can be right about the fault and still wound rather than heal.
- **Meekness without restoration** becomes sentimental tolerance, a gentleness that never actually addresses the problem. Paul assumes something must be mended.
- **Both without self-suspicion** become self-righteousness. Correction easily becomes a stage for superiority, which is a real danger in a Galatian church already "biting and devouring one another" (5:15) and prone to vainglory (5:26).

Self-suspicion is the hinge. It produces the meekness, because someone who knows they could fall does not speak from a height. It also keeps the goal true, because you want for the fallen person what you would want for yourself in their place.

## The broader flow

Verse 2 continues: "Bear ye one another's burdens." Correction is a form of burden-bearing, not judgment from a distance. Verse 3 warns that "if a man think himself to be something, when he is nothing, he deceiveth himself", which names the exact pride that poisons correction.

None of this abolishes firmness. Other passages (Matthew 18, 1 Corinthians 5) allow serious discipline. But Galatians 6:1 sets the spirit in which even firm correction must be done: as one sinner helping another toward healing, not as a judge handing down a verdict.
