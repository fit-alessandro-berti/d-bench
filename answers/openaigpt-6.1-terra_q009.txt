Romans 12:20–21 defines victory over evil as **refusing to let evil determine your response**. Instead of returning harm for harm, you meet even an enemy’s basic needs. The victory is not necessarily making the enemy change; it is doing good without becoming captive to hatred or revenge.

“Heap coals of fire on his head” is an image drawn from Proverbs 25:21–22. Interpreters understand it in different ways: it may describe the shame that kindness can awaken, perhaps leading to repentance, or it may evoke divine judgment. Either way, Paul’s immediate instruction is to **feed and give drink**, not to take revenge.

The command is genuinely strong, though it can be misunderstood:

- **Not weak:** It requires restraint and courage when retaliation feels justified.
- **Not manipulative:** Kindness becomes manipulative if it is merely a tactic to humiliate or control. Paul calls for an actual good to be done to the enemy.
- **Not passive:** Doing good does not mean approving wrongdoing, refusing justice, or remaining in danger. Boundaries and protection can be consistent with refusing vengeance.

The passage’s strength lies in confronting evil without reproducing it.
