### In short

Paul is not giving a random checklist. In Ephesians 4:17–5:2 he is describing what it means to **“put off the old self” and “put on the new self”** (4:22–24). Verses 25–32 apply that to everyday life, and the pattern is “take this off / put that on.”

At the head of the vices to be stripped off is **bitterness**. At the climax of the graces to be put on is **forgiveness**. They are placed directly against each other because forgiveness is the precise, gospel-shaped remedy for the poison of bitterness.

And the text treats bitterness as more than a passing feeling. It is better understood as:

- **a settled disposition of the heart** that *includes* feeling,
- **a cultivated habit** of the “old self,”
- **a chosen posture** we are responsible to “put away,” and
- **a contagious poison** that spreads through speech and relationships in the church.

---

## 1. The context: “Put off” and “Put on”

Ephesians 4:22–24 sets the framework:

> “Put off… the old man… and… put on the new man, which after God is created in righteousness and true holiness.”

Then Paul gives concrete examples.

- **4:25** — put off falsehood, speak truth.
- **4:26–27** — be angry, but do not let anger curdle into sin or give the devil a foothold.
- **4:28** — stop stealing, work and share.
- **4:29** — let no corrupt speech come out, only what **gives grace** to hearers.
- **4:30** — do not grieve the Holy Spirit.
- **4:31** — **put away bitterness, wrath, anger, clamor, evil speaking, malice.**
- **4:32** — instead, **be kind, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, as God in Christ has forgiven you.**

Bitterness heads the “off” list. Forgiveness crowns the “on” list. That is deliberate.

---

## 2. Bitterness is the root; the other sins are its fruit

The Greek word is **πικρία** (*pikria*): sharpness, harshness, a bitter, poisonous quality. It is the same word used in Acts 8:23, where Simon is described as being in “the gall of bitterness.” Hebrews 12:15 picks up the agricultural image:

> “Lest any root of bitterness springing up trouble you, and thereby many be defiled.”

So bitterness is not merely a momentary emotion. It is what happens when hurt or anger is **retained, rehearsed, and allowed to take root**.

Notice the progression in verse 31:

1. **Bitterness** — the inner, settled resentment.
2. **Wrath and anger** — the emotional eruptions that come from it.
3. **Clamor** — loud, quarrelsome conflict.
4. **Evil speaking** — slander and injurious speech.
5. **Malice** — the underlying desire to see the other person harmed.

In other words, **bitterness is the root; the rest are the bitter fruit.** You can trim the leaves by suppressing angry outbursts, but if the root remains, it will keep producing poison.

---

## 3. Why forgiveness is placed directly against it

### A. Forgiveness is the exact opposite of bitterness

“Forgiving” translates **χαριζόμενοι** (*charizomenoi*), from the same root as **χάρις** (*charis*), “grace.” It means to release a debt freely, as an act of unearned favor.

Bitterness is the refusal to release the debt. It keeps a record of wrongs and quietly demands payment.

Forgiveness cancels the debt because God has already canceled ours (see the parable of the unforgiving servant in Matthew 18). They cannot coexist. You cannot simultaneously:

- nurse a root of bitterness, and
- forgive “even as God… hath forgiven you.”

One must die for the other to live.

### B. Forgiveness is grounded in the gospel, not merely willpower

Paul does not say, “Forgive because the other person deserves it.” He says:

> “Forgiving one another, even as God for Christ’s sake hath forgiven you.”

The Greek literally says God forgave you **“in Christ.”** Christ is the sphere and ground of that forgiveness. The cross is where God absorbed the cost of our sin rather than pouring out the bitterness we deserved.

So Christian forgiveness is not pretending the sin didn’t happen. It is **costly grace**: naming the wrong, entrusting justice to God, and releasing personal vengeance because Christ has borne the ultimate debt.

### C. Forgiveness is the “grace” that counteracts corrupt speech

There is a beautiful wordplay in this passage.

In **4:29**, Paul says our speech should **“minister grace”** (*charin*) to those who hear.

In **4:32**, we are told to be **“forgiving”** (*charizomenoi*) one another—literally, “gracing one another.”

Bitterness is un-grace. It is harsh, acidic, unyielding. Forgiveness is grace extended horizontally because grace has first been received vertically.

---

## 4. Is bitterness a feeling, a habit, a chosen posture, or something contagious?

It is **all of these, in a certain order**.

### It is not *merely* a feeling

Bitterness has an emotional texture—resentment, hurt, smoldering anger. But it is more than an unbidden emotion.

Scripture commands believers to “put away” bitterness. That implies responsibility. You cannot be commanded to instantly stop feeling an involuntary emotion, but you *can* be commanded to stop **entertaining, nursing, and consenting to** a bitter heart.

Paul even allows for a non-sinning form of anger in 4:26:

> “Be ye angry, and sin not.”

So the first sting of anger is not automatically bitterness. Bitterness begins when that anger is held onto after the sun goes down (4:26), when it is meditated on, justified, and allowed to harden.

### It is a habit / settled disposition

Yes. Bitterness is a vice of the “old self.” It is a cultivated orientation of the heart. Like a root, it grows over time through repeated choices.

The command in 4:31 is an aorist imperative: “Let all bitterness… be put away.” This suggests a **decisive break**. But the very next verse uses ongoing terms: “Be ye kind… tenderhearted, forgiving one another.” So the decisive break is followed by a lifetime of re-learning a new pattern.

### It is a chosen posture

Primarily, yes.

The imperatives place responsibility on believers. By the Spirit, we are to:

- refuse to rehearse the offense,
- release the right to repay,
- actively seek the other person’s good,
- and keep “gracing” them as God has graced us.

This does not mean forgiveness is easy or that our emotions instantly cooperate. Often the will must lead and the feelings follow later. But the posture is chosen, not accidental.

### It is also contagious

This is where the passage is especially searching.

Paul places these commands in a community setting. He says “one another” repeatedly. Bitterness does not stay private. It comes out as:

- **clamor** — public strife,
- **evil speaking** — slander,
- **malice** — ill will toward others.

And Hebrews 12:15 is explicit: a root of bitterness “defiles many.” Left unchecked, bitterness spreads through a church like poison or leaven.

Ephesians 4:27 adds another layer: unresolved bitterness gives “place to the devil.” It creates a foothold for the accuser, who is always eager to divide the body of Christ.

So Paul treats bitterness as **communal dynamite**. It is not just “my private hurt.” It is something that can infect the whole fellowship.

---

## 5. What forgiveness is and is not

Biblical forgiveness is often misunderstood, so it is worth being clear:

**Forgiveness is not:**

- pretending the sin was not real,
- denying the pain,
- immediately trusting someone who has not repented,
- removing all consequences or boundaries,
- or waiting until we “feel like it.”

**Forgiveness is:**

- releasing the person into God’s hands,
- refusing to pay back evil for evil,
- absorbing the cost as Christ absorbed ours,
- and actively willing the other person’s good—which is why Paul pairs it with being “kind” and “tenderhearted.”

It is, at its core, **grace received becoming grace extended**.

---

## The bottom line

Paul sets bitterness and forgiveness directly against each other because **forgiveness is the gospel applied to a grudge.**

Bitterness says, “I will make them pay.”  
Forgiveness says, “Christ has already paid for me, so I release this debt.”

Bitterness is a poisonous root that hardens the heart and infects the church.  
Forgiveness is the Spirit-enabled posture of the new humanity—kind, tenderhearted, and gracious—because we have been forgiven “in Christ.”

So the command is not merely “cheer up and forget it.” It is a summons to **dig up the root** by the grace of God, and to keep “gracing one another” until the root is dead and the fruit of Christlike love remains.
