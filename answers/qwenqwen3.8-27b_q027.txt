

The clearest answer is: **the Pharisee is condemned primarily for self-righteous pride — trusting in himself that he is righteous — and that pride is expressed through comparison, contempt, and the use of his own good deeds as proof of his standing before God.**

The parable does not first attack the Pharisee for a formally false doctrine. It is possible that his words are factually true: he may not be an extortioner, he may not be an adulterer, he may fast and tithe. The issue is not that tithing or fasting are wrong. The issue is that he turns his observance into a ground of self-justification.

Luke frames the story in verse 9:

> “And he spake this parable unto certain which trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and despised others.”

So the two chief sins are already named:

1. **Self-trusting righteousness** — “trusted in themselves that they were righteous.”
2. **Despising others** — “despised others.”

The Pharisee’s prayer shows both.

## 1. What exactly condemns the Pharisee?

### A. Not mainly false doctrine

The Pharisee’s statement may not be doctrinally false in the way a heresy would be. He does not say, “I am God.” He does not explicitly deny grace. He does not make a formal theological error that is easy to refute with a proposition.

But there is a deeper, implicit error: **he treats his own moral performance as the ground of his acceptance before God.** His prayer gives the impression that he is right before God because he is different from others and because he performs religious duties.

That is a false self-assessment. It is also a false practical theology: he assumes that righteousness before God can be based on his own moral résumé rather than on God’s mercy.

So if “false doctrine” means a mistaken proposition about God, the text does not say that explicitly. But if “false doctrine” means a hidden belief about how one stands before God, then yes: the Pharisee implicitly trusts in his own righteousness rather than in God’s mercy.

### B. Pride is the core

The root problem is pride, more precisely **self-righteous pride**.

His prayer is self-referential:

> “God, I thank thee, that I am not as other men are…”

He thanks God, but the content of the thanksgiving is his own moral distinctiveness. He does not say, “Thank you that you have kept me from becoming like them.” He does not say, “Thank you that your grace has made me merciful, faithful, or self-controlled.” He says, in effect:

> “Thank you that I am not like them. I have kept my distance. I have kept my standards. I have kept my record clean.”

The thanksgiving is directed toward his own excellence. That turns prayer into a mirror rather than a petition.

### C. Comparison becomes the problem

Comparison itself is not automatically the sin. The Pharisee says, “I am not as other men are.” That could be a true observation. But comparison becomes fatal when it becomes the means by which he establishes his own spiritual superiority.

The danger is not that he notices moral difference, but that he uses that difference to build a throne for himself.

He does not merely say, “God, keep me from pride.” He says, “God, I am not like them.” The neighbor becomes the foil for his self-approval. The other person’s sin is used to confirm his own rightness.

And then it becomes personal:

> “or even as this publican.”

That phrase is crucial. It is not just an abstract comparison. The publican is present. The Pharisee includes the actual man in the temple within his list of people he is not like. The comparison becomes contempt.

### D. Contempt is explicit

Jesus already said in verse 9 that these people “despised others.” The Pharisee’s phrase “or even as this publican” shows that contempt directly.

The publican, in the ancient setting, would have been socially despised. As a tax collector, he was often associated with Rome, with commercial exploitation, and with impurity. The Pharisee does not say, “Lord, have mercy on him.” He does not say, “Lord, forgive him.” He says, “I am not even like this man.”

That is not humility. That is spiritual elitism. It places the Pharisee above the publican and, implicitly, above those who need mercy.

### E. The use of virtue as self-display

Yes, the Pharisee uses virtue as self-display — but not necessarily public display in the theatrical sense. The KJV says he “prayed thus with himself.” Other translations render it as “stood by himself and prayed.” The point is that his prayer is self-centered.

His good deeds become his credentials:

> “I fast twice in the week, I give tithes of all that I possess.”

These are not bad things. In Luke’s Gospel, tithing, fasting, and justice are not rejected. But here they are not offered as fruits of grace. They are presented as evidence: “Look how good I am.”

That is the danger of virtue when it becomes a substitute for repentance. The Pharisee’s virtues do not save him because they have become the ground of his self-justification.

So the exact condemnation is not simply that he compared himself to others. The condemnation is that **comparison produced self-exaltation, self-exaltation produced contempt, and contempt closed him off from mercy.**

## 2. Why is the tax collector’s posture spiritually truer?

The tax collector’s posture is not treated as the essence of his righteousness. The physical posture — standing far off, not lifting his eyes, striking his breast — is important, but it is not magical. It is an outward sign of an inward reality.

The spiritual truth is in his prayer:

> “God be merciful to me a sinner.”

That is the decisive difference.

### A. He acknowledges his sin

The publican does not say, “I am not like this Pharisee.” He does not list his good deeds. He does not compare himself with anyone. He says, “I am a sinner.”

This is spiritually truer because it is honest before God. The Pharisee sees the publican’s sin, but he does not see his own. The publican sees himself.

The Pharisee’s blind spot is pride. The publican’s self-awareness is humility.

### B. He depends on mercy

The publican’s prayer is a petition for mercy:

> “God be merciful to me a sinner.”

He does not ask God to approve his performance. He asks God to pardon his guilt. He comes before God not as a moral exemplar but as a supplicant.

That is the only posture that can receive justification. The Pharisee thinks he is already justified. The publican says, “I need to be justified. Have mercy on me.”

In other words:

- The Pharisee seeks **approval**.
- The publican seeks **pardon**.

That distinction is central.

### C. He does not make another person’s sin the proof of his own righteousness

The Pharisee’s prayer depends on the publican: “I am not as this publican.” His standing is partly defined by the other man’s unworthiness.

The publican’s prayer does not depend on the Pharisee. He does not say, “God, have mercy on me because I am better than the Pharisee.” He simply says, “God, have mercy on me, a sinner.”

His right relation to God is not built on being better than someone else. It is built on acknowledging his need for God’s mercy.

That is why it is spiritually truer: it is oriented toward God, not toward self-comparison.

### D. The posture expresses humility

The publican’s posture — standing far off, not lifting his eyes, striking his breast — expresses shame, reverence, and a sense of unworthiness before God. It is not necessarily a formal religious requirement, but in the parable it functions as the bodily expression of his heart.

He does not come forward with confidence in himself. He comes with a broken spirit. The posture says, “I do not presume before you. I know my place. I know my sin. I depend on your mercy.”

That is why Jesus says:

> “I tell you, this man went down to his house justified rather than the other…”

Not because the publican was morally superior. Not because his physical posture saved him. But because his heart corresponded to the truth: before God, he was a sinner, and the only ground of hope was mercy.

## 3. The deeper lesson

The Pharisee’s prayer is a closed circuit:

> “I am not like them. I fast. I tithe. Therefore I am right.”

The publican’s prayer is an open appeal:

> “I am a sinner. Therefore have mercy on me.”

The first trusts in self. The second trusts in God’s mercy.

The Pharisee may be practicing good religion, but he has turned religion into a proof of his own excellence. The publican is not necessarily practicing superior religion; he is practicing the only relation to God that can be true: repentance, humility, and dependence on mercy.

So if we must choose among the options:

- **False doctrine?** Only implicitly — a false belief that one can stand before God on one’s own moral record.
- **Pride?** Yes — this is the root.
- **Comparison?** Yes, but as an expression of pride, not as the main charge by itself.
- **Contempt?** Yes — explicit in “or even as this publican.”
- **Use of virtue as self-display?** Yes — but the deeper issue is that virtue has become self-justification.

The most exact answer is: **the Pharisee is condemned for self-righteous pride, expressed in comparison, contempt, and self-display, and hidden under a false trust in his own righteousness. The tax collector’s posture is spiritually truer because it expresses honest self-knowledge, humility, and dependence on God’s mercy.**
