## The grammar already answers half the question

The phrase "to be seen of them" renders *pros to theathēnai autois* — a purpose construction. It names not a circumstance but an aim. Matthew does not say "beware of doing your alms where men can see," which would make visibility itself the offense; he says "beware of doing them *in order to be looked at*." The verb is *theaomai*, a spectator's word, the root behind *theater* — and it sits in a passage that will shortly call the offenders *hypokritai*, stage-players.

The decisive comparison is five verses earlier: "Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father which is in heaven." The same audience, the same visible works, even the same phrase *emprosthen tōn anthrōpōn*. But the sentence doesn't stop at *see*. Seeing is penultimate there; the glory of the Father is terminal. In 6:1 the sentence stops at being seen. The deed's arrow has been shortened. It terminates in the eye of the beholder instead of passing through it.

So: visibility is the medium, craving is the corruption. But that answer is too easy, and the verse is more interesting than that.

## What the craving actually does

Notice that the alms are not fake. Real bread reaches a real hungry person. The danger here is not ordinary hypocrisy — professing virtue you don't have — but something subtler: *genuine goodness performed as a means to something else*. The beggar is fed, and the beggar is used. His hunger becomes convenient raw material for my self-presentation. He was the occasion of my virtue rather than its object. That is the moral injury, and it is done to him, not merely to me.

Two further corruptions follow.

**Attention bends back on itself.** To perform goodness is to split into actor and spectator, and the spectator is watching the actor, not the sufferer. There is only so much attention available; whatever is spent monitoring my own generosity is withdrawn from the person it was supposed to serve. This is why the verse condemns something that can coexist with sincere feeling. You may genuinely pity the man *and* be watching yourself pity him, and the watching is a subtraction.

**The audience begins to choose your virtues.** Once goodness is calibrated to what registers with onlookers, you will drift toward the visible, legible, praiseworthy goods and away from the ones that cannot be shown: patience with an unbearable person, work no one will ever attribute to you, forgiveness offered without announcing that it was needed. A reputation-fed morality is systematically skewed toward the photogenic. The damage isn't just to individual acts but to the shape of a whole life.

## "They have their reward"

The language in 6:2 is commercial — *apechousin*, the idiom of a receipt marked paid in full. This is not a thunderbolt. It is an accounting closure. You wanted to be seen; you were seen; the transaction is complete and there is nothing outstanding. The frightening thing is not that God refuses to pay but that he has nothing left to pay for. The deed was already cashed out in applause.

That reframes the danger. Public goodness is not risky because it attracts punishment but because it is *self-liquidating*. It converts an act of love into currency, and currency spent is gone.

## Why hiding won't save you

Here is the trap in "take heed." The instruction requires self-monitoring, and self-monitoring is precisely the disease. *Look at me, not looking at myself.* Concealment can become its own theater — the ostentatiously anonymous donor, the ascetic whose humility is his loudest possession. Diogenes trampling Plato's carpet with more pride than Plato had in it.

Which is why the remedy in 6:3 is not "hide it from them" but "let not thy left hand know what thy right hand doeth." That is hyperbole aimed at a stranger target than the crowd: it aims at your own bookkeeping self, the internal accountant who tallies merits. The goal is not that others shouldn't know, but that you should stop keeping the ledger — a self-forgetfulness in which the need in front of you simply occupies the whole field.

So the test the verse implies is not *was anyone watching?* Two better ones: **Would this act survive the subtraction of the audience?** and **Where did the glory finally come to rest?**

On that second test, even the desire to be seen is not uniformly corrupt. To be seen in a way that turns others toward the good, or toward God, is 5:16, and it is commanded. What condemns 6:1 is the curve of the act back upon the doer — the deed that leaves home, circles the neighbor, and returns to be deposited in the self's account. Visibility is only the road it travels. The problem is the destination.
