John is not giving a gentle suggestion; he is giving a test. The claim “I love God” can be spoken sincerely, but if the same person hates a brother, John says the claim is a lie. Why?

Because **God is invisible, but the neighbor is visible**. The unseen God cannot be used as a test case for love in the same concrete way that a brother can. The brother is right there — he can be seen, helped, forgiven, served, or ignored. The neighbor is made in God’s image, and in Johannine terms, the love of God becomes visible in the world precisely through love for one another:

> “No one has ever seen God; if we love one another, God abides in us and his love is perfected in us.”  
> — 1 John 4:12

So the visible neighbor is the *testing ground* for love of the invisible God. If the love of God is truly in a person, it will overflow to the people they can see. If it does not overflow there, then it was never really love for God at all. The rhetorical question in 1 John 4:20 — “How can he love God whom he has not seen?” — means: **he cannot**. The two loves are inseparable.

The self-deception John exposes is the comfortable lie that we can separate **faith from ethics**, or **devotion to God from love of people**. People can convince themselves that they love God intensely while despising a brother. They may even use religious language to justify their hatred. But this is a self-made God, not the God of Jesus Christ. An invisible God can be imagined as approving our resentment; a visible brother or sister confronts us with an undeniable call to love. So John’s test is severe:

> “If anyone says, ‘I love God,’ and hates his brother, he is a liar.”

It exposes the self-deception of **spiritualized love** — love that is only a feeling, an idea, or a claim, but not a life. Real love, John says earlier, is “not in word or talk but in deed and truth” (1 John 3:18). The brother you can see is the test of whether you truly love the God you cannot see.
