Both — but the accent falls on training. The final clause, "for where your treasure is, there will your heart be also," works in two directions at once, yet the grammar and the structure of the passage make the formative reading primary.

1. What the text actually claims

Notice the direction of the sentence in verse 21. Jesus does not say "where your heart is, there your treasure will be" — which would make treasure a mere symptom, a diagnostic readout of a heart already fixed. He says the reverse: where the treasure is, there the heart *will be* (estai — a future tense). The heart follows the treasure. Treasure is named as the leading term, the heart as the trailing one. That is the language of formation, not just revelation.

This matches the imperatives that precede it. Verses 19–20 are commands: "do not lay up... but lay up." If treasure only revealed the heart, the natural instruction would be "change your heart, and your treasuring will follow." Instead Jesus commands a change in the treasuring itself — in the practice of storing, investing, accumulating — with the implied promise that the heart will be drawn after it. The practical act is given as the lever; the heart is what moves when the lever is pulled. You are told to relocate your treasure precisely because the heart is portable and will migrate to wherever the treasure has been sent ahead.

2. Why the revelatory reading is still true

At the same time, the saying obviously functions as a diagnostic. Verse 21 is offered as a "for" (gar) — a reason, an explanation. It states a general truth about human beings that anyone can use as a mirror: look at where your treasure actually is (what you protect, insure, check on, worry over), and you will discover where your heart actually is, whatever you may profess. In the wider context of Matthew 6 — where Jesus has just exposed almsgiving, prayer, and fasting done for human applause — this fits his running concern with the gap between exterior religion and interior reality. Your ledger is more honest than your testimony.

So the text is not forcing a choice. It reveals *and* trains: it reveals because the heart has already been trained by past treasuring, and it commands new treasuring because the heart can be retrained. The saying assumes a feedback loop, and the imperatives break into that loop at the point of practice.

3. How possession, attention, and love become tied together

The passage binds these three by a chain of implicit psychology:

Possession commands attention. "Treasure" (thēsauros) is not casual property but what is stored up, hoarded, secured. The very images Jesus chooses — moth, rust (or "eating," brōsis, perhaps vermin), thieves digging through mud-brick walls — are images of anxiety. Earthly treasure must be watched, mended, guarded. To possess something fragile and threatened is to have your attention conscripted by it. What you own in this way begins to own your vigilance. (This is why the passage flows directly into vv. 22–24 on the eye and the two masters, and then vv. 25–34 on anxiety: the single eye, the impossibility of serving God and mammon, and "do not be anxious" are all elaborations of the same point about captured attention.)

Attention becomes love. "Heart" (kardia) in biblical idiom is not sentiment but the command center of the person — the seat of intention, loyalty, desire, and will. What we attend to habitually, we come to care about; what we guard, we cherish; what we invest in, we long for the flourishing of. Attention is the tribute the heart pays, and over time the tribute becomes allegiance. Hence verse 24 can escalate from treasure to servitude and from servitude to love and hate: "he will hate the one and love the other." Mammon is not merely kept; it is served, and eventually loved.

Therefore, redirect the possession to redirect the love. Since the heart tracks the treasure, the way to relocate your love is to relocate your investments. "Lay up treasures in heaven" — in Jewish usage this meant concrete acts: almsgiving, mercy, obedience stored with God (cf. Matthew 19:21, "sell what you have, give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven"; Luke 12:33–34). These acts place your stake in God's keeping, where decay and theft cannot reach, and the heart, following its stake, is drawn toward God rather than toward the strongbox.

4. The upshot

The logic is almost mechanical: you love what you attend to, you attend to what you have staked yourself in, and you choose where to stake yourself. So the text refuses the comfortable view that the heart is a fixed interior fact that possessions merely express. The heart is trainable, and treasure is the training apparatus. Verse 21 does double duty: as a mirror, it lets you read your present heart off your present treasuring; as a promise attached to a command, it assures you that new treasuring will make a new heart. The permanence contrast (moth, rust, thieves versus incorruptible heaven) adds the final motive: a heart tethered to perishable treasure will share its fate — perpetually anxious and finally bereaved — while a heart sent ahead into heaven is secured where its treasure is secure.
