It is hard to take seriously because **it threatens the architecture of the self.** Your enemies do not merely disagree with you; they confirm your boundaries, your suffering, and your identity as someone worthy of defense. The command in Matthew 5:44 is shocking because it does not ask for tolerance *within* a shared society—“love your neighbor”—but for active blessing, doing good, and prayer *toward the one who is actively harming you*. It demands that you treat a source of threat not as a threat, but as a person for whom you are responsible before God.

Why is that so difficult?

**1. It violates the logic of reciprocity and tribal survival.**
Human morality usually runs on a calculus of worth: the good deserve good, the wicked deserve harm, and love is a reward for belonging. To love the enemy collapses that distinction. The text anchors this not in sentiment but in cosmology: God “maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good.” The sun does not ask if the crop is just before shining. Enemy-love is difficult because it asks us to imitate a generosity that is not contingent on the other’s merit—an imitation that feels like self-erasure if you still believe your security depends on winning.

**2. It looks like weakness, or worse, complicity.**
To “turn the other cheek” (in the wider context of Matthew) was not passive submission; it was a refusal to play the game of honor and domination. But to modern ears, blessing a persecutor can sound like sentimental denial of justice, or like a strategic ploy to “convert” them through kindness. That is why you ask whether it is sentimental, strategic, or strong.

So which is it?

**Not sentimental.** Sentimentality wishes away the enmity; it wants everyone to feel warm and fuzzy. Jesus’s command is issued in the context of persecution—“despitefully use you, and persecute you.” It is not asking you to feel affection for your abuser. It commands *actions*: bless (speak well of), do good (material action), pray (intercede for their flourishing). This is love as will and habit, not mood. It is hard-won because it requires seeing the enemy’s full humanity while still naming their hostility.

**Not merely strategic, though it is strategically devastating.** Nonviolent resistance—Gandhi, King—showed that enemy-love can break cycles of violence because it refuses to mirror the oppressor’s logic. But if you reduce the command to strategy—“I will love you so you will stop hurting me”—you still make the enemy the center of the equation, and love becomes a tool for control. The text says the goal is not victory but *filial participation*: “That ye may be children of your Father which is in heaven.” The point is ontological. You become what you do; by loving without condition, you are formed into the image of a God who gives common grace to all.

**It is a deeper form of moral strength.** To curse back is easy; it takes no character, only reaction. To pray for someone who is destroying your life requires a self so securely rooted that it does not need the enemy’s validation, concession, or destruction to hold identity together. It is strength *under control*—the refusal to let the enemy define your soul. In this sense, enemy-love is not the absence of anger or justice; it is the presence of a love that will not let the other become demonic, because it refuses to become demonic itself.

So why is it so hard to take seriously? Because **it asks you to relinquish the right to define yourself against your enemy.** It demands that your moral world be larger than your grievance. And that is not sentiment. It is not just tactics. It is the moral strength of a life that has been given a new father—one whose sun rises on everyone.
