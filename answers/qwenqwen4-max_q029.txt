## The posture, in the grammar itself

Notice how the sentence moves. It begins in the plural — *ye which are spiritual* — and ends in the singular: *considering thyself, lest thou also be tempted*. Paul starts by addressing a group with a responsibility and ends by isolating each member of it under a warning. You cannot discharge this duty as a committee. Whatever the community does, the one doing it stands personally exposed while he does it. That grammatical slide from "ye" to "thyself" is already the moral argument.

The posture, then, is not that of a judge who has arrived at a settled position and now pronounces from it. It is that of someone who is *at work on another person while remaining under observation himself*.

## The four words that set the terms

**"Overtaken"** (*prolēmphthē*) — caught, taken by surprise, overtaken before he could get clear. Paul does not describe a man who devised a fault and settled into it; he describes a man whom sin outran. This is a deliberate construal, and it is already mercy. The verse opens by telling the story of the fall in the way you would want your own fall told: *he was overtaken*. Compare the alternative framings available — *he chose this, he is that kind of man, this is what he really is*. The framing precedes the instruction and governs it.

**"Restore"** (*katartizō*) — used of mending nets, and in medical Greek of setting a dislocated limb. The image carries three things at once. Something is genuinely out of joint: this is not a matter of taste, and the limb really does need setting. The work requires knowledge of where the bone belongs — restoration has a shape, an aim, a norm. And the injured place is exquisitely tender, so that force does damage even when it is technically correct. A man who wrenches a shoulder into place has not restored it. Gentleness here is not a softening of the task; it is what the nature of the injury demands *if the task is actually to be accomplished*.

**"Meekness"** (*prautēs*) — just named in 5:23 as fruit of the Spirit, and just contrasted in 5:26 with vainglory, provoking, and envying. Which means "ye which are spiritual" cannot mean *the advanced party, the rigorous ones*. In this letter, of all letters, spiritual status is not earned by rigor. Paul has spent five chapters dismantling the idea that anyone holds standing before God by performance. So he now defines the spiritual man by what he does with someone else's failure: the measure of maturity is the capacity to mend without dominating. The genuinely *pneumatikos* is the one whose handling of a fallen brother bears the Spirit's own manner.

**"Considering thyself"** (*skopōn seauton*) — a present participle, continuous: keeping watch on yourself, not consulting yourself once and moving on. And what you watch for is not past sin but future possibility: *lest thou also be tempted*. The "also" quietly concedes that the fallen man was tempted — the same merciful framing again — and simultaneously denies that you are made of different material.

## Why the three cannot be separated

Each one guards against a specific corruption, and each corruption is what the other two become when left alone.

**Restoration without meekness becomes reconstruction in my own image.** The corrector has a real norm and real conviction, and therefore real power over someone in shame. That combination is combustible. What begins as mending ends as breaking — the man is made to feel the weight of his fault as the price of readmission, and the corrector's satisfaction becomes indistinguishable from love of the standard. The limb is set, but the person is crushed. Paul's word for the antidote is not *technique* but *pneuma*: a spirit of meekness, an atmosphere, a settled disposition toward the person, not a gentleness performed over an unaltered contempt.

**Meekness without restoration becomes sentimentality.** If nothing is out of joint, there is nothing to do but reassure — and reassurance abandons the man to the very thing that is disabling him. The dislocation is real. Paul is not proposing that the community overlook faults; he is proposing that it *mend* them. Kindness that will not name the injury is not kindness but withdrawal, and it usually protects the onlooker from the cost of involvement rather than protecting the fallen from pain.

**And both together, without self-suspicion, become condescension.** This is the subtlest failure and the one Paul aims at hardest, because it can survive intact alongside correct doctrine and gentle manner. You may restore accurately and speak softly while privately occupying a different tier of being. The whole transaction then confirms your rank. This is precisely the disease of Galatia — righteousness as comparative standing — reappearing inside the church's discipline. Hence the immediate sequel: *Bear ye one another's burdens* (6:2), which makes correction a weight taken onto yourself rather than a verdict issued from outside; and *if a man think himself to be something, when he is nothing, he deceiveth himself* (6:3); and *let every man prove his own work* rather than measure himself against his neighbor (6:4). The self-suspicion of 6:1 is the seed of that whole passage.

## What self-suspicion is not

It is crucial that Paul says *lest thou also be tempted*, not *since thou also art guilty*. The latter dissolves all correction: nobody may say anything to anybody, and the argument becomes a shield for the unrepentant. Paul's version leaves the mandate intact and qualifies the *manner and the motive*. You still set the bone. You do it as a fellow patient who knows he is not immune, and who therefore handles the tender place the way he would want his own handled.

Nor does meekness mean soft words. The same Paul withstood Peter to his face (Gal 2:11), and the same New Testament that says *restore such an one* also says *count him not as an enemy, but admonish him as a brother* (2 Thess 3:15), and elsewhere puts the defiantly unrepentant outside (1 Cor 5). The distinction Paul draws here is between a man *overtaken* and a man entrenched. Meekness governs the disposition toward the person, not the volume of the sentence.

## The unity beneath them

All three flow from one thing: that standing before God is received, not achieved. Because I have been restored, restoration is what I owe. Because I was handled gently when I was bruised — *a bruised reed shall he not break* — gentleness is the family manner. Because I stand by grace and not by constitution, I have no immunity to trade on and no rank to defend.

So the posture is solidarity in the work of mending: another man's fall is not an occasion for establishing distance from him but evidence of a condition we share. The three are kept together because correction is an exercise of power over someone's shame, and only all three at once can keep that power from becoming the very sin it is meant to mend.
