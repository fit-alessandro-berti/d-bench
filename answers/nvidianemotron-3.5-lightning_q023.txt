### Why “Way” and “Thoughts”? 

In Isaiah 55:6–7 the Hebrew words are telling:

* **“Way”** (*derek*) — denotes one’s lifestyle, moral direction, habits, and outward conduct. It’s the path a person walks.
* **“Thoughts”** (*machashabah*) — denotes inner intentions, plans, imaginings, and the ruling mental or volitional orientation of the heart.

The pairing is deliberate and comprehensive. In the biblical view, genuine repentance never divides the person into “outer” and “inner” compartments that can be edited separately. The prophet is drawing a line through every realm of human existence:

| “Way” (Outward) | “Thoughts” (Inward) |
| :--- | :--- |
| Conduct, behaviors, relationships, moral habits | Motivations, desires, attitudes, cognitive patterns |
| What one **does** | What one **loves, trusts, or trusts in** |
| Visible trajectory | Invisible grid that shapes the trajectory |

**Why both?** 
1. **To prevent religious hypocrisy.** People have a natural talent for cleaning up their actions while keeping their hearts unchanged (or vice versa). Isaiah refuses to let Israel (or any reader) settle for half-measures.
2. **To mirror God’s own completeness.** Later in the same chapter, God declares, *"For my thoughts are not your thoughts, nor are your ways my ways"* (55:8). The call to repentance meets the sinner with the same totality that God brings to reconciliation.
3. **Because the root produces the fruit.** In Scripture, “thoughts” (the inner life) are the wellspring of “ways” (the outward life). A change that stops at behavior often proves brittle; a change that reaches the heart produces lasting directional shift.

### What This Implies

#### ✦ Outward Conduct
Repentance is not merely an emotional turning or a intellectual assent. It involves a **renunciation of patterned sin**—a deliberate forsaking of the moral trajectories that characterize the “wicked” and “unrighteous.” It is observable, directional, and often costly, because it requires breaking habits, restructuring relationships, and choosing God’s path over cultural or personal convenience.

#### ✦ Inward Imagination
The call to forsake “thoughts” repentance targets the mind, will, and affections. It means subjecting one’s fantasies, ambitions, grudges, and self-justifications to the lordship of Christ (cf. 2 Cor 10:5). It implies that God cares about *why* we do what we do, not just *that* we do it. Hidden pride, envy, lust, fear, or self-reliance are brought into the light and turned toward God.

#### ✦ The Possibility of Mercy
The structure of the verse itself answers the question of how mercy is received:
- The invitation is **universal** (“let the wicked… let the unrighteous…”) but **conditional** on a whole-person turning.
- The mercy promised—*“he will have mercy… and he will abundantly pardon”* — is proportionate to the completeness of the response. God does not pardon a divided heart; He abundantly pardons the person who turns from *both* the wrong path and the wrong imagination.
- It also underscores that mercy is not earned by perfect forsaking, but **made real** by genuine repentance. The “abundant pardon” is God’s generous response to a sinner who, empowered by His grace, lays down every competing allegiance and mindset.

### In Summary
Isaiah 55:6–7 presents repentance as a **holistic turnaround**: a leaving behind of the sinful “way” one walks, and a surrender of the “thoughts” that drive it. The implication is clear—God seeks not mere behavioral modification or private piety, but a total realignment of the person. When that happens, the “abundant pardon” is not a token gesture but a full, covenantal restoration, because the one returning has given God everything He asked for: the whole road, and the whole heart.
