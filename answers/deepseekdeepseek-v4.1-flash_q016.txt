The elder brother’s deepest problem is not merely anger, envy, wounded fairness, or even pride in obedience as isolated faults. Those are symptoms. The root is a **self-righteous, transactional heart** that does not truly know the father’s grace and therefore cannot share the father’s joy.

He says, “These many years do I serve thee, neither transgressed I at any time thy commandment: and yet thou never gavest me a kid…” He views his relationship with the father as a ledger: service rendered, reward owed. His obedience has become his identity and his claim on the father. So when the father shows mercy to the younger son, it does not merely hurt his feelings—it contradicts his whole works-based understanding of acceptance.

So among the options, **“discomfort with mercy itself” is closest**, because he refuses to enter the feast of grace. But even that is an expression of something deeper: **pride in his own obedience and self-righteousness**. He boasts of never transgressing, yet he does not love his brother, does not call him “my brother” but “this thy son,” and does not rejoice with the father. He wants a kid to make merry with his friends, not communion with the father.

The father’s answer exposes the real issue: “Son, thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine.” His problem was not lack of blessing. It was lack of a son’s heart. He was outwardly near the father but inwardly estranged from him.

So the elder brother’s deepest problem is an **ungracious, self-righteous heart that trusts its own obedience and resents grace**. He is lost while thinking he is found—outside the father’s feast, needing repentance not just of anger, but of self-righteousness.
