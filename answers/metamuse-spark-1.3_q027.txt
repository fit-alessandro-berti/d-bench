It's not his *doctrine* that condemns him. It's his *posture*.

Luke tells you how to read it before the parable even starts:

> "he spake this parable unto certain which **trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and despised others**" - Luke 18:9

That is the diagnosis: self-trust + contempt. Everything else flows from that.

### 1. What the Pharisee gets wrong

His prayer isn't factually false. That's what makes it so sharp.

> "God, I thank thee, that I am not as other men are, extortioners, unjust, adulterers, or even as this publican. I fast twice in the week, I give tithes of all that I possess."

First-century Pharisees *did* fast twice a week (Monday and Thursday) — more than the Law required. They *did* tithe meticulously. He probably isn't lying.

He is condemned for what he *does* with true virtues:

**a) Pride as self-justification:** He trusts in himself *that he is righteous*. The Greek is *pepoithotas eph' heautois* — to have confidence upon oneself. God is addressed, but not actually needed. His opening "God, I thank thee" quickly becomes "I... I... I..."

Luke hints at this with a brilliant phrase: "The Pharisee stood and prayed *thus with himself*." In Greek, *pros heauton* can mean "to himself" or "with himself." Either way, it's a monologue, not a prayer. God is the audience for a resume.

**b) Righteousness by comparison:** He doesn't measure himself by God's holiness, but by being "not as other men are... or even as this publican." Holiness becomes relative superiority. If you can find someone worse, you feel justified.

**c) Contempt:** That comparison hardens into *exoutheneo* — the word in v.9 for "despised," literally to treat as nothing. He doesn't just notice the tax collector; he uses him as a prop: "even as *this* publican." In the temple, in prayer, he turns a fellow human into a contrast device to elevate himself.

**d) Virtue as self-display:** Fasting and tithing are good. Here they become currency. Thanksgiving is perverted into self-congratulation. He is not confessing dependence; he is presenting evidence.

In short: pride is the root, comparison is the method, contempt is the fruit, and self-display is the setting — prayer itself turned into performance.

Jesus isn't condemning gratitude, fasting, tithing, or moral effort. He's condemning a religion where God is no longer the justifier; the self is.

### 2. Why the tax collector is "justified"

> "And the publican, standing afar off, would not lift up so much as his eyes unto heaven, but smote upon his breast, saying, God be merciful to me a sinner."

Tax collectors were not romantic figures. They were collaborators with Rome, notorious for extortion. Jesus isn't saying his job is fine or that sin doesn't matter.

His posture is treated as spiritually truer for three reasons:

**1. Truthful anthropology:** He doesn't compare himself to others; he stands *coram Deo* — before God alone. No resume, no "at least I'm not..." Just: "a sinner." In Greek it's emphatic: *to hamartolo* — *the* sinner, as if he's the one. His body says the same thing: eyes down, far off, beating his breast — a sign of grief and guilt. No pretense.

**2. Correct theology of grace:** His prayer is *hilastheti moi* — "be propitiated toward me," "make atonement for me." It's the language of sacrifice, of mercy-seat. He doesn't bring virtue as payment; he asks for mercy as gift. He understands what the Pharisee doesn't: justification is something God *does*, not something I achieve.

**3. Right relationship to both God and neighbor:** The Pharisee is close to the temple center but far from God, and he pushes the other man further down. The tax collector stands far off socially and liturgically, but he doesn't push anyone down. He claims no superiority. That humility makes room for grace.

That's why Jesus concludes:

> "I tell you, this man went down to his house justified *rather than* the other: for every one that exalteth himself shall be abased; and he that humbleth himself shall be exalted."

The Pharisee went up to pray and only talked to himself. The tax collector could barely look up, and he was heard.
