The parable in Luke 18:9–14 is less a courtroom judgment on specific theological errors and more a surgical diagnosis of the heart’s orientation toward God. All four elements you mention—false doctrine, pride, comparison, contempt, and self-display—are present, but they converge on one root issue: **self-reliance instead of God-reliance**. The Bible never condemns moral discipline, religious practice, or social comparison *in themselves*; it condemns treating those things as substitutes for grace.

### What condemns the Pharisee?
The decisive factor is **the chief intent of the heart**, specifically the movement of the will toward self rather than toward God. The text repeatedly points to this:

1. **“Prayed *with himself*” (or “with himself”)** – The Greek implies a prayer that turns inward, making himself the center. His words begin with “I thank thee,” but the structure is essentially “I have done this, I am not that.” The posture reveals a heart that treats God as a witness to his own righteousness rather than the source of it.
2. **Comparison and contempt** – He measures himself against “other men”: extortioners, unjust, adulterers, and *this publican*. The contempt is explicit in his implicit judgment. Comparing others may not be inherently sinful, but using comparison to inflate oneself and to morally discount another is. Here it functions as a tool of self-justification.
3. **Virtue as self-display** – Fasting twice a week and tithing are not condemned; in fact, they were legitimate piety. The problem is the *witness* he seeks: he wants to be seen by God and implicitly by others as righteous. The religious acts become a script for his own reputation, not a means of receiving grace.
4. **Pride and self-righteousness** – This is the engine. Pride is not just “feeling superior”; it is trusting in one’s own status, effort, or possession rather than in God’s mercy. The Pharisee’s closing line, “I am not as this publican,” reveals that his theology is really his self-esteem. He does not ask God for mercy; he boasts in his distance from sin.

False doctrine is present in a limited sense (e.g., his implicit theory that God owes him a debtor-creditor relationship), but the parable’s sharpest indictment is **moral pride and the misuse of religious practice as a substitute for dependence on grace**. The comparison, contempt, and self-display are the outward expressions of that inward posture.

### Why is the tax collector’s posture spiritually truer?
The publican’s posture is not spiritually truer because it looks better, is more impressive, or adheres to a stricter rule. It is truer for three interconnected reasons:

1. **Awareness of need** – He stands “afar off” and does not lift his eyes to heaven. This is not mere physical distance but recognition that he cannot approach God on his own terms. The “smote upon his breast” is a physical expression of inner confession: “God, be merciful to me a sinner.” His words are brief, but they declare his total dependence on God’s mercy, not on his own merits.
2. **Acknowledgment of sin** – He names his state plainly: “a sinner.” In the ancient Jewish context, the publican was a tax collector, often viewed as a collaborator with Rome and a breaker of the law. By admitting this openly, he aligns himself with the reality of human fallenness, which the law and conscience already confirm. He does not minimize, rationalize, or compare.
3. **Trust in mercy, not merit** – His prayer ends with a request: “be merciful to me.” He does not offer a transcript of his virtues. He trusts that God’s righteousness is not something he earns, but something he receives. Jesus’ conclusion, “this man went down to his house justified rather than the other,” is decisive: justification comes through humility and received mercy, not through the self-construction of righteousness.

The contrast is not between religion and irreligion, but between **two different relationships to God**: one that leans on self (and thereby rejects grace) and one that leans on God (and thereby receives grace).

### Key Theological Takeaways
- **Righteousness is a gift, not a product.** The parable turns the common Jewish-Judean assumption that moral/religious performance makes one “acceptable” to God on its head. Justification is God’s declaration of favor, received by faith, not manufactured by comparison or piety.
- **Religious discipline is morally neutral until its direction is known.** Fasting and tithing can be acts of humility before God (as in the tax collector’s context) or performances of self-glory (as in the Pharisee’s). What matters is the direction of the heart.
- **Contempt and self-righteousness are spiritually corrosive.** The Pharisee’s “righteousness” does not save him; it isolates him. The tax collector’s humility does not diminish his worth; it opens him to God’s favor.
- **The parable is not primarily about “how to pray” but about “who we are before God.”** The posture of the body and the words of the mouth are simply the visible evidence of the invisible reality: whether we stand before God in self-assurance or in need.

In short, the Pharisee is condemned not by his theology or his deeds, but by the heart’s refusal to acknowledge its own need. The tax collector is justified not because his sin was smaller, but because he did not try to be righteous; he asked God to be merciful. That posture—bare, aware, and trusting—is the threshold of true righteousness.
