The danger is not visibility itself, but the motive that gives visibility its force.

In Matthew 6:1, the issue is not “do not be seen” but “do not do alms before men *to be seen of them*.” Giving in front of people is only condemned when publicity is the reason for giving. The danger is that the person’s aim becomes honor, praise, or reputation rather than service and love. In that case the alms are no longer offered as a response to God’s grace; they become a performance seeking recognition. That is why the verse says, “otherwise ye have no reward of your Father which is in heaven”: not because the act is invalid, but because the motive has been twisted.

So there are two distinct things:

- **Visibility**: public visible acts of kindness can be morally good, even necessary.
- **Being seen**: seeking to be seen is an inner craving for recognition; that is what redirects the value of the act away from God.

The verse is close to a psychology of motive. It distinguishes between “being seen” and “being known by God.” Honor among men can be fleeting or even poisonous; reward from God is secure precisely because it does not depend on human acclaim. In this light, Matthew is diagnosing righteousness that becomes self-justification.

That said, Matthew does not necessarily forbid all public witness. The same chapter concerns fasting, prayer, and alms all together: the problem is not outward piety, but piety performed for display rather than for God. So the verse’s real warning is: don’t turn love into a spectacle designed to earn praise. Public goodness is morally dangerous when visibility becomes the end in itself—or when it replaces, rather than serves, an inner posture of generosity.

A useful rule: visibility can be the medium of goodness; it becomes dangerous when it is the measure of goodness.
