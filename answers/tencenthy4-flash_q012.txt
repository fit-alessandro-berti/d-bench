Short answer: **mainly realistic about self-deception**—though it is deeply sobering about human nature. It is not saying, “Humans are so evil that nothing they say about themselves is true.” It is saying, **“The human heart is not a reliable final judge of itself.”**

In Jeremiah’s context, “heart” means more than emotions. It is the inner person: mind, will, desires, loyalties, moral orientation. “Reins” means the inward parts, the hidden center of a person. The point is that outward religious words and appearances can hide inward corruption. The heart rationalizes, conceals, excuses, and misreads its own motives.

The KJV phrase “desperately wicked” is often rendered “desperately sick,” “incurable,” or “beyond cure.” That nuance matters: the heart is not merely “mean” or “evil” in every impulse; it is morally wounded and self-deceiving in a way it cannot fully diagnose or repair by itself. “Who can know it?” is answered in verse 10: **the Lord**.

So the passage is not pessimistic about being human as such, nor does it deny genuine self-knowledge. We can know some things about ourselves—especially when Scripture exposes us. But it denies **self-trust as ultimate authority**. The heart is like an interested witness in a trial: it may say things that are true, but it cannot be assumed to be neutral.

For talking about motives, then:

- **Distinguish action, stated reason, and inferred motive.**  
  “What happened was X. The effect on me was Y. My guess about the motive is Z.”

- **Use provisional language.**  
  Say “It seemed to me,” “I may be wrong,” “One possible motive is,” rather than “I know exactly why you did that.”

- **Look at fruit and patterns, not one moment.**  
  Jeremiah 17:10 itself says God judges not only the heart but also “according to the fruit of his doings.” Observable conduct matters, even if we cannot see the heart exhaustively.

- **Practice self-suspicion without self-loathing.**  
  A mature person can say, “There was a good desire in that, but also some pride,” or “I wanted to help, but I also wanted to be seen as helpful.”

- **Invite correction.**  
  Ask trusted people, “What am I missing?” Pray, “Search me, O God” (Psalm 139:23–24). Let Scripture, community, and consequences expose what self-examination alone may miss.

- **Be slow to condemn others.**  
  “Only God knows the heart” should make us humble, not careless. It does not mean we ignore harmful behavior; it means we name behavior without pretending to have infallible knowledge of someone’s inner soul.

So Jeremiah 17:9–10 calls for neither cynicism nor naive introspection. It calls for **humility about motives, honesty about actions, and trust in God as the only perfect discerner of the heart**.
