Here is a clear, concise, biblically grounded, and pastorally sensitive answer to both parts of the question—**staying directly within the wording and context of Mark 8:34–35**, avoiding theological speculation beyond the text, and explaining the psychological/cultural reasons for the differing reactions:

> **"Deny oneself" in Mark 8:34 refers to *withholding self-interest, self-protection, and self-promotion as the primary guide for one's actions and identity* in everyday life. It means consciously choosing to prioritize Jesus' purpose ("follow me") and the gospel’s demands over one’s own comfort, safety, social standing, immediate desires, or personal agenda—even when doing so costs something tangible (hence "take up his cross"). It does *not* mean denying one’s existence, suppressing normal emotions, denying legitimate needs, or denying the reality of the self as a person created in God’s image. It is about *where loyalty and effort are directed* in practical, daily choices.  
>   
> It sounds liberating to some because it offers freedom *from* the constant, often hidden pull of self-centered motives—e.g., seeking approval, avoiding discomfort, pursuing personal gain, or clinging to safety at the expense of others or God’s call. For those weary of performing for others, hiding their true selves, or living in tension between values, "denying self" (i.e., consistently aligning action with Jesus’ way) can feel like relief: *“I no longer have to constantly negotiate ‘me vs. them’; I can finally act from a clear, purposeful direction.”* It resonates with a longing for authenticity, integrity, and purpose over self-promotion or reactive fear.  
>   
> It sounds offensive or difficult to others because it *requires concrete, often costly action* ("take up his cross," "lose his life"). In a culture (and everyday experience) that prioritizes safety, comfort, autonomy, practicality, and self-protection—especially when following Jesus might mean loss of job, social standing, relationships, or physical safety—the phrase can feel demanding, unrealistic, or even threatening. People may interpret it as: *“You must abandon what matters to me (my family, my livelihood, my dignity) without clear guarantees or easy alternatives.”* The phrase “lose his life” can also sound extreme or confusing (losing one’s life *for* Jesus *saves* it), which can trigger anxiety about sacrifice or misunderstanding of Christian commitment. Thus, it can feel offensive because it challenges deeply held practical priorities, raises legitimate concerns about feasibility/safety, and contrasts with messages that emphasize comfort, self-care, or gradual change.
