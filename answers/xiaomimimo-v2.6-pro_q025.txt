# The Craving to Be Seen, Not Visibility Itself

## Parsing the Key Language

The decisive evidence lies in the Greek. The phrase **"to be seen of them"** (πρὸς τὸ θεαθῆναι αὐτοῖς) is an infinitive of **purpose** — it tells us *why* the alms are being done. Jesus does not simply say *"don't give alms where people can see you."* He specifies the corrupting aim: giving **in order to be seen**.

This distinction is confirmed by the "otherwise" clause — *"ye have no reward of your Father which is in heaven."* If the mere fact of visibility damned the act, there would be no reason to mention reward at all. The problem is that the person has **already sought and received** a different reward: human admiration. The bank account of divine approval is untouched because nothing was deposited with Him in mind.

## Why Visibility Alone Cannot Be the Sin

Several lines of reasoning converge here:

- **Jesus himself acts publicly** — healing, teaching, feeding multitudes in full view. If visibility were inherently corrupting, His own ministry would be self-condemning.
- **The broader passage** (Matt 6:2–4) uses the word **ὑποκριταί** (hypocrites) — actors, performers. Their sin is performance for an audience, not location. The problem is theatricality of motive.
- **The parallel instructions** on prayer and fasting (vv. 5–6, 16–18) preserve this same structure: it is not that praying aloud or fasting outwardly is evil, but that doing these things **for the display** of them is.

## The Deeper Tension

That said, the verse carries a warning that the *craving* and the *visibility* are entangled in practice. The corrupt motive tends to **seek out** the stage. The person who craves recognition will naturally arrange to be noticed. So while the text locates the moral disease in the intention, it is careful to prescribe a remedy — *"in secret"* (v. 4) — that dismantles the conditions under which that craving feeds.

## The Structural Principle

The logic runs like this:

| Purpose | Result |
|---|---|
| Give alms **to be seen by men** | Human praise *is* the full reward (v. 2) |
| Give alms **in secret** | "Thy Father which seeth in secret shall reward thee" (v. 4) |

The framing reveals the underlying theology: **the act of goodness has two possible audiences, and the audience you perform for determines who pays you.** The moment you orient your action toward the gaze of human spectators, you have not merely *risked* losing God's reward — you have **deliberately chosen** a different one. Nothing was withheld from you; you simply traded upward.

## Conclusion

The problem is emphatically the **craving to be seen** — the subordination of the good act to the lust for recognition. Visibility is the *occasion* on which this craving operates, but not the sin itself. Jesus draws a line not between public and private acts as such, but between **Godward** and **humanward** intention. The moral danger arises when the goodness of the deed becomes merely a *means* to something else — the applause — and the deed itself is no longer loved for what it is.
