The verse does not say that moral distinctions are unreal or that we must never evaluate conduct. It targets a particular posture: the rush to sentence other people, as if their guilt were our to settle and our own were already clear.

Luke 6:37 sits inside a call to mercy. The next lines are about forgiveness, generosity, and the measure we use being the measure we receive. The point is reciprocal: the person who lives by condemnation invites condemnation; the person who lives by forgiveness is formed by it. That is a warning about the spirit in which we relate to others, not a claim that right and wrong have collapsed.

Discernment and the appetite to condemn part ways here.

**Discernment** asks what is true, what is harmful, and what love requires. It can name a lie, refuse a false teacher, protect the vulnerable, or say “this action is wrong.” In the same Gospel tradition, Jesus tells people to judge with right judgment and to know a tree by its fruit. Moral sight is assumed. Without it, forgiveness becomes sentiment and wisdom becomes naivete.

**The appetite to condemn** wants a verdict on the person: final, superior, and pleasing to the one who delivers it. It enjoys exposure more than restoration. It treats another’s failure as proof of one’s own cleanness. It confuses “I can see that this is evil” with “I am authorized to write this person off.” That appetite is what “judge not… condemn not” restrains.

So the difference is not between having standards and having none. It is between judging an act in order to tell the truth, and condemning a person in order to stand above them. The first can coexist with humility, because the judge knows they are also under judgment. The second cannot, because it needs the other person to remain the guilty one.

Mercy, on this reading, is not the suspension of moral reality. It is the refusal to turn moral reality into a weapon, and the willingness to want for others the same forgiveness one hopes to receive.
