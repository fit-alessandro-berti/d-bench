## First, the verse undoes the relativist reading by itself

Notice that Luke 6:37 *is* a moral judgment — an uncompromising one. It declares an entire human practice (condemning others) wrong, and prescribes another (forgiving) as obligatory. If moral judgments "never mattered," this command would collapse along with everything else — including the call to forgive. Whatever Jesus is forbidding, he is plainly not teaching that moral distinctions are unreal. The whole surrounding sermon is one long series of moral verdicts: blessings, woes, commands about enemies and generosity. He presupposes evaluation everywhere.

What he targets is something narrower and stranger: the human *delight in occupying the bench*.

## What is actually forbidden: the seat, not the sight

Two details in the text confirm this. Luke uses two different verbs — *krinō* (to sit in judgment, to put on trial) and *katakrinō* (to pronounce sentence). If judging simply meant condemning, the second clause would be redundant; the sequence *ascends*: refrain from the courtroom posture, refrain from the sentence, and go further — actively release (*aphiēmi*, the ordinary word for letting go, cancelling debts). Restraint is the floor; release is the ceiling.

Then verses 41–42 supply the operative image: the mote and the beam. Crucially, the parable does **not** deny that the mote exists. The brother really has a splinter in his eye, and once the beam is removed, the duty stands: *"then shalt thou see clearly to pull out the mote."* The named failure is *hypocrite* — a matter of asymmetric optics. Infinite magnification of another's flaw; infinite leniency toward one's own. Elsewhere the same Lord commands: *"Judge not according to the appearance, but judge righteous judgment"* (John 7:24); maturity is described as senses *"exercised to discern both good and evil"* (Hebrews 5:14).

The prohibition, then, is of the **seat**, not the **eyes**.

## Discernment versus the appetite

Discernment is perception in the service of love: it asks, slowly and accurately, *what is true here?* — and what it finds usually wounds the discerner first. The appetite to condemn is perception in the service of the self: it asks, quickly, *how am I confirmed by this?*

They differ at almost every joint:

- **Object:** Discernment evaluates deeds, claims, situations. Condemnation sentences *persons* — their past, their future, their essence. "That decision was unjust" versus "that person is beyond the pale, and always was."
- **Epistemics:** Discernment weighs evidence and revises. Condemnation seizes appearances and closes the file; it loves rumor and resents verification (John 7:24).
- **Sequence:** Discernment begins at home — beam before mote; *"considering thyself, lest thou also be tempted"* (Galatians 6:1). The appetite never looks inward; there is always a convenient speck available.
- **Affect:** Discernment grieves what it sees — *"rivers of waters run down mine eyes"* (Psalm 119:136); Jesus weeps over Jerusalem. The appetite *savors*. There is a tell-tale flicker of pleasure when someone falls. Accuracy cannot launder that delight; 1 Corinthians 13:6 draws the line sharply: love *"rejoiceth not in iniquity."*
- **Aim:** Discernment works toward repair — *"gained thy brother"* (Matthew 18:15). The appetite works toward distance, exposure, diminishment.
- **Payment:** Discernment *costs* — attention, patience, honesty. Simone Weil called attention "the rarest and purest form of generosity." Condemnation *pays instantly*: the sweetness of superiority, the belonging of the crowd, and above all the relief from one's own ledger.

Why does it pay? Romans 2:1 explains the mechanism: *"wherein thou judgest another, thou condemnest thyself."* Every verdict on another smuggles in an implicit self-acquittal — we appear in the transcript as prosecutor, which *feels like* innocence. That is the appetite's wage. C.S. Lewis noted that pride gets no pleasure from having anything, only from having more of it than the next man; condemnatory judgment is the cheapest available method of manufacturing that margin.

## Why the reciprocal promises aren't threats

*"With the same measure that ye mete, it shall be measured to you again"* (Luke 6:38) is less a threat than a description of formation. The measure you habitually use becomes the perceptual organ through which reality reaches you. A heart trained in condemnation builds an indicted world — and loses the capacity to *receive* mercy, because it now reads everything, including grace, as prosecution. That is the elder brother keeping the ledger outside the feast: not barred from joy by decree, but incapacitated for it by habit. As James puts it, *"he shall have judgment without mercy, that hath shewed no mercy; and mercy rejoiceth against judgment"* (James 2:13). Those trained in release, meanwhile, develop the very muscles that recognize forgiveness when it is finally offered to them.

## Five working tests

1. **Log test:** Did self-examination precede the observation, or get skipped?
2. **Grief test:** At news of another's fall — did I grieve, or did I relish? (Check the micro-pleasure.)
3. **Aim test:** Does my verdict move toward that person's restoration, or toward their exposure? Am I trying to heal a brother or perform for an audience?
4. **Measure test:** Would I consent to this verdict-procedure applied to myself, with all facts known?
5. **Finality test:** Am I settling an account that only God can close — motives, interiors, endings (1 Corinthians 4:5: *"judge nothing before the time"*)?

One safeguard on the other flank: none of this licenses the denial of real harm. Naming cruelty, abuse, or injustice is part of loving the victim — which is why wielding this verse to hush the wounded inverts its meaning. Accountability has proper venues; what is forbidden is the private tribunal of the heart.

## The deepest ground

John 3:17 supplies the reason the seat is off-limits: *"God sent not his Son into the world to condemn the world."* The one being who had every right to pronounce sentence deferred it — and absorbed it instead. Human beings volunteering for the portfolio the Incarnation itself declined is trespass at maximum irony.

Discernment remains ours, because truth demands it and love is impossible without it. Condemnation belongs to God alone, because only omniscience can read a heart and only sovereignty owns the ending of anyone's story. What we are given is not the gavel. It is attention.

The eyes were made for seeing. The seat was never offered.
