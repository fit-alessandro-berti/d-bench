In Isaiah 55:6–7, the pairing of the **“way”** (*derek*) of the wicked and the **“thoughts”** (*maḥăšābôt*) of the unrighteous presents a holistic definition of biblical repentance (*teshuvah* / *shuv*—literally "turning" or "returning"). 

By addressing both the interior mind and the exterior life, the passage reveals the root-and-fruit nature of sin, the depth required for genuine transformation, and the profound basis for divine mercy.

---

### 1. Outward Conduct: Forsaking the “Way”
The Hebrew word *derek* refers to a beaten path, a journey, or a habitual lifestyle. 
* **Action and Habitation:** Forsaking one's "way" means abandoning the visible, practical patterns of sin—unjust business practices, violence, neglect of the vulnerable, idolatry, and selfish living.
* **The Reality of Moral Responsibility:** It implies that sin is not merely a theoretical flaw; it leaves footprints in the world. Repentance cannot remain an abstract feeling; it must manifest in concrete, observable cessation of harm and a reorientation of daily conduct.

### 2. Inward Imagination: Forsaking “Thoughts”
The Hebrew word *maḥăšābôt* encompasses schemes, deliberations, intentions, worldviews, and imaginations.
* **The Root of Action:** Outward sin begins as an inward conception. Long before a person walks in an evil "way," they have entertained the "thought"—justifying selfishness, harboring resentment, imagining autonomy from God, or devising deceit (cf. Prov. 4:23; Matt. 15:19).
* **Guarding Against Hypocrisy:** Mere behavioral modification is insufficient. A person can conform outwardly to religious rules while inwardly harboring bitterness, pride, or lust. Forsaking one's "thoughts" requires dismantling the internal justifications, idols of the mind, and false narratives that fuel sinful behavior.

### 3. The Interconnection: Total Reorientation
Why must both be forsaken?
* **They form a continuous loop:** Inner thoughts give birth to outer ways, and repeated outer ways reinforce inner thought patterns. 
* **The Whole Person is Addressed:** God does not seek partial reform—neither a purely external morality (which is legalism/hypocrisy) nor a purely internal, sentimental piety that produces no practical change (which is antinomianism). Genuine "returning" (*shuv*) to the Lord involves the redemption of the intellect, desire, imagination, and bodily action.

---

### 4. What This Implies About the Possibility of Mercy
The second half of verse 7 moves from the requirement of repentance to the promise of grace: *“and let him return unto the Lord, and he will have mercy upon him; and to our God, for he will abundantly pardon.”*

1. **Mercy is Radically Accessible, but Morally Real:**
   * Mercy is not cheap indulgence. It is granted to those who truly surrender both their outward autonomy ("ways") and their inward pride ("thoughts"). 
   * However, no one is excluded because of how corrupt their ways or thoughts have been. The invitation is extended universally to the "wicked" and "unrighteous."

2. **Pardon is "Abundant" (*Yarbeh Lisloach*):**
   * The text stresses that God will *multiply* pardons. Human beings often struggle to forgive repeated or deep-seated offenses, but God’s capacity to forgive exceeds the sinner's capacity to transgress.

3. **Preparation for Divine "Thoughts" (The Broader Context):**
   * Just two verses later, God declares: *“For my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways”* (Isa. 55:8–9). 
   * Forsaking our fallen ways and imaginations creates the space to receive God’s higher ways and thoughts. Mercy is not merely a legal acquittal; it is an invitation to have one's entire mind and walk elevated into communion with God.
