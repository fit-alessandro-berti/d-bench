Hebrews calls bitterness a **root**, not merely an act, because it is not just something a person occasionally *does*; it is something that grows *in* a person and from which acts eventually come.

### Hidden moral growth
A root is underground, unseen, and central to the life of the whole plant. By the time bitterness becomes visible—harsh words, slander, withdrawal, division—it has usually been growing for a long time. The image suggests that:

- Resentment often begins with real hurt, then is fed by rehearsed grievances, self-justification, envy, or unforgiveness.
- A person may appear outwardly fine while the inner life is becoming poisoned.
- Bitterness is not only a bad mood. It is a growing moral condition, a settled way of interpreting people and God.

So the warning is about the heart, not just behavior. The visible fruit may be only a symptom of a much deeper problem.

### Delayed damage
Roots develop slowly, and damage is often done long before it is noticed. A bitter root can lie beneath the surface while a person seems strong, devout, or even stable. Then, under pressure, it “springs up” suddenly—revealing years of hidden decay.

That delayed quality makes bitterness especially dangerous. It may appear in a relationship, a church, or a whole community like a sudden crisis, but the crisis is late-stage fruit. By then, the damage is not merely external; it may have distorted someone’s view of God, grace, forgiveness, and other people.

### Spread to others
The image also suggests contagion. Roots spread. One “root of bitterness” is not contained; it “troubles” others and “defiles” many. Resentment rarely stays private. It spreads through:

- gossip and complaint,  
- suspicion and cynicism,  
- faction-building,  
- recruiting sympathy,  
- hostility toward leaders or seemingly innocent people.

One person’s unresolved bitterness can begin to shape the mood of a whole community. Others may become infected with the same resentment, or they may be forced into sides, distrust, and conflict. That is why Hebrews says “many be defiled.”

The larger context matters: pursue peace and holiness. Holiness has to do with root-level health. Therefore, the command to “look diligently” means not merely watching for occasional sinful acts, but watching for early signs of hidden resentment—grudges, wounded pride, refusal to forgive, cynicism about grace—so that the root can be exposed and removed before it bears its poisonous fruit.
