Ezekiel is not contrasting outward obedience with mere inward sincerity, as though actions did not matter. He is contrasting **behavior imposed on an unchanged person** with **obedience arising from a person renewed by God**.

### External compliance and inward renewal

Someone may keep commandments outwardly because of fear, social pressure, habit, pride, or self-interest while remaining resistant to God within. Such compliance can restrain evil and may even be socially valuable, but it does not necessarily involve trust, love, or willing allegiance.

A “new heart” concerns more than emotions. In biblical anthropology, the **heart** is the center of thought, desire, decision, and loyalty. Its renewal therefore means a new orientation of the whole person: God’s will is no longer only an external demand but something the person is increasingly disposed to understand, love, and choose. Nor does this mean instant perfection or the end of struggle.

Crucially, Ezekiel does not set inner renewal against practical obedience. Verse 27 joins them: God puts his Spirit within the people, **and** they walk in his statutes, keep his judgments, and do them. The new heart is not an excuse to disregard God’s commands; it is what makes obedience willing and fruitful rather than merely constrained.

### Why “stone” and “flesh”?

The material imagery communicates the **depth of the problem**:

- **Stone** suggests rigidity, insensibility, and resistance. God’s word can be heard without making any impression; the person remains unmoved by correction or appeal.
- **Flesh** here suggests something living, soft, and responsive. It does **not** carry the later Pauline sense of “flesh” as sinful human nature, nor does it imply that the physical body is bad.

The metaphor also explains why replacement language is so strong. Polishing stone, surrounding it with rules, or forcing it into a different shape does not make it living tissue. Likewise, more information or external pressure alone cannot produce the responsiveness God seeks. Something more radical than behavioral adjustment is needed.

The repeated divine **“I will”** emphasizes that this renewal is ultimately God’s gift, not an achievement of self-reformation. Yet the imagery should not be pressed into a literal theory that sin is a physical substance or that people bear no responsibility. It is prophetic language for entrenched moral and spiritual resistance.

In its original context, this is God’s promise to cleanse and restore exiled Israel. Its basic logic is profound: **God does not merely demand a different life; he promises to renew the people from whom that life must come.**
