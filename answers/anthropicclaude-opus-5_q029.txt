## The posture, in brief

Paul does not simply tell the Galatians *that* they should correct one another; almost every word specifies *how*, and the how is not decoration on the act. The verse assembles a whole moral stance out of four elements: an address (*brethren*), a description of the offense (*overtaken in a fault*), an aim (*restore*), a manner (*meekness*), and a discipline of self-attention (*considering thyself*).

**The offense is framed as a stumble, not an identity.** The word behind "fault" (*paraptōma*) means a false step, a slip. And "overtaken" (*prolambanō*) suggests being caught — either caught out by others, or caught unawares by the sin itself, ambushed. Either way the grammar is passive: something happened *to* him. This is not a naive minimizing of guilt; it is a refusal to let a single visible act become a summary of the person.

**The aim is repair, not verdict.** *Katartizete* is the word used of mending nets and, in medical usage, of setting a fractured bone. It answers the question "what is correction *for*?" The answer is: for function, for wholeness, for the person's return to usefulness. Anything that satisfies the corrector without repairing the corrected has missed the object entirely.

**The credential is not office but condition.** "Ye which are spiritual" sounds like an elite until you remember what Paul has just said the Spirit produces — and *meekness* (*prautēs*) is in that list (5:23). So the qualification for correcting is possession of exactly the temper that makes correction survivable. The people entitled to do this are the people unlikely to enjoy it.

**Then the grammar narrows.** "Ye which are spiritual" is plural; "considering thyself" is singular. The community acts, but the scrutiny is individual and non-transferable. And the verb *skopeō* means to keep watch, to spy out, to fix the eye upon — the same searching gaze that has just been trained on the offender is swung around onto oneself.

## Why the three cannot be separated

Each of the three collapses into a vice when isolated. That is the practical reason Paul binds them.

*Restoration without meekness* becomes surgery with a hammer. The stated goal is healing, but the method produces shame, and shame hardens rather than mends. One can pursue a man's repentance in a way that guarantees he will not repent — or will repent to you rather than to God, which is worse.

*Meekness without restoration* becomes collusion dressed as kindness. Paul is not recommending softness about truth; this is the man who withstood Peter to the face and called the Galatians foolish. Gentleness that leaves the brother lying in the ditch is not gentleness; it is cowardice with good manners, or indifference that has learned to sound tender.

*Self-suspicion without either* becomes paralysis — "who am I to speak?" — which is frequently self-protection in humble clothing. It costs nothing and risks nothing and lets the community rot politely.

*Restoration and meekness without self-suspicion* is the most plausible counterfeit, and the most dangerous. Here the aim is right and the tone is right, but the corrector has stepped outside the human situation. He stands as the well toward the sick. And this is precisely the position from which the fall comes, because — Paul says — "lest thou *also* be tempted." Not merely "lest you commit the same sin later," but: the very act you are performing is itself a site of temptation. Judging another's failure is one of the most reliable sources of self-congratulation available to a religious person. The moment you have genuine standing to correct is the moment of maximum danger to that standing.

## The deeper logic

Self-suspicion is not only a brake on arrogance; it is the *source* of intelligent gentleness. If you know from the inside what it is to be overtaken — how quickly, how stupidly, how without deciding — then meekness is not a technique you adopt for the occasion but an accurate perception of the facts. You are gentle because you are not confused about where you stand. Conversely, a corrector who has no working knowledge of his own liability will always misjudge the situation, because he is missing the main datum: that he and the offender are the same kind of creature.

So the three are one posture, not three requirements. Self-knowledge yields meekness; meekness makes restoration possible; restoration is what love wants in the first place. Remove any one and the others deform.

## The Galatian point

There is a reason this comes at the *end* of this particular letter. Paul has spent five chapters against teachers who used other people's compliance as material for boasting — "that they may glory in your flesh" (6:13). Correction performed from above, that enlarges the corrector, is the Galatian heresy reproduced in miniature at the level of temperament. Having demolished a legalism of doctrine, Paul closes by inoculating against a legalism of *manner*: you can be right about grace and still relate to your brother as a creditor.

Hence what immediately follows — bear one another's burdens (v. 2), and if anyone thinks himself to be something, he is nothing (v. 3), and let each prove his own work (v. 4). The correction Paul envisions is not a transaction between the upright and the fallen. It is one burdened man getting under the weight of another, and the only ones fit for it are those who have felt the weight.
