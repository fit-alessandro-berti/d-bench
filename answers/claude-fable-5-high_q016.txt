The elder brother's deepest problem is not any single emotion on the list but the thing underneath all of them: he has been living with his father on the terms of a contract rather than a relationship. Anger, envy, wounded fairness, and pride in obedience are all symptoms; discomfort with mercy is the point at which the underlying disease finally becomes visible. But the root is that he has never actually understood — or wanted — sonship. He has understood wages.

Consider how the text exposes this, layer by layer.

1. The symptoms on the surface: anger and envy.
"He was angry, and would not go in." The anger is real, and the envy is audible in "thou never gavest me a kid... but thou hast killed for him the fatted calf." Yet anger and envy are reactive emotions; they tell us something has been touched, not what that something is. A man is angry when he believes he has been robbed. The question is: what does the elder brother think he has been robbed of, and why does he think it was owed?

2. The next layer down: wounded fairness and pride in obedience.
His own speech gives the diagnosis: "Lo, these many years do I serve thee, neither transgressed I at any time thy commandment." Notice the vocabulary. He does not say "Father"; he does not say "I have lived with thee" or "loved thee." He says "I serve thee" — the language of a hired hand, ironically the very status the prodigal rehearsed asking for ("make me as one of thy hired servants") and was never allowed to finish requesting. The elder brother has, in effect, made himself the hired servant his brother only proposed to become. His obedience has been real, but it has functioned as an invoice. Years of compliance were quietly accruing as credit, and the fatted calf reveals that the father has been keeping no such ledger. That is the "unfairness" that wounds him: not that he received nothing, but that the accounting system he had staked his identity on turns out not to be the currency of his father's house.

His pride in obedience is the same fact seen from the other side. "Neither transgressed I at any time thy commandment" is a self-assessment no honest person can make — and the very speech in which he makes it is itself a transgression: he refuses his father's invitation, shames him publicly by staying outside the feast, and repudiates his brother ("this thy son," he says, not "my brother"). His flawless record is a self-image, and the celebration for the sinner threatens the self-image more than it threatens his inheritance.

3. The deepest layer: mercy exposes that he never knew his father.
This is why "discomfort with mercy itself" is the closest of the five options to the heart of it — but even that needs sharpening. Mercy offends him because mercy is unearnable, and everything the elder brother has is, in his own mind, earned. Grace to the prodigal doesn't merely benefit someone else; it retroactively devalues the elder brother's entire strategy of life. If the father's love can be had for nothing — can be had even after squandering everything with harlots — then twenty years of joyless service purchased nothing that wasn't freely available all along. Mercy to his brother feels like an insult to himself, and that feeling is diagnostic: only a wage-earner is insulted by a gift given to someone else. A son who loved his father would share the father's joy, because he would care about what the father cares about — "this thy brother was dead, and is alive again."

The father's reply confirms this reading with surgical gentleness. "Son, thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine." Both halves answer a complaint the elder brother didn't know he was making. "Thou art ever with me" — the reward was never going to be a goat or a calf; the reward was the presence itself, which he possessed the whole time and evidently never valued. "All that I have is thine" — legally true (the inheritance was already divided, v.12) and relationally true; nothing was withheld. He never received a kid to make merry with his friends because he never asked, and he never asked because his frame was servitude, and servants wait to be paid rather than enjoy what is theirs. Strikingly, his fantasy of joy is "make merry with my friends" — away from the father. Even his imagined happiness excludes the person he claims to have served. The prodigal wanted the father's goods without the father's presence and left home to get them; the elder brother wants the same thing while staying home. Both sons are lost; only one knows it.

So, to answer directly: anger and envy are the surface; wounded fairness and pride in obedience are the mechanism; discomfort with mercy is the revelation. The deepest problem is the loveless, mercenary heart those all point to — he relates to his father as an employer and to goodness as leverage, and therefore he cannot rejoice, cannot go in, and cannot recognize either his brother ("this thy son") or himself (a son who is "ever with" a father he does not enjoy). Mercy is intolerable to him because a house run on grace has no use for his ledger, and the ledger is who he has decided to be.

The parable's open ending — we never learn whether he goes in — is the final stroke. Jesus told the story to the murmuring Pharisees and scribes (Luke 15:2), the elder brothers of Israel, and left the door of the feast standing open. The father comes out and entreats him, just as he ran out to the prodigal: both sons must be met outside, and both are invited in on identical terms — not wages, but welcome. Whether the elder brother's problem proves fatal depends on whether he can bear to be loved for nothing.
