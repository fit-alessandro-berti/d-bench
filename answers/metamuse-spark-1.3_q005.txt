Enemy-love is difficult to take seriously precisely because Jesus doesn't mean it unseriously.

"Love your enemies, bless them that curse you, do good to them that hate you, and pray for them which despitefully use you" — those are not feelings. They are verbs. Bless, do good, pray. That's what makes the command so unsettling.

Here is why we tend to dismiss it:

**1. It violates our moral wiring.**

We live by reciprocity: be good to those who are good to you, punish those who hurt you. That feels like justice. Enemy-love feels like betraying justice, betraying ourselves, and betraying the victims. How can you love a persecutor, an abuser, an occupier — in Jesus' day, quite literally the Romans — without saying what they did didn't matter?

So we reclassify it as impossible idealism, something beautiful for Sunday but unworkable for Monday, for war, politics, divorce courts, and trauma.

**2. It sounds sentimental.**

When modern culture hears "love," we hear "like, affirm, feel warm toward, keep close." That version of enemy-love *is* sentimental — and impossible, and even unhealthy. You cannot feel affection on command for someone who cursed you.

But Jesus is not talking about sentimentalism. The Greek word is *agape* — willed good toward another. It doesn't mean: approve them, trust them, reconcile without repentance, stay in harm's way. It means: refuse to reduce them to their worst act, and will their ultimate good even while you resist their evil.

You can love an enemy and still set a boundary, tell the truth, seek justice, leave, or even go to war to stop him. Love does not equal niceness. Niceness avoids conflict. This engages it differently.

**3. It can be strategic — but it's not *merely* strategic.**

There is a strategic wisdom to it. Martin Luther King Jr., Gandhi, Nelson Mandela all saw that returning hate for hate just guarantees the cycle continues. Doing good to an enemy can shame him, disarm him, free *you* from being defined by him.

Paul echoes it later: "if thine enemy hunger, feed him... for in so doing thou shalt heap coals of fire on his head" (Romans 12:20). Kindness burns the conscience in a way revenge never can.

But Jesus in Matthew 5 doesn't ground it in "because it works." He grounds it in "because that's who God is."

> "for he maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good, and sendeth rain on the just and on the unjust."

Sun and rain in an agrarian world were life itself, and God gives them indiscriminately. That's what theologians call common grace. God is not indiscriminate about right and wrong — but He is impartial in His generosity. To be "children of your Father" is to family-resemble that: a strength that can give without needing the recipient to deserve it.

That's why it's best understood as a deeper form of moral strength.

Sentimentality is weak because it collapses when hurt. Strategy is conditional because it stops when it doesn't work. What Jesus describes is different:

* The strength not to mirror your enemy. Hate makes you become what you hate.
* The strength to hold justice and mercy together. "I will stop you *and* I will pray for you" is harder than either "I will destroy you" or "I will excuse you."
* The strength to be free. The one who curses you no longer controls your inner life. You can bless, do good, pray — you are acting, not just reacting.

Nietzsche called this slavish and weak. In reality, try it: the next time you are cursed, instead of ruminating or retaliating, deliberately pray for the person's good. You will find it is not weak at all. It takes a whole different muscle.

That is likely why Jesus puts it at the heart of the Sermon on the Mount. He isn't asking us to pretend evil is not evil. He is asking us to refuse to let evil set the terms for what love is.
