## The logic of the argument

John's "how can he" (*pōs dynatai*) is an argument from the easier case to the harder, but it works in two directions at once, and the tension between them is where the verse gets its force.

On the surface: the brother is the *easier* object of love. He is visible, near, of the same kind, made of the same flesh; natural affection, kinship, and reciprocity all assist. God is unseen, and to love what you have never seen would seem to require a rarer and more spiritual capacity. If a man fails at the easy thing, he cannot be doing the hard thing.

But turn it around and the deeper point appears. Loving an unseen God is, in one respect, far *easier* — because it encounters no resistance. God never interrupts me, never has an unpleasant manner, never wrongs me, never asks me to forgive an actual injury or share actual money. He never appears in the form of a person I find tedious or offensive. The unseen God can be loved entirely within my own head, on terms I supply. The brother cannot. He is the point at which love meets a will other than my own, and therefore the point at which love must either become real or reveal itself as imagination.

So the seen brother is not the lower rung of a ladder to God. He is the only available *testing ground*, because he is the only object of love who can contradict my account of myself.

## Why this is a test and not merely an illustration

John is not making a rhetorical flourish about consistency. In his theology the two loves are not two acts but one.

"God is love" (4:8), and "love is from God" (4:7); we love only "because he first loved us" (4:19). Love of God is therefore not an achievement of human religious aspiration but the presence in a person of God's own love, the fruit of being "born of God." That single reality cannot be present in one direction and absent in the other. John makes the same point with family logic in 5:1: whoever loves the parent loves the child. You cannot love the Father while despising those who bear his likeness and carry his life.

And crucially, 4:12 has just said: "No one has ever seen God; if we love one another, God abides in us and his love is perfected in us." The invisibility of God is not a gap that pious feeling must leap over. It is a gap that God himself has filled by making his love visible in a community of concrete people. The brother is where the unseen God becomes an object of actual love. To refuse the brother is not to fail a secondary duty while fulfilling the primary one; it is to refuse the only place the primary duty can be performed. Hence 4:21: the commandment is *one* — love God *and* love your brother.

This is also why love in this letter is always defined by a bodily act before it is demanded of us: "he laid down his life for us, so we ought to lay down our lives for the brothers" (3:16); "let us not love in word or talk but in deed and truth" (3:18). Love has an incarnate shape or it has no shape.

## The self-deception exposed

The verse belongs to a repeated formula in the letter: *if anyone says* — "I know him" (2:4), "I abide in him" (2:6), "I am in the light" (2:9), "we have fellowship with him" (1:6), "we have no sin" (1:8). Each claim is followed by a life that contradicts it. John's opponents were almost certainly not cynics. They were confident, sincere, spiritually ambitious people who had left the community — presumably convinced that their fellowship with God was of a higher order than that of the people they abandoned.

The self-deception has several layers:

**An unfalsifiable claim mistaken for a fact about oneself.** Religious affection is easy to produce and impossible to disprove. Intensity of feeling toward God feels like evidence, and there is no counter-evidence available, because the object never answers back. So a man can carry a warm conviction that he loves God for decades while the actual state of his will toward other people goes unexamined. The claim is not tested; it is merely *felt*, and the feeling is taken for the thing.

**Self-love wearing the name of God.** To love an unseen God whom I have furnished with my own preferences, causes, and standards is very often to love my own conception — which is to say, to love myself at one remove. The tell is hatred of the brother, especially the brother who is wrong, embarrassing, insufficiently pure, on the other side of a dispute. The zeal that feels like love of God and produces contempt for persons is frequently love of one's own rightness. John's word "liar" is unsparing precisely because the mouth and the life are saying opposite things, whatever the man's sincerity.

**Hatred as a form of blindness.** John has already said that "the hatred has blinded his eyes" (2:11). Hatred does not merely coexist with the false claim; it helps generate it. Having refused love, a man needs an account of himself that makes the refusal invisible, and exalted piety is available for exactly that purpose. The profession of love for God becomes the covering for the refusal of love to the neighbor.

**A refusal of the visible as such.** In this letter the test of love and the test of Christology are woven together: confessing Jesus Christ "come in the flesh" (4:2–3) sits immediately beside love of the brother (4:7–12). That is not accidental. A spirituality that wants God without flesh wants a God who costs nothing and encounters nothing. The same impulse that dissolves the incarnate Christ into pure spirit dissolves love of God into pure feeling — and in both cases what is discarded is the concrete, the visible, the neighbor who is actually there.

## What the test does and does not establish

Note the direction: the verse is a *negative* criterion. Hatred of the brother falsifies the claim to love God; it does not follow that affection for people proves love of God, since natural kindness, temperament, and mutual usefulness can look identical from outside. John uses love of the brothers positively as evidence of new life (3:14), but as fruit, not as a substitute. God is not reducible to the neighbor, nor the neighbor to a means.

The pastoral consequence is uncomfortable and precise. If I want to know whether I love God, the question is not how I feel in prayer or how orthodox my convictions are. It is whether there is someone I have written off, someone whose need I see and step around (3:17), someone I would not sit at a table with. That is the place where the invisible God has made himself available to be loved — and where my answer, whatever I say, is already on record.
