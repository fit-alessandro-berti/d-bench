Light is hated in this passage because it reveals what darkness allows people to hide—not only from others, but from themselves. If someone’s deeds depend on secrecy or self-deception, truth can feel less like a gift than a threat.

The passage suggests a movement from **exposure to shame to avoidance**. To come into the light is to risk having one’s actions “reproved”: recognized and judged for what they are. That can bring guilt—*I have done wrong*—but also shame—*If I am seen clearly, I will be rejected*. Darkness offers an apparent refuge from both. It lets a person preserve a flattering story about themselves or continue benefiting from actions they do not want examined.

That helps explain why the light might be *hated*, not merely feared. When truth threatens something we love or rely on, it can seem like the enemy. A person may resent the one who tells the truth, question the truth itself, or retreat further into concealment. The problem is not that light creates the wrongdoing; it makes denial harder.

By contrast, “he that doeth truth cometh to the light” describes more than knowing correct facts. Truth is something **lived**: a willingness to let one’s life be examined and, where necessary, changed. The passage does not say such a person comes to display perfect self-sufficiency. Their deeds are made manifest as “wrought in God”—their life is open to God’s work rather than protected by secrecy.

This distinction matters because not every desire for privacy is a love of darkness. People may fear being seen because they have been humiliated, abused, or judged harshly. Honest exposure requires safety and wisdom; it is not a demand to reveal everything to everyone. In the surrounding passage, John says that God sent the Son not to condemn the world but to save it (John 3:17). The light is therefore both revealing and invitational: it uncovers what is wrong so that a person need not remain hidden.
