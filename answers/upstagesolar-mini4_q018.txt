To understand why bitterness and forgiveness are placed so directly against each other, and how the text categorizes bitterness, we have to look at the Greek grammar, the logical flow, and the theological context of the passage.

### 1. The Grammatical Structure: A Cascade of Corruption
The first sentence is a cumulative list that describes a progression of sin. The Greek words are often paired or chained to show intensity and escalation:
*   **Pikria (Bitterness):** The root of all the others.
*   **Oryza (Wrath) / Charin (Anger):** Emotional explosions that stem from bitterness.
*   **Kenantia (Clamour) / Kako-logia (Evil speaking):** The verbal and social explosions of that emotional state.
*   **Kakologia (Malice):** The underlying intent to harm.

The cumulative command—*"put away... with all malice"*—suggests that these are not isolated incidents but a systemic condition. Paul treats bitterness not as a fleeting mood, but as a **chosen posture** that, if left unchecked, metastasizes into a culture of destruction within the church. It is a "house" that needs to be gutted, not just a "furniture" item that needs to be moved.

### 2. Bitterness as a Contagious Posture (The "We" Context)
The passage begins with *"Let... be put away **from you**."* This is key. It implies that bitterness is communal and contagious. In the context of Ephesians, the church was likely under pressure from outside persecution and internal tension. Bitterness can easily become a shared sentiment among believers if they begin comparing their suffering or blaming one another.

By placing forgiveness directly against it, Paul addresses the **social dynamic**. Bitterness says, *"I will remember your debt to me and keep it against you."* Forgiveness says, *"I release you because Christ released me."* The text implies that if the church remains bittersweet, it will fracture. If they forgive, they remain united. Therefore, the "contagious" element is the primary risk: a single bitter root can poison the whole "you."

The second half of the verse provides the **motive** for forgiveness: *"even as God for Christ's sake hath forgiven you."*

This is the crucial theological engine. The text is not saying, *"Forgive others because they deserve it."* It is saying, *"Forgive others because **you** have been forgiven."*
