## Short answer

The verse does both, but its syntax and its command-logic tip it toward *trains* — with "reveals" as the other half of a single loop. The common diagnostic reading ("show me your checkbook, I'll show you your heart") isn't wrong, but it's only the indicative half of what Jesus says. The imperative logic of the passage depends on the heart being able to *follow* the treasure.

## What the grammar is doing

Notice the tense sequence, which the KJV actually preserves: "Where your treasure **is**, there will your heart **be** also." Greek: *estin* ... *estai*. The treasure takes up residence first; the heart arrives afterward. The treasure is the fixed point ("where"), the heart is the dependent variable being located. We'd naturally expect the reverse order — "where your heart is, there you'll send your treasure" — because that's how desire makes its first investment. Jesus states it in the direction of *consolidation*.

Then look at what Jesus commands and what he leaves uncommanded. He issues no imperative to the affections — no "love God more." He commands the hands and the ledger: *lay up* (v. 20), *stop laying up* (v. 19). The *gar* of v. 21 is the warrant for the command: redirect the investment *because* the heart will follow the money. You cannot command the heart directly; you can command what the heart feeds on.

This is why the command-logic matters. If v. 21 were purely diagnostic — treasure merely mirroring a heart already settled — the imperative would have no leverage; you'd have to heal the heart first and let the treasure trail behind. But Jesus puts the lever where we actually have access: the will can decide *today* where to invest, before the affections consent. The affections arrive later, by a slower train. This is Thomas Chalmers' old insight about "the expulsive power of a new affection": an old love is never evicted by prohibition; it is displaced by a new object becoming wealthier to us. Hence the command is constructive, not merely negative — and the dative "for yourselves" (*hymin*) survives into v. 20. Jesus isn't condemning self-interest; he's indicting our investment performance.

## Reveals and trains as one loop

The present-tense "where your treasure *is*" still states a standing fact, so the verse works as an audit: to find your heart's address, read your outflows. The two readings are therefore two phases of a feedback loop — desire makes the first investment, investment then trains desire, and the loop tightens until heart and treasure are fused and "your heart" has moved into the storehouse and lives there. Luther's gloss on the First Commandment names the diagnostic endpoint: whatever your heart clings to and trusts, *that* is your God. Treasure is functional deity.

## How possession, attention, and love get knotted

The passage implies a whole psychology, and the surrounding verses make the chain explicit:

1. **Possession extends the self.** "Lay up for yourselves" — treasure is the self projected into things, gathered and secured. We are present in what we own, which is why losing property wounds like losing a limb.

2. **Perishable possession conscripts attention.** The three corrosives are chosen with care: moth devours garments (clothing was a primary form of ancient wealth); *brōsis* — literally "eating" — corrodes grain and metal; thieves *dig through* (*dysōsin*) the mud-brick walls where valuables were kept. Every form of earthly wealth comes with its bespoke parasite. To own the perishable is to be enrolled as its sentry. Possession under threat is vigilance without discharge.

3. **Attention is the hinge.** The next unit (6:22–23) makes this explicit: the eye is the lamp of the body, and what the eye fixes on fills the body with light or darkness. Treasure captures the gaze, and the gaze is the channel through which ownership interiorizes into affection. You cannot love what you never look at; you cannot stop loving what you must watch.

4. **Love is a residence, not a feeling.** *Kardia* translates Hebrew *lēb* — the seat of the whole person, will and allegiance included. "There will your heart be" means the self takes up dwelling in its storehouse. Augustine states the physics: *pondus meum amor meus* — "my weight is my love; wherever I am carried, it is my love that carries me." Treasure is the gravity of the heart.

5. **Love crystallizes into service, and service into anxiety.** v. 24 — you cannot serve God and Mammon, with money personified as a rival lord. vv. 25–34 — anxiety is the emotional receipt for an earthly treasury: the standing dread of the sentry guarding what cannot be guarded. Conversely, treasure "in heaven," where moth and rust and thieves have no jurisdiction, is the only storehouse that doesn't demand watchfulness — freeing the heart to rest rather than patrol.

The chapter's larger context sharpens this. 6:1–18 is all about *audience*: practicing righteousness to be seen by others ("they have their reward") versus before "your Father who sees in secret." Heavenly treasure is the Father's reward (cf. 5:12) — which is to say, communion with the one who beholds you. Bonhoeffer pushed this to its end: the treasure in heaven is Christ himself.

## The synthesis

The passage's anthropology in one line: we do not merely possess our treasures; over time our treasures possess us — attention is the acquisition mechanism, and love is the closing of the sale. The indicative diagnoses (treasure reveals where love already lives), and the imperative trains (relocate the treasure and the heart will migrate, because the heart is not commanded directly; it is fed through what we attend to, and we attend to what we must guard).

And this is good news rather than a trap, because the law runs both ways. The same gravity that drags the heart into the granary drags it after alms, prayer, and the God who rewards in secret. Luke's parallel (12:33–34) makes the mechanism surgical: "Sell your possessions and give alms; provide yourselves bags which wax not old." The physical release of money is the operation by which the heart is transplanted.
