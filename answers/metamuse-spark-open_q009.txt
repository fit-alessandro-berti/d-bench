This passage does not define victory as defeating your enemy, getting revenge, or even getting an apology. It defines it as **not becoming what you hate.**

> *Be not overcome of evil, but overcome evil with good.* - Romans 12:21, KJV

Paul is quoting Proverbs 25:21-22 in verse 20, and his argument in Romans 12:17-21 is a single unit:

> 19 Dearly beloved, avenge not yourselves, but rather give place unto wrath: for it is written, Vengeance is mine; I will repay, saith the Lord.
> 20 Therefore if thine enemy hunger, feed him; if he thirst, give him drink: for in so doing thou shalt heap coals of fire on his head.
> 21 Be not overcome of evil, but overcome evil with good.

### 1. How Victory Is Defined

For Paul, there are two possible losses and one true victory:

**1. To be overcome *by* evil:** Evil is treated as an active, contagious power. If someone does evil to you and you retaliate with evil - hatred for hatred, insult for insult, violence for violence - evil has not just hurt you, it has infected you. You have been conformed to it. That is defeat, even if you "win" the fight.

The verb for overcome is *nikao*, a military term meaning to conquer, vanquish, prevail. Paul is saying evil is trying to conquer you.

**2. To overcome evil *with* good:** Victory is not passive endurance, but active counter-action. The "good" here is not abstract well-wishing, it is concrete: feed him, give him drink. You meet a real need of a real enemy.

This redefines victory in three ways:
* **Moral victory over self:** you retain moral agency instead of letting the enemy dictate your character.
* **Redemptive victory over the other:** you break the cycle of retaliation and create the possibility for the enemy's transformation, rather than just his punishment.
* **Theological victory by trust:** you entrust justice to God, *Vengeance is mine; I will repay, saith the Lord* (v.19). You don't have to be both victim and judge.

In short: Victory is not that evil is punished through you, but that evil is disarmed and transformed through you.

This was written to Christians in Rome - a powerless minority under Nero - for whom armed revolt or legal revenge was neither possible nor Christlike. Paul's ethic mirrors Jesus' in Matthew 5:44: *Love your enemies.*

### 2. Is the Command Weak, Manipulative, or Strong?

#### Is it weak?
No. In both Greco-Roman and modern culture, feeding an enemy looks like weakness, a doormat ethic.

But in Paul's logic it requires far more strength than retaliation. Retaliation is instinctive and easy. To absorb harm without passing it on, to discipline your anger, and to take initiative to do costly good to someone who does not deserve it requires self-control, courage, and inner freedom.

It is not "let evil win." It is a refusal to win on evil's terms.

#### Is it manipulative?
This question comes from the strange phrase: *thou shalt heap coals of fire on his head.*

If that means "be nice to him so God will punish him more severely," then kindness would be passive-aggressive manipulation - a weaponized niceness.

That is almost certainly not what Paul means. Scholars note three main interpretations of the "coals" image from Proverbs:

1. **Pangs of conscience leading to repentance:** Fire often symbolizes burning shame or conviction. Your unexpected kindness brings shame that might melt his hostility and lead him to repentance. This is the majority view.
2. **An ancient Egyptian ritual:** Some commentators cited a custom where a penitent person carried a pan of coals on his head as public sign of shame. Your goodness would bring the enemy to that place of repentance. The evidence for this custom is weak, and most modern scholars have abandoned it.
3. **God's judgment:** The coals are God's final judgment, which you leave in God's hands rather than taking yourself. In doing good, you leave his punishment to God.

In all three, the point is *not* "do good in order to hurt him more." Romans 12:19-20 forbids revengeful motive. The coals are the *result* in God's hands, not your motive. Your motive must be genuine *agape* love, as in Romans 12:9: *Let love be without dissimulation.*

If you are kind only to make someone feel guilty or to store up divine payback, you have already been overcome by evil, because your "good" is just disguised malice.

#### Is it genuinely strong?
Yes. This is why this ethic is considered the heart of Christian non-retaliation.

1. **It retains power:** The victim is not powerless. He has the power to initiate good, to not mirror evil.
2. **It exposes evil:** Unreturned good unmasks evil for what it is. While two people trading insults both look the same, one-sided goodness makes injustice visible.
3. **It imitates God:** Paul's ethic is grounded in Romans 5:8 - *while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us.* God overcame human evil not with retaliatory evil, but with sacrificial good on the cross. The command is to practice the same divine logic you have received.

Therefore, "overcome evil with good" is not optimism that every enemy will change. Some will not. But it ensures that evil does not multiply - at least it stops with you - and it leaves the door open for evil to be transformed into good, rather than guaranteeing it perpetuates itself.

> **Summary:** In Romans 12:20-21, victory is not defeating your enemy *with* evil, but defeating evil *itself* - both in your enemy and in yourself - with good. It is not weakness, nor a clever technique for revenge. It is costly, active strength that refuses to be conformed to evil while trusting God for ultimate justice.
