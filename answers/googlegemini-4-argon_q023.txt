In Isaiah 55:6–7, the prophet provides one of the richest and most foundational definitions of repentance in the Hebrew Bible. By pairing **"way"** (Hebrew: *derek*) and **"thoughts"** (Hebrew: *machashavot*), Isaiah reveals that true repentance is not a superficial adjustment of behavior, nor is it merely an internal feeling of regret. It is a total, holistic reorientation of the human person. 

Here is an exploration of why both are required and what this implies about human nature and divine mercy.

---

### 1. Why Repentance Involves Both “Way” and “Thoughts”

In Hebrew poetic parallelism, pairing two concepts often serves to show two sides of a single reality, representing the totality of a thing. By mentioning both “way” and “thoughts,” Isaiah covers the entire spectrum of human existence: the **external** and the **internal**.

*   **The “Way” (*Derek*):** This refers to a trodden path, a manner of life, or habitual conduct. Sin is not just a series of isolated mistakes; it is a direction one is walking in. To forsake one’s "way" is to change one's outward habits, practices, and trajectory.
*   **The “Thoughts” (*Machashavot*):** This word goes deeper than passing ideas. In Hebrew, it refers to plans, devices, intentions, and imaginations—the blueprint of the mind. 

Repentance requires forsaking both because **outward actions are the fruit of inward thoughts.** Jesus later echoed this exact theology when He said, *"For out of the heart proceed evil thoughts, murders, adulteries..."* (Matthew 15:19). If a person forsakes their "way" without forsaking their "thoughts," they have only achieved behavioral modification or hypocrisy. Conversely, if someone claims to change their "thoughts" while continuing in their wicked "way," they are living in self-deception.

Furthermore, looking at the immediate context (Isaiah 55:8–9), God immediately follows this call to repentance by saying: *"For my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways."* Repentance, therefore, is the act of abandoning our broken ways and thoughts so that we can adopt God’s ways and thoughts.

---

### 2. Implications for Outward Conduct

The inclusion of "way" implies that **grace is never an excuse for passivity.** Spiritual transformation must become visible and tangible in the physical world. 

*   **Repentance is Active:** The word "forsake" (*azav*) means to leave behind, abandon, or let go. It implies a decisive break with the past. 
*   **Breaking Habits:** Because a "way" is a worn path, forsaking it means stepping off the easy, familiar ruts of sin—whether that involves dishonesty, injustice, or mistreatment of others—and walking in a new direction (the Hebrew word for "return," *shuv*, literally means to turn around and walk back).
*   **No Gnostic Detachment:** One cannot claim to have a "good heart" before God while living a destructive outward life. Outward conduct is the proving ground of inward sincerity.

---

### 3. Implications for Inward Imagination

The inclusion of "thoughts" implies that **God is not merely a behavioral policeman; He is the searcher of hearts.** Morality is not just about what we do when others are watching; it is about what we cultivate in the privacy of our own minds.

*   **The Sanctification of Desire:** Our "imagination" is the workshop where we construct our idols, nurse our resentments, fantasize about sin, and plot our self-sufficiency. Isaiah implies that we are morally responsible for what we allow our minds to dwell upon.
*   **Worldview and Ambition:** "Thoughts" also encompass how we view the world—our cynicism, our pride, and our belief that we can be the masters of our own destiny. An "unrighteous man's thoughts" might also include false thoughts about God (e.g., thinking God is too harsh to forgive, or too distant to care).
*   **Getting to the Root:** By demanding we forsake our thoughts, God invites us to pull sin up by the roots rather than merely mowing the weeds of our outward behavior. 

---

### 4. Implications for the Possibility of Mercy

At first glance, demanding that humans forsake not only their bad actions but their very *thoughts* seems terrifyingly impossible. Who can completely control their own mind? If this is the prerequisite for mercy, is anyone eligible?

Paradoxically, this high standard actually **magnifies the depth and possibility of God’s mercy** in three profound ways:

**A. Mercy Reaches the Deepest Corruption**
If God only called us to change our outward ways, His mercy would only be skin-deep—a legal pardon for external crimes. Because He calls us to bring our *thoughts* into this transaction, it implies that His mercy is capable of cleansing our subconscious, our imaginations, and our secret shame. He offers not just a clean slate, but a clean mind.

**B. Repentance is Relational, Not Meritorious**
Notice the progression in verse 7: *Forsake* $\rightarrow$ *Return unto the Lord* $\rightarrow$ *He will have mercy*. We do not clean up our thoughts *in order to earn* God's mercy; rather, forsaking our thoughts is the act of turning toward Him so we can receive it. You cannot walk toward God while walking away from Him. Forsaking sin and returning to God are two sides of the same coin. 

**C. God’s Mercy Exceeds Our Sin ("Abundantly Pardon")**
The Hebrew phrase translated "abundantly pardon" literally means **"He will multiply to forgive."** Why does God have to *multiply* pardon? Because our wicked ways and unrighteous thoughts are multiplied! Every day, our minds generate countless selfish, anxious, and unrighteous thoughts. Isaiah reassures the seeker that no matter how deeply ingrained their habits ("ways") or how twisted their internal life ("thoughts"), God’s capacity to forgive is infinitely greater. 

In fact, when God says in the very next verse, *"My thoughts are not your thoughts,"* He is speaking primarily of His mercy. Human thoughts are petty, vengeful, and calculating; we struggle to forgive even small offenses. God’s thoughts are vast, generous, and eager to pardon the worst of sinners the moment they turn back to Him. 

### Summary
Repentance in Isaiah 55:6–7 involves both "way" and "thoughts" because God desires the whole person. It implies that our outward conduct must be decisively altered, our inward imagination must be surrendered, and that God's mercy is so vast that it can heal and forgive us from the inside out.
