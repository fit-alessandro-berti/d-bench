## One motion, two directions

The opposition in verses 31–32 is not decorative. Bitterness and forgiveness are set face to face because they are rival answers to the *same question* — what do you do with a real debt? — and the text even uses the same physical gesture for both. "Let it be *put away*" (v. 31) belongs to the same gesture-family as the New Testament's characteristic forgiveness verb, *aphiemi* — to send away, release, cancel a debt (as in "forgive us our debts," Matt 6:12). Both are the motion of lifting something off and letting it go. Which means the verse is not asking you to do two things. It is saying that you cannot put bitterness down empty-handed. You put it down by handing over the account.

And note what forgiveness is called: *charizomenoi* — "gracing one another," from *charis*, grace, the same root as God's act toward you in the next clause. Forgiveness here is not calm; it is generosity. Bitterness is the economy of desert (holding the account open until it is paid); grace is the economy of gift (closing the account at your own cost). You cannot hold and release the same debt simultaneously — hence the direct opposition. Hebrews 12:15 makes the pairing explicit in a single sentence: "lest any fail of the *grace* of God... lest any root of *bitterness* springing up... and many be defiled."

## The career of bitterness

Your four options aren't rivals — the text treats bitterness as all four *in sequence*, a trajectory:

**A feeling — at first, and involuntarily.** *Pikria* comes from *pikros*, sharp to the taste, the opposite of sweet. Bitterness is spoiled sweetness: you do not grow bitter over what you never loved. It is the aftertaste of wronged love, and the text does not condemn the wound.

**A habit — if it is renewed daily.** This is why verses 26–27 sit just upstream: "Be angry and sin not; *let not the sun go down* upon your wrath, neither give *place* to the devil." Anger is granted a sunset clause. Bitterness is what wrath becomes when it outlives the sunset — anger rehearsed nightly until it has a *topos*, a settled address, and the address becomes the devil's. Paul's parallel in Romans 12:19 says it flatly: "give no *place* to wrath." Bitterness is wrath with a lease. The command "let it be put away" would be cruel if bitterness were only a feeling; it is merciful precisely because bitterness is a tenancy — a feeling that has been given lodging.

**A posture — when the tenancy calcifies.** Watch the vice list's internal trajectory: bitterness (the hidden root) → wrath and anger (the heat) → clamor (the raised voice) → evil speaking (the story told about the offender) → "with all malice" (*kakia*, the settled ill-will beneath them all). Bitterness is the headwater; the rest are downstream. As posture, it changes the eyes — suspicion, scorekeeping, the ledger carried unopened into every new relationship.

**Contagious — and the Bible says so in its own metaphor system.** Notice that Scripture consistently imagines bitterness botanically and hydrologically: a root bearing gall and wormwood *among the congregation* (Deut 29:18), a root that springs up and defiles *many* (Heb 12:15), a fountain that cannot send forth sweet and bitter water at the same place (Jas 3:11), the "gall of bitterness and bond of iniquity" (Acts 8:23). Roots and springs spread through shared soil and water — that is, through the common life of a community. And the mechanism is narration. Bitterness is the one pain that seeks an audience: pain isolates, but bitterness *recruits*. It retells the offense until others carry the grievance too. Clamor and evil-speaking in the list are exactly this — bitterness going public, looking for corroboration. Even Jesus' parable of the unforgiving servant ends with contagion: the fellow servants *saw* and *were grieved* and reported it (Matt 18:31). Unforgiveness is always witnessed.

## Why forgiveness, and what kind

The counter-practice is specified with three markers: "even as God, *in Christ*, forgave *you*." The standard (God), the cost (in Christ), the direction (toward *you* — the former enemy, Rom 5:8–10). God's forgiveness was not a feeling that eventually arrived; it was an act performed at cost while reconciliation was still undeserved, and the feeling followed the act. So the command here has the same governance logic as the sunset clause: it does not wait for bitterness to dissolve. It performs the release — and trusts the heart to catch up.

The "even as... *you*" clause also quietly repositions the forgiver. In the transaction being invoked, you were the offender. My grievance against my brother is being measured on a ledger where my own enormous account was cancelled (Matt 18:23–35). That memory is the text's leverage against the posture: both bitterness and Christian forgiveness are narrative practices — the question is which story governs. Bitterness rehearses what *they* did to me; forgiveness rehearses what *God* did for me.

One more reason the opposition is so direct: the context is body-language (4:25, "members one of another"), and verse 30 stands immediately before the list — "grieve not the Spirit." The Spirit is the seal of the unity bitterness saws at. Bitterness is anti-body; grace is the body's circulation.

So: the text treats bitterness as a *career* — feeling becoming habit becoming posture becoming plague — and forgiveness as a *practice*. You interrupt a career with a practice, one sunset at a time. As Chrysostom put it on this passage: nothing makes us so like God as being ready to forgive.
