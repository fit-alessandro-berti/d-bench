The direct juxtaposition is rhetorical as much as moral: “put away” names a whole inward-outward economy of grievance, and “be kind… tenderhearted, forgiving” names the opposite telos. If the verse were only about a fleeting mood, the command would be much weaker and much easier to satisfy. It is not framed as, “Don’t feel angry once in a while.” It is framed as a change in formation: what belongs to the “old man” is to be removed; what belongs to the forgiven new member is to be produced.

So bitterness is best treated as a disposition with an affective core, not merely a passing feeling. The list moves from inside to outside: bitterness, wrath, anger, clamor, and evil speech, “with all malice.” That is not the vocabulary of private emotion alone. It is the vocabulary of speech and social order. The preceding verses make that explicit: “put away lying,” “speak truth with your neighbor,” “we are members of one another.” Bitterness is therefore already implicated in lying, slander, strife, and public harm.

It is also a habit/posture rather than a secret thought. Because the command is “be put away” and then “be kind… tenderhearted… forgiving,” the text is urging identity and practice, not just degree of sensitivity. But it is not simply a voluntarist “decision.” Ephesians does not turn forgiveness into a technique of willpower. It grounds the command in God’s forgiveness “in Christ.” The ordinary Christian is not commanded to manufacture tenderness from scratch; the text assumes that forgiveness is formed under grace.

“Contagious” is useful if handled carefully, but not in a literal biomedical sense. Bitterness does not spread like a virus; it spreads through speech, suspicion, complaining, gossip, conflict, and the atmosphere it makes. What makes it hard is not only that it hurts the victim but that it locks the offender/victim into a posture where grievance becomes identity: “I was wronged,” “I must be protected,” “they still owe me.” Forgiveness is placed immediately against this because bitterness is the opposite of receiving mercy as a gift. To forgive is to reorient oneself from keeping the wound as power into being reintegrated with the neighbor.

So the text treats bitterness as: affective in origin, habitual in structure, communal in effect, and spiritual in consequence. It is not a mere feeling to be “released,” nor a simple habit to be broken, nor only a posture chosen at the will’s edge. It is the poisoned configuration of speech and relation that blocks the body of Christ’s new character. Forgiveness is not placed beside it as an optional personality improvement; it is placed beside it as the direct counter-formation.
