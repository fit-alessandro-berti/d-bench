Matthew 12:34–35 tightly binds speech to the heart’s hidden moral wealth. Jesus says that the mouth speaks “out of the abundance of the heart,” and then pictures the heart as a treasury or storehouse: a good person draws forth good things from a good treasure, and an evil person draws forth evil things from an evil treasure.

In other words, speech is not treated as merely surface noise. It is an outflow from what has been stored within. The heart is the moral center of the person—the seat of desire, intention, allegiance, and character—and words are what happen when that inner stockpile overflows into public life.

So, to your question: the passage suggests that words are symptoms, choices, and revelations, though each category functions slightly differently.

### 1. Words are symptoms: they show what is inside

The metaphor of “abundance” or “overflow” makes words sound almost diagnostic. Just as a tree is known by its fruit, a person’s speech reveals the condition of the heart. Jesus has just said:

> “Either make the tree good, and its fruit good; or make the tree bad, and its fruit bad; for the tree is known by its fruit.”  
> — Matthew 12:33

Then he applies this to speech:

> “Out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaks.”

This means words are symptoms in the way fruit is a symptom of a tree’s health. They are not random. They disclose a deeper condition. A person who repeatedly produces bitter, malicious, deceitful, or arrogant speech is revealing that there is a bitter, malicious, deceitful, or arrogant treasury within.

The Pharisees’ accusation against Jesus—claiming that he casts out demons by the power of Beelzebul—is the immediate example. Their words are venomous because, Jesus says, they are a “brood of vipers.” The speech is not an isolated mistake; it is symptomatic of a corrupted moral condition.

### 2. Words are revelations: they make hidden treasure visible

The word “treasure” is important. A treasure is something stored up, hidden, accumulated. Jesus is saying that every person carries an inner storehouse. The heart collects and stores loves, loyalties, resentments, desires, habits, judgments, and affections.

Speech is the act of drawing from that storehouse.

A person may keep the heart hidden for a time, but words expose it. When someone speaks, the hidden treasure becomes partially visible. The mouth makes public what the heart has been holding privately.

This is why Jesus can say that an evil person “out of the evil treasure bringeth forth evil things.” The evil words are not created from nothing at the moment. They are brought out of something already present within.

In this sense, words are revelations. They reveal the moral contents of the heart. They show what a person treasures.

### 3. Words are choices: they are morally accountable acts

At the same time, Jesus does not treat words as involuntary spasms. He rebukes the Pharisees. He calls them a “generation of vipers.” He holds them responsible. The surrounding context makes this even stronger:

> “But I say unto you, That every idle word that men shall speak, they shall give account thereof in the day of judgment. For by thy words thou shalt be justified, and by thy words thou shalt be condemned.”  
> — Matthew 12:36–37

If words were merely mechanical symptoms with no moral agency, judgment would make no sense. But Jesus treats words as deeds. They are spoken by responsible persons. They express the will, not merely the temperament.

This is the paradox of the passage: words flow from the heart, but they are still chosen acts of the person. The heart inclines the person toward certain kinds of speech, but the person is not an innocent bystander when the mouth speaks.

Jesus’ rhetorical question is striking:

> “How can ye, being evil, speak good things?”

This suggests a kind of moral inability. Because they are evil, they cannot easily or genuinely produce good speech. Their heart corrupts their words. Yet Jesus still addresses them as responsible moral agents. Their inability does not excuse them; it exposes how deep their corruption is.

So words are choices, but choices shaped by the heart’s dominant condition.

### The main point: speech discloses the heart’s moral abundance

If we put the three ideas together, the passage says something like this:

> Words are chosen acts that overflow from the heart and thereby reveal what the heart contains.

They are:

- **Symptoms**, because they show the inner condition.  
- **Revelations**, because they make hidden treasure visible.  
- **Choices**, because they are morally accountable expressions of the person.

The dominant emphasis is revelatory and diagnostic. Jesus is exposing the Pharisees. Their blasphemous words show that their inner treasury is corrupt. But the moral force of the passage depends on the fact that they are still responsible for those words.

### Practical implication

This passage does not merely call for better speech management. It calls for heart transformation. If words are fruit, then merely trimming the branches is not enough. The root must be changed. If words are withdrawals from a treasury, then the question is not only “What did I say?” but “What am I storing up?”

Good speech is not finally a matter of clever self-control. It flows from a heart that has been filled with good treasure. Evil speech, likewise, is not merely a bad habit; it is evidence of a deeper moral stockpile.

So yes: in Matthew 12:34–35, words are symptoms, choices, and revelations. They are the visible fruit, the chosen utterance, and the public disclosure of the heart’s hidden wealth.
