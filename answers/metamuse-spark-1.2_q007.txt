This verse is often read as *"moral judgments never matter and all behavior is equal"* - but that is not what Jesus is saying. In fact, if moral judgments never mattered, the second half of the verse would be meaningless.

If there is nothing truly wrong, there is nothing to *forgive*.

> Forgiveness only makes sense if a real wrong has occurred.

Luke 6:37 is not a call to moral blindness. It is a call to moral *clarity* without a hunger to destroy.

### 1. What "Judge Not" Does NOT Mean

In context, Luke 6:27-36 is Jesus teaching on loving your enemies and being merciful *as your Father is merciful.* Verse 37 is the application.

The Greek helps here. Two different words are used:

*   **Krino - "Judge"** - to separate, to decide, to play the judge, jury, and executioner. In this context, it means to assume the position of the final judge over a person - to render a verdict on their worth and destiny.
*   **Katadikazo - "Condemn"** - to pass final sentence. To condemn someone *down*, to declare them hopeless and worthy of punishment.

Jesus is not forbidding *evaluation*. He is forbidding *condemnation as an identity and as a pleasure.*

Elsewhere, Jesus himself commands moral discernment:

> "Judge with right judgment." John 7:24
> "You will know them by their fruits." Matthew 7:16
> "If your brother sins, rebuke him." Luke 17:3

The early church was explicitly told it *must* make moral judgments within its community - 1 Corinthians 5:12. Without discernment, you cannot love people well, protect the vulnerable, or even know what to forgive.

### 2. The Difference: Discernment vs. The Appetite to Condemn

They look similar on the surface, but their source, aim, and taste are opposite.

| **Discernment** | **The Appetite to Condemn** |
| :--- | :--- |
| Says: "This *action* is false, harmful, or unjust." | Says: "This *person* is false, harmful, and worthless *because* of their action." |
| **Separates** the person from the problem to see clearly. | **Fuses** the person with the problem to dismiss them entirely. |
| Aim is **truth and redemption.** "Is this right? Is this healthy? What will heal this?" | Aim is **superiority and punishment.** "How can I prove I am right and you are lower than me?" |
| Is humble: "I could be wrong. I am also in need of mercy." | Is proud: "I would never do that. I am qualified to write you off." |
| Leaves the door open. | Slams the door and locks it. |
| Grieves over wrong. | *Enjoys* wrong being exposed. |

Discernment is like a doctor's diagnosis. Condemnation is like a judge's final sentence.

A doctor must say, "This is cancer, and cancer is dangerous" - that is discernment. That judgment *matters* immensely, because without it there is no treatment. But the doctor does not say, "You *are* cancer, you are disgusting, and I enjoy telling you that you will die."

The appetite to condemn is not hunger for justice. It is hunger for *status*.

It savors catching someone, sharing the story, feeling morally elevated, and watching them shrink. It uses another person's sin as food for our self-righteousness. Jesus confronts that appetite directly in Matthew 7:3-5 - we obsess over the speck in another's eye while ignoring the log in our own, because focusing on the speck *feels good*.

### 3. Why Moral Judgments Still Matter - Deeply

Jesus takes morality *more* seriously, not less. That is why forgiveness is needed.

1.  **Without judgment, forgiveness is theater.** To say "I forgive you" you must first be able to say "What you did was wrong and it hurt." A cheap "it doesn't matter, don't worry about it" is not forgiveness - it's avoidance.
2.  **Without judgment, love is blind.** Love cannot be good if it cannot tell good from evil. A parent who cannot discern that lying, stealing, or cruelty is harmful cannot love or correct a child.
3.  **Without judgment, mercy has no weight.** The mercy of Luke 6:36 - "Be merciful, as your Father is merciful" - is profound precisely because God *does* see clearly, judges truly, and *still* chooses mercy.

Jesus is not saying, "Pretend you don't see." He is saying, "See clearly, and then refuse to do what only God can do - declare a person's final worth and close off their future."

God's judgment is perfect because He sees the whole heart, the whole story, the whole motive. Ours is always partial and biased. When we claim the final gavel, we play a role we are not qualified to play.

### 4. How to Tell Which One You're Doing

Ask yourself three diagnostic questions in the moment you feel judgment rising:

**1. What is my emotion?** Discernment often feels sober, even sorrowful. The appetite to condemn feels energized, titillated, self-righteous, or contemptuous. Contempt is the tell.

**2. What do I want to happen next?** Discernment wants restoration, truth, boundaries, and healing. Even if it requires strong boundaries or consequences, it hopes for repentance and wholeness. Condemnation wants the person to be shamed, canceled, and diminished so I feel bigger.

**3. What measure am I using?** The verse ends with a principle of reciprocity: "with the measure you use, it will be measured to you." If I want my own worst moments to be treated with context, patience, and hope for change - am I offering that same measure to others?

> Discernment: "That was wrong. You are more than what you did. How do we make it right?"
> Condemnation: "That was wrong. You are what you did. And I will make sure no one forgets it."

Luke 6:37 is an invitation to live without the burden of being everyone else's final judge. You are free to see clearly, name what is wrong, set wise boundaries, and still release the person from your private prison of contempt - forgiving as you have been forgiven.
