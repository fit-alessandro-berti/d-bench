## Why it's so hard to take seriously

The command offends us at the level of grammar. Nearly every moral system humans have lived by is built on reciprocity—desert. Good to friends, harm (or at least withdrawal) from enemies isn't a failure of morality in that grammar; it *is* morality. Aristotle says the man who is a friend to his friends' enemies is a nullity. Jesus doesn't add a rule to this system; he breaks it. So we instinctively file the command somewhere safe.

Then there's the economics of hatred. Enemies are useful: they define the tribe, simplify the world, and certify our own goodness by contrast. In a polarized age, contempt for the right people is a credential. The command asks us to surrender something psychologically valuable—righteous anger is genuinely pleasurable—and socially valuable besides. Nobody resents a command because it's easy.

Finally, it has a bad record of being quoted. Admiring readers have spiritualized it into a private inner niceness that costs nothing. Powerful readers have weaponized it, urging the abused to "love" their abusers in the sense of staying quiet. Both misuses give everyone reason to distrust the verse.

## Sentimental? No—the text forbids it

The love commanded here is not a feeling. It is four verbs—*love, bless, do good, pray*—matched precisely against four escalating hostilities: curse, hate, revile, persecute. The Greek *agapaō* in this context means willed benefaction toward someone actively willing your harm. King, who had fire hoses and dogs sent against him, insisted it meant "creative, redemptive goodwill"—explicitly *not* affection, and explicitly not weakness. Sentiment does not survive a Birmingham jail.

Notice also the hidden premise of the command: "Vengeance is mine; I will repay" (Rom. 12:19). You can only leave your enemy to God if you're confident God will actually deal with him. Otherwise you *must* take justice into your own hands. Enemy-love, as Miroslav Volf argues from the wreckage of the Balkans, is not softness about evil. It is a refusal to become evil's administrator.

## Strategic? It has effects—but that can't be its engine

Enemy-love does disarm, shame, and sometimes convert; Paul's "heap coals of fire on his head" (Rom. 12:20) gestures at this, and nonviolent movements have built real leverage on it. But two problems kill the strategic reading.

First, if love is a tactic, it's merely hostility with better manners—kindness as a weapon is manipulation. And it collapses the moment the enemy fails to convert, because it was aimed at outcome, not at the person.

Second, the passage's stated motive isn't effectiveness but *resemblance*: "that ye may be the children of your Father." The proof text offered isn't "it works"—it's "this is what God is like." In the ancient world, rain was read as verdict: harvest was reward, drought was punishment (Deut. 28). Jesus says the sun rises and the rain falls on the unjust *anyway*—providence is not pay. That subverts an entire retributive cosmology. Nobody deserves weather; it is pure gift, rationed by nothing. The disciple is not commanded to be effective but to be as unrationed as that.

## What it actually is: the strongest thing asked of anyone

It's strength for at least three reasons.

**It's a third way between fight and flight.** Retaliation mirrors the enemy—his single unbeatable move is reproducing himself in you. Withdrawal abandons both him and justice. Love stays engaged without mirroring, which takes more fortitude than either. This is why King could say that nonviolent resistance was not for cowards; violence is the easier discipline.

**It keeps the enemy human.** Hatred is a form of cognitive simplification—it turns a person into a category. To pray for the one who reviles you is to keep seeing a face where hatred had installed a function. Solzhenitsyn's line cuts here: the line dividing good and evil runs through every human heart—which means loving the enemy is also refusing the lie that evil is wholly *over there*.

**It is participation, not achievement.** "While we were enemies, we were reconciled to God" (Rom. 5:10). God's love *is* enemy-love; the disciple's enemy-love is the family resemblance—"children of" being the Hebrew idiom for character, not merely status. And Luke's parallel to Matthew's "be ye therefore perfect" is illuminating: Luke says "be ye therefore *merciful*" (Luke 6:36). The perfection in view isn't spotlessness; it's wholeness of love—a love that, like rain, isn't indexed to desert.

## Honest caveats

The command is addressed to the wronged as a discipline of their own hearts—not to comfortable bystanders as a gag for the wronged. It does not forbid flight, testimony, resistance, or justice: Paul appealed to Caesar; King fought Bull Connor politically while refusing to hate him. Aquinas held that willing the enemy's good is compatible with his correction. The aim of the struggle changes—not to destroy the enemy but to end the enmity; King's Beloved Community, not annihilation. And Bonhoeffer, who taught the Sermon with total seriousness and then joined a plot against Hitler, shows the tragic depth here: not a man who found the command easy, but a man who stood under it, took responsibility, and let himself be judged.

## So why the difficulty?

We find enemy-love hard to take seriously because we've pre-classified it as soft or clever—and both classifications are evasions. Sentimentality lets us admire it without obeying it; strategy lets us use it without being changed by it. The real thing is neither. It is the refusal of the enemy's final victory—the insistence that no one's cruelty gets to author who I am—offered on the grounds that the Father himself has never once rationed his rain. Every sunrise is the argument.
