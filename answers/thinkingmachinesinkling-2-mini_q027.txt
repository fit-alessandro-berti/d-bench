The text condemns several things at once, but they are not equal rivals. The root problem is **self-trust as righteousness**—pride dressed as virtue. The other things follow from that root.

- **False doctrine** is present only in a broad, implicit sense: he treats his spiritual standing as secured by his own record rather than given by mercy. But Luke does not give us an argument over a doctrinal formula. The Pharisee is not primarily condemned for “believing wrongly” in the way a false theological theory might be; he is condemned for the **shape of his prayer**.
- **Pride** is the most exact answer to “why is he condemned.” He prays to himself. “God, I thank thee, that I am not…” turns God into the witness of his superiority. Prayer becomes self-validation.
- **Comparison** is not automatically evil. Comparison can humble; it becomes deadly when it is asymmetrical—“not like these others,” or “I am better than this unclean man.” The Pharisee’s comparisons are upward self-exaltation, not honest self-examination.
- **Contempt** is named explicitly in the frame: these are “certain which trusted in themselves… and despised others.” The detailed story makes that explicit: he looks toward his virtuous self and, by contrast, down toward the publican. “Extortioners, unjust, adulterers” are not merely listed as facts but used to mark him as above them.
- **Virtue as self-display** is the mechanism. Fasting twice a week and tithing are not themselves the sin. But in this prayer they function less like fraternal acts of worship or embodied obedience and more like credentials offered to an audience—a Supreme tribunal whose verdict is already anticipated. Even though he prays “with himself,” the effect is boasting: his works are displayed, not offered in dependence.

The tax collector’s posture—standing far off, not lifting his eyes, beating his breast, saying “God be merciful to me a sinner”—is spiritually truer not because of a particular technique but because it embodies the opposite orientation. He treats himself as the one in need; he does not seek vindication; his shame is directed toward his need before God; his language of mercy acknowledges an outside source of justification. The physical posture is not the cause of salvation, but it psychologically/spiritually concretizes what his prayer already says: I am sinful; I lack righteousness; I need mercy.

So the parable’s judgment is not “the tax collector is morally better” or “legal holiness condemns all religious observance.” In Luke’s telling, both men are before God, but the Pharisee’s piety stands as a mirror of self-sufficiency, while the tax collector’s prayer stands as a mirror of reliance on mercy. The posture is truer because it makes the inward posture unmistakable: humility oriented toward pardon rather than superiority oriented toward recognition.
