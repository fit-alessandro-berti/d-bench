The five candidates are not rivals; they are layers. Anger is what we see first, envy and wounded fairness are what he himself would name, pride in obedience is the structure that holds his complaint together, and discomfort with mercy is the point where all of it collides with the father. But none of these is quite the bottom. Beneath them is a single condition that the text exposes almost word by word: the elder brother is a son who has been living as a hired servant. He relates to his father as an employer and to his brother as a rival, and therefore he cannot share the one thing the father cares about in this scene, which is joy. That is his deepest problem. Everything else is a symptom of it.

Take the layers in order.

1. Anger is the surface. "He was angry, and would not go in." Anger is never a root; it is a verdict. It tells us that he has judged something wrong and that the judgment has become bodily. The useful thing about his anger is what it makes him do: it keeps him outside the house. In a village feast of this kind the elder son would have been expected to stand beside his father and host; refusing to enter is a public insult on the same scale as the younger son's departure. The father's response is to "come out" to him, exactly as he ran out to the prodigal. Both sons have to be met outside, which is the first hint that both are lost.

2. Envy is real but oddly aimed. He does not envy his brother's life; he despises it ("devoured thy living with harlots," a detail the narrator never supplied and which the elder brother adds himself). What he envies is the fatted calf, the music, the father's delight being spent on someone else. It is envy of the father's favor, and it is bitter because the favor was apparently free. That already points past envy toward the real issue: he had assumed favor was earned, and he is watching that assumption collapse.

3. Wounded fairness is his own diagnosis. "Lo, these many years do I serve thee, neither transgressed I at any time thy commandment: and yet thou never gavest me a kid." This is a ledger: years of service in one column, a single goat unpaid in the other. Two things undo it. First, it is factually false. In verse 12 the father "divided unto them his living"; the estate is already his, which the father confirms: "all that I have is thine." He could have killed a kid any day of those many years. Nothing was withheld; he simply never took what was his, because he did not think of himself as someone who owned the house. Second, the ledger itself is the disease. A son does not invoice his father. The moment you keep accounts, you have turned the home into a workplace. His sense of unfairness is not the root; it is what a servant's self-understanding feels like from the inside when the master appears to overpay someone else.

4. Pride in obedience is the deeper layer, and it is where the parable's original audience sits. Luke 15 opens with Pharisees and scribes murmuring that Jesus "receiveth sinners, and eateth with them," and the elder brother's speech is their speech: "neither transgressed I at any time thy commandment." Notice that his obedience is joyless and instrumental. It is offered as leverage, not as love. And notice what he wanted it to buy: a kid "that I might make merry with my friends." His picture of happiness is a party somewhere else, with his friends, away from the father, which is precisely the picture the younger son had when he left for the far country. The two brothers wanted the same thing. One took it up front by rebellion; the other has been saving up for it by compliance. Both wanted the father's goods rather than the father. The obedient son's obedience was, at bottom, a long-deferred form of the same departure.

5. Discomfort with mercy is where the illness becomes visible, and it is the most nearly correct of the five names. He cannot enter a feast of mercy because mercy is a direct threat to him. If the wastrel can be received for nothing, then years of service purchase nothing; the currency he has been hoarding is declared worthless in a single afternoon. Mercy to his brother feels like injustice to himself because his standing was built on merit. This is why the Pharisees are offended by Jesus eating with sinners: not because they misunderstand what he is doing, but because they understand it exactly. A table open to sinners empties the value of their separation. So yes, he is uncomfortable with mercy. But ask why, and you are driven one step further down: he is uncomfortable with mercy because he does not love what the father loves.

That is the bottom. The elder brother's deepest problem is that he does not have his father's heart, and therefore cannot share his father's joy. The proof is in the small words. He says "this thy son"; the father answers "this thy brother." He has dissolved the brotherhood; the father reinstates it. He speaks of "these many years"; the father speaks of "ever with me." He frames the matter as reward and payment; the father frames it as death and life: "thy brother was dead, and is alive again." Those are different moral universes. In the elder brother's universe the question is what has been earned. In the father's universe there is a corpse walking in the door, and the only fitting response is a feast. The father's word is "meet," in Greek a word of necessity: it was necessary that we rejoice. Joy, for the father, is not a discretionary extra; it is what reality demands when the dead live. The elder brother cannot feel that necessity, and that inability is the whole of his tragedy.

Two details sharpen the point. The younger son, rehearsing his return in the far country, planned to say "make me as one of thy hired servants," and the father would not let him finish the sentence; he restored him as a son with robe, ring and shoes. The elder son has made himself a hired servant without ever being asked, and has stayed that way inside the house. The father addresses him as teknon, "child," the tenderest word available, and the whole reply is an attempt to talk him back out of servanthood: you are always with me, everything is yours, this is your brother. He is offered exactly what the prodigal was given. He is, in Henri Nouwen's phrase, the lost one who never left, and his lostness is harder to cure because it looks like virtue.

And the parable ends without telling us whether he went in. That silence is the point. Jesus leaves the elder brother standing in the courtyard because the elder brother is the audience. The first two parables of the chapter have someone go out to seek the lost sheep and the lost coin; in the third, nobody goes looking for the younger son, and the person who should have gone is the elder brother. His failure of mercy is a failure of brotherhood, and his failure of brotherhood is a failure to be his father's son in anything but name. Anger, envy, unfairness, pride, resistance to grace: all of them are true, and all of them are the shape this one thing takes when it is confronted with a father who runs to embrace the undeserving. He is not, in the end, angry that his brother came home. He is angry that his father is the kind of father who would rejoice about it, which means he has never really known him.
