Paul is contrasting **two ways of handling injury—and ultimately two kinds of community**, not simply telling people to replace an unpleasant feeling with a pleasant one.

### Bitterness is more than a passing feeling

The word translated “bitterness” (*pikria*) can denote resentful, harsh animosity. The words that follow—wrath, anger, shouting, slander or reviling, and malice—show bitterness in relation to its outward fruits. This is not necessarily a strict psychological sequence, but the list moves from inner hostility toward speech and conduct that damage others.

So bitterness here is best understood as **a settled disposition or posture**: injury and resentment are harbored until they begin governing how one regards and treats others. It can become habitual, and it can be deliberately cultivated, but the text does not justify saying that everyone who feels bitter has simply chosen that feeling or could instantly switch it off.

In fact, Ephesians 4:26 says, “Be ye angry, and sin not.” Paul is not treating every experience of hurt or anger as sinful. The question is what happens to anger when it is retained, fed, and given control.

### It also has something like a contagious quality

“Contagious” is not Paul’s word, but it describes the social dynamic well. The commands are addressed to the community: bitterness is to be removed “from you,” and its opposite is to be practiced “one to another.” The surrounding verses are likewise concerned with truthful speech, anger, corrupting talk, words that build up, and not grieving the Spirit.

Bitterness therefore does not remain neatly private. It can emerge as shouting, accusation, slander, and malice; others respond in kind, and resentment becomes part of the community’s atmosphere. **Private injury can thus become shared hostility.**

### Forgiveness is the corresponding practice of grace

Forgiveness is not placed merely against “bitter feelings.” It stands against the whole bitter–wrathful–malicious pattern. And it is accompanied by kindness and tenderheartedness, so Paul is not recommending cold, gritted-teeth emotional suppression. The opposite of bitterness is ultimately **a grace-formed heart and manner of life**.

The forgiving verb here, *charizomai*, belongs to the same word-family as “grace” and can mean to give graciously or, as here, to forgive. Its basis is crucial: “even as God … hath forgiven you”—more literally, “just as God in Christ forgave you.” Paul does not say, “Forgive because the offense was small,” or “because the other person earned it.” God’s forgiveness in Christ is both the motive and the pattern.

This does not mean forgiveness requires denying evil, abandoning justice, or instantly restoring trust. Nor does it guarantee that painful emotions disappear immediately. It does mean refusing to let injury mature into malice and retaliation.

So, among your options: **bitterness is primarily a settled, morally consequential posture, often habitual, with genuine emotional dimensions and potentially contagious communal effects.** Forgiveness is its counterpart because it interrupts that whole progression. The contrast is not “stop hurting and feel nice,” but:

> Do not let injury organize your life together around malice; let the grace you have received in Christ organize it around compassion, kindness, and forgiveness.
