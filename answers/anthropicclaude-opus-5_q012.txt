## The translation does some heavy lifting

"Desperately wicked" is doing more moralizing than the Hebrew requires. The word is *ʾanush* — sick, weak, incurable. Jeremiah uses the same root for untreatable wounds (15:18; 30:12). And "deceitful" is *ʿaqob*, crooked or twisted — the same consonants as Jacob, the heel-grabber. The line is closer to: *the heart is crooked above all things, and sick — who can know it?*

That shifts the register from prosecution to diagnosis. It isn't primarily saying "you are a villain"; it's saying "the organ you would use to assess yourself is the organ that's compromised." And *leb* in Hebrew isn't the seat of feeling as in modern usage — it's mind, will, deliberation, planning. The claim is precisely about the reliability of the faculty doing the introspecting.

## Not pessimism, because it isn't a dead end

Pessimism would leave the verdict standing with no recourse. This passage does two things that keep it from that.

First, the rhetorical question gets answered immediately. "Who can know it?" — not you. But: "I the LORD search the heart." The point isn't that self-knowledge is impossible in the abstract; it's that jurisdiction over the final verdict has been transferred. Your own self-assessment is testimony, not judgment. That's a limit on your authority, not an abolition of meaning.

Second, five verses later Jeremiah prays "Heal me, O LORD, and I shall be healed" (17:14) — deliberately picking up the *ʾanush* language. The incurable heart is turned into a petition. And the same book eventually promises a covenant written *on* the heart (31:33), which only makes sense as an answer to this diagnosis. The bleak line is a movement in a longer piece, not the last word.

Context matters too. This sits just after the contrast between the man who trusts in flesh (a desert shrub) and the man who trusts God (a tree by water) — and Judah's problem in these chapters is confident religiosity: temple, alliances, ritual, all while claiming covenant loyalty. The target is complacent self-assurance, not human beings as such.

## The realism has independent support

Whatever you make of the theology, the descriptive claim is now unusually well corroborated. Nisbett and Wilson's classic work showed people confidently reporting reasons for choices that demonstrably weren't the operative causes. Confabulation, choice blindness, motivated reasoning, the introspection illusion — the finding is not that people lie about motives but that they generate plausible narratives and believe them. Butler's *Sermon on Self-Deceit* got there in 1729 without a lab. So did Augustine: *factus sum mihi magna quaestio* — I have become a question to myself.

## So how do you talk about motives?

**Shift from verdict to petition.** The corresponding move is Psalm 139: "Search me and know my heart." Notice the grammar — a request, not a report. "I know my intentions were good" claims an authority the text denies you. "As far as I can tell, I meant well, but I'd want that checked" is honest and still usable.

**Route around introspection where you can.** Verse 10 is quietly interesting: God searches the heart, but repays "according to his ways, and according to the fruit of his doings." Even divine judgment is stated in terms of conduct and consequence. That's a permission slip to talk about motives *through* their traces: What did I actually do? Who bore the cost? What did I conveniently not look into? Does the pattern hold over time or was this an exception? These are checkable in a way that "my heart was in the right place" never is.

**Keep the asymmetry.** Jeremiah addresses a people about themselves. Turning the verse outward — "your heart is deceitful, so your objection is really just resentment" — inverts it, and is itself a fine specimen of the crookedness it names. Motive-attribution to others is simultaneously the least verifiable and most damaging kind of claim you can make; it's unfalsifiable by design, since any denial can be absorbed as more evidence. Suspect yourself, take others at their word until behavior forces otherwise.

**Learn your particular tells.** Generic suspicion is useless; specific suspicion is diagnostic. Look for reasoning that always terminates in your own favor. Principles that appear when convenient and go quiet when costly. The remarkable coincidence that duty and self-interest keep pointing the same direction. Urgency that spares you a conversation you were dreading. You can build a small personal list, and it works better than global self-distrust.

**Use outside witnesses.** If the instrument is unreliable, you calibrate it against others — friends who will contradict you, confession, spiritual direction, therapy, a spouse's read on your mood before you notice it. The tradition's insistence on confessing to *someone* is partly an epistemic device, not just a ritual.

**Avoid the two ditches.**

*Scrupulosity*: if I can never certify my motives, I can never act. But verse 10 judges ways and fruit, not certified interior purity. You are not asked to achieve a verified clean motive before doing the next right thing. Mixed motives are the normal human case, and waiting for unmixed ones is usually itself a stalling tactic — often a rather comfortable one.

*Cynicism*: if all motives are corrupt, distinctions collapse and nothing matters. But the text keeps distinguishing ways and fruits. "Everyone's a hypocrite" is a way of using the diagnosis to escape it.

The most compact answer to your question is that the passage denies you the role of judge in your own case while insisting the case is still real and still matters. The right speech-register for motives, then, is something like: *here is my best account, offered provisionally, open to correction, and here is what I actually did* — where the last clause is what anyone else, including you, should weight most.
