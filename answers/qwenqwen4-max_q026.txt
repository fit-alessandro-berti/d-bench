## The image itself: a warehouse, not a valve

The controlling metaphor is not plumbing but economics. *Thēsauros* is a treasury, a storehouse, a granary — something accumulated, deposited, guarded, and drawn upon. And *kardia*, in the Hebrew idiom standing behind the Greek, is not the seat of sentiment but the center of thought, intention, and will: the place where a person decides what things are worth.

So the picture is of an invisible hoard. Two verbs govern its relation to speech, and they pull in different directions:

- **"Out of the abundance (*perisseuma*) of the heart the mouth speaks."** *Perisseuma* is surplus, overflow, what a full vessel cannot hold. Speech here is what spills.
- **"Bringeth forth" is *ekballei*** — casts out, puts out, dispenses. This is an active verb of expenditure. Matthew uses it again of the scribe who "bringeth forth out of his treasure things new and old" (13:52), where the drawing-out is plainly a deliberate professional act.

The whole question you're asking lives in the gap between those two verbs. Overflow is something that happens to you; dispensing is something you do. Jesus says both in consecutive breaths, without embarrassment.

## Words as symptoms

The overflow model makes speech diagnostic. You cannot inspect a sealed jar, but you can jostle it and see what runs over the rim. Under pressure — insult, threat, surprise, fatigue — the mouth reports on contents the speaker did not choose to display and may not know he possesses.

This is exactly how the passage functions in its context. The Pharisees have just said that Jesus expels demons by Beelzebul. He does not treat this as a mistake of reasoning to be corrected but as a specimen to be read: *generation of vipers*. The slander is evidence of a settled orientation, a heart so stocked that it can look at the Spirit's work and taste poison. Hence the immediately following saying about blasphemy against the Spirit — not because one phrase is unforgivably naughty, but because a mouth that reliably calls light darkness has told you what is in the jar.

Two limits keep this from becoming a decoder ring for individual sentences. First, the surrounding metaphor is agricultural (v. 33: the tree and its fruit), and fruit is a *yield*, a season's pattern, not a single apple. Second, Matthew's Jesus knows very well that speech can lie in the other direction: "this people honoureth me with their lips, but their heart is far from me" (15:8). Pious words do not prove a good treasure, because goodness can be counterfeited. The claim runs one way: heart to mouth, not mouth to heart. Editing the output does not restock the warehouse.

## Words as choices

Three considerations keep the passage from collapsing into mere symptomatology.

**Treasuries are stocked.** Nobody awakes one morning possessing a hoard of malice by accident. A *thēsauros* implies a history of deposits: what one has repeatedly attended to, consented to, rehearsed, enjoyed, resented, forgiven, or refused to forgive. The overflow of the moment is the residue of ten thousand small prior elections. So even where speech is involuntary *now*, it is symptomatic of a self that was *made* — largely by the speaker. This is why symptoms can be culpable without ceasing to be symptoms. The drunk driver's swerve is not chosen at the moment of swerving.

**Dispensing is an act.** *Ekballei* is addressed, aimed, spent on someone. The Pharisees did not merely leak an opinion; they published an accusation designed to discredit a rival before a crowd. That is rhetoric, and rhetoric is chosen.

**Accountability presupposes agency.** Two verses later: "every idle word that men shall speak, they shall give account thereof in the day of judgment" (v. 36). Fevers are not judged; deeds are. And the immediately preceding verse is an imperative — "either make the tree good, and his fruit good" — which would be cruel nonsense if the tree were fate. The heart's stock is not destiny; it is *responsibility*, which is a different thing.

There is even a feedback loop elsewhere in Matthew. In 6:21 the arrow points the other way: "where your treasure is, there will your heart be also." There, deposits *drag the heart after them*. Put the two together and you get a circuit: what you store shapes what you are, what you are overflows into what you say, and what you say is a further deposit. Speech is not merely the readout of the heart; it is one of the things that funds it.

## Words as revelations

The heart is, in the biblical anthropology, genuinely hidden — "man looketh on the outward appearance, but the LORD looketh on the heart"; "I the LORD search the heart"; "judge nothing before the time... until the Lord come, who will bring to light the hidden things of darkness." Speech is the ordinary, standing exception: the one place where the invisible person becomes publicly available without special insight.

That is why judgment "by thy words" (v. 37) is not an arbitrary tariff on syllables. It is evidentiary. The verdict reads what was already there; the words are the self made audible, submitted as the record. Condemnation by speech is closer to a diagnosis confirmed than to a fine levied.

And the revelation runs inward as well as outward. Most people learn what is actually in their treasury not by introspection — which is flattered and unreliable — but by hearing, appalled, what came out of their own mouth when they were tired, wounded, or anonymous online. Speech is the heart's testimony against itself, and often the speaker's first honest information about his own condition.

## All three — but ordered in time

The categories are not rivals; they occupy different moments of one arc:

**Choices lay up the treasure → the treasure overflows as symptom → the overflow stands as revelation → the revelation confronts the speaker with a new choice.**

That last turn is what saves the passage from fatalism, and it explains an otherwise odd feature of it: Jesus is *talking to the vipers*. If the mouth could only ever confirm the heart, address would be pointless — you would be informing a stone of its stoniness. The rebuke presupposes that hearing it can begin a restocking. "Make the tree good" is an invitation, not a taunt.

Two things follow, and they cut in opposite directions. Against verbal hygiene: the project of managing one's language while leaving the hoard untouched is cosmetic, and the cosmetics will fail precisely under pressure, which is when it matters. Against expressive innocence: "I was just venting, that wasn't really me" is unavailable. The vent is the report. It may not be the last word about you — but it is a true word about what you have been storing.

Words are the interest the heart pays on its capital: involuntary in the moment, earned by a history of choices, and public — which is why they can be read by neighbors, and entered as evidence.
