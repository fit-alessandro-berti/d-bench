Isaiah 55:6–7 is an urgently tender invitation to come back to God. The prophet chooses one word for what the wrongdoer must forsake—**“way”**—and another for what the inward person must forsake—**“thoughts.”** These two words describe repentance from the outside in and the inside out.

## 1. “Way” is outward conduct

In Hebrew, *derek* means “road,” “path,” or “manner of life.” To forsake your way means to change your actual lifestyle. It is not just feeling sorry; it involves stopping the wrong actions, leaving the path you’ve been walking on, and turning your feet in a different direction.

So repentance always has visible fruit. “Let the wicked forsake his way” means the hands, feet, and habits that were engaged in sin must be turned away from it. A repentance that produces no change in behavior is not real repentance.

## 2. “Thoughts” are inward imagination and intention

The Hebrew word here is *machshavot*—thoughts, plans, designs, inward devices. It points to the hidden world of the heart and mind. The prophet says the “unrighteous man” must also forsake *his thoughts*. That is deeper than behavior.

This includes the “imagination” in its moral sense: the inner world of daydreams, schemes, desires, and plans. In Scripture, the heart is the workshop of the life. Jesus later said, “For out of the heart come evil thoughts” (Matthew 15:19). Solomon warned, “Keep your heart with all vigilance, for from it flow the springs of life” (Proverbs 4:23).

Why do “way” and “thoughts” both need to be forsaken? Because sin lives in both places. It lives in the world of action, and it lives in the world of imagination. A robber can stop robbing but still keep the fantasy of robbery in his heart. Or a person can change their mind but never actually live it out. True repentance deals with both the fruit and the root.

This is why Isaiah 55:8–9 says right after:

> “For my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways” (KJV).

God is calling us to forsake our lower ways and our lower thoughts so that we can receive His higher ones.

## 3. Returning to God is the heart of repentance

Notice the direction: “Let him return unto the Lord.” Repentance is not only *forsaking*; it is *returning*. The point of leaving the old way and the old thoughts is to turn to the Lord Himself. Without turning to God, our “repentance” is only moralism. Without forsaking, our “returning” is only hypocrisy.

So the text demands a whole-person turning: outward conduct and inward imagination. The feet turn away, and the mind turns away, because the heart turns to the Lord.

## 4. The possibility of mercy

The beautiful part is the ending. God says, “He will have mercy upon him, and to our God, for he will abundantly pardon.” Or literally, “He will multiply to forgive.”

This is why the possibility of mercy is real. Mercy is not based on how clean we can make ourselves. It is based on God’s character. When a sinner turns—even an unrighteous person with a dark inner world—God is ready to meet them with compassion and abundant forgiveness.

At the same time, the phrase “while he may be found” is serious. It is not saying that God hides, but it warns us not to delay. God is near, and His mercy is being offered. The door of grace is open, but it should be entered now. The promise is sure for all who seek and return:

> “For everyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved” (Romans 10:13).

So the gospel of Isaiah 55 is this: turn all the way—your outward life and your inward world—toward the Lord. The God who finds you near is a God who multiplies pardon. No one who truly returns is turned away.
