It does all three, but not in the same sense. Jesus does not forbid the judgment that someone has done wrong. He interrupts the passage from **“she has done wrong”** to **“therefore I am entitled to throw this stone.”**

The hinge is *“first.”* A crowd can demand a stoning, but someone has to begin it. Under the law, the witnesses were to cast the first stones (Deuteronomy 17:7). Jesus turns that responsibility back on the accusers. He does not ask them to decide, once more, what *she* deserves. He asks who among them will answer for what *he* is about to do. Nor does he have to prove that they are guilty of her particular offense. The hypocrisy lies in treating her guilt as a warrant for their own righteousness.

The ending keeps this from becoming a rule that only the sinless may make moral distinctions. Jesus says both *“Neither do I condemn thee”* and *“go, and sin no more.”* The second refuses to call the wrong harmless; the first refuses to make it the last word about her.

That is the deeper disturbance: **you can be right about another person’s wrongdoing and still be wrong in what you do to them.** The sentence does not end moral judgment. It makes the act of judging answerable to judgment too.
