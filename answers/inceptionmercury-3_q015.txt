The “sharpness” of Matthew 7:3–5 comes from its deliberately absurd, exaggerated picture: a person with a **plank in his own eye** trying to help someone with a **tiny speck** in theirs. The image is comic and hyperbolic, but precisely because it is so ridiculous, it exposes the blindness of self-righteous judgment.

**Why the image is sharp:**

1. **The contrast is extreme.** A “mote” (or speck) is barely noticeable; a “beam” (or plank) is huge, grotesque, and impossible to miss. The absurdity is that the person with a log in his eye claims to see clearly enough to perform delicate eye surgery on someone else.  
2. **It reverses the expected roles.** The speaker assumes the position of the wise helper, but Jesus flips it: the “helper” is the one who is actually blind.  
3. **It indicts the heart.** The problem isn’t that the person sees a fault in another; it’s that he refuses to see a far greater fault in himself. The image forces a laugh, then a shock of recognition: *I am that person.*

**How it criticizes hypocrisy without forbidding correction:**

Jesus does not say, “Never help your brother with his speck.” He says, **“First cast out the beam out of thine own eye; and then shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote out of thy brother’s eye.”** The correction is still to happen — “then thou shalt see clearly” implies the action continues. But it must happen in the right order, with the right posture.

- **Not:** “You have no right to address another’s sin.”  
- **Rather:** “Address your own sin first, so that when you address your brother’s sin, you are humble, honest, and genuinely helpful rather than hypocritical.”

So the passage condemns hypocrisy, not moral correction. In fact, seeing clearly after removing the beam enables **better** correction: a person who knows his own need for grace is far more likely to help a brother with gentleness and truth.
