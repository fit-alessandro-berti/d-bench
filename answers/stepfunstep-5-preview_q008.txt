John treats love for the visible brother as the test because, for him, love of God is not a private feeling or abstract spiritual attainment. It is participation in God’s own life—and “God is love” (1 John 4:8). If God’s love really dwells in someone, it must take visible form in how that person treats others.

The logic of the verse is:

- No one can directly inspect another person’s love for an invisible God.
- But the person’s attitude toward a brother or sister whom he *has* seen is observable.
- Hatred of the visible brother reveals that the supposed love for the invisible God is not real, because it contradicts the character of God and the command of God.

So the visible neighbor is not a substitute for God; he is the place where love for God becomes checkable. Words about God can be endlessly sentimental or self-flattering; treatment of actual people exposes what the heart actually loves.

The self-deception being exposed is the deception of **religious self-justification**: claiming intimacy with God while remaining unloving, resentful, contemptuous, or indifferent toward people made in God’s image. Such a person may love a “god” of their own imagination—one who blesses them, comforts them, or approves their habits—but not the God revealed in Christ, whose nature is self-giving love.

In John’s language, this person is a “liar” because the claim “I love God” is contradicted by the plain fact of hatred or neglect toward a visible brother. The lie is not only before others; it is a lie the person tells himself. He mistakes religious language, warm feelings, or correct belief for the transforming love of God.

A key qualification: John is not saying that every failure or irritation in relationships disproves love for God. He is exposing a settled pattern of hatred or refusal to love. But he also refuses to let love for God remain merely “invisible”: if it is real, it will be seen.
