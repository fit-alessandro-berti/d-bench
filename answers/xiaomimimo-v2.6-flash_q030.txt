## Reading the passage as a whole

First, a note on the shape of the text. In Greek (the KJV's "Charity" is *agapē*), the passage is markedly lopsided: almost every clause in verses 4–5 is a negation — *ouk* constructions. Pride is refused (*ou perpereuetai, ou physioōtai*), envy is refused, self-display, self-advancement, irascibility, and suspicion are refused. Then, in verse 5c and verse 6, the verbs turn positive: love *rejoices* — twice. And verse 6b–7 piles up four affirmative verbs: bears, believes, hopes, endures. So the negations are not the destination; they are the clearing that makes the destination visible. The structure runs: **what love refuses → what love does → what love ventures.**

That structure explains much of the answer.

---

## 1. The negations are diagnostic: they address Corinth

Paul is not writing a free-floating hymn. He is writing to a congregation whose specific sins are precisely the ones he negates:

- **"Puffed up"** (*physioōtai*) is Paul's own recurring word for Corinthian boasting (4:6, 18–19; 5:2; 8:1). The Corinthians were *inflated* — by eloquence, by knowledge, by spiritual gifts, by factional identity.
- **"Seeketh not her own"** answers their lawsuits against one another before secular courts (6:1–8) and their liturgical grabbing (11:21).
- **"Is not easily provoked"** answers their quarrelling and party-spirit (1:11–12; 3:3).
- **"Envieth not"** answers the rivalry over tongues, prophecy, and status.
- **"Thinketh no evil"** — more precisely, *ou hypo-lambanei kakon*: does not fasten an evil construction on another, does not suspect the worst. This answers the very judgment Paul forbids at 4:5 ("judge nothing before the time").

So the negative form is not accidental. Each negation is a surgical removal of an actual wound in the body Paul is addressing. Love is defined *against* the congregation's pathologies, because a definition that ignored them would be useless to this reader.

---

## 2. The negations strip away every way the self defends and inflates itself

Look at what the refused vices have in common. Pride *inflates* the self. Envy *ranks* the self against another. Irritability *punishes* others for threatening the self. Suspicion *pre-empts* the other's freedom and goodness in order to keep control. Self-seeking *advances* the self. Every one of them is a reflex of self-protection or self-promotion — a mechanism by which the ego maintains its size and its borders.

To negate them all at once is to define love as **kenosis**: an emptying (cf. Phil 2:5–8). Love is not first a feeling added to the ordinary self; it is the dismantling of the ordinary self's defensive apparatus. Paul says as much in 13:3: even the most extreme self-sacrifice "profiteth nothing" without love — because the sacrifice can still be *mine*, still be display. The negations ensure that love cannot be co-opted as a technique for self-enhancement.

This also guards the concept against sentimentality. Love that is merely warmth, intensity, or eloquence can be entirely self-referential — "puffed up." Only the refusals tell you whether it is love at all.

---

## 3. Negation as the right mode for defining love

There is a deeper reason love is defined by what it *does not* do. Love cannot be possessed by description; it is recognized in practice, under pressure. A positive definition ("love is feeling X") risks turning love into a possession or a mood. A negative definition has the character of a discipline: love is what remains when every self-serving reaction has been foreclosed.

Notice too that Paul is not merely importing Stoic *apatheia*. The Stoic sage is defined by what he does *not* feel; Paul's love, having been stripped of envy, suspicion, and irascibility, immediately *rejoices in the truth* and *bears, believes, hopes*. The negations clear the ground; what grows there is not impassivity but a positive, directed, and risky activity toward another person.

---

## 4. The vision of strength being proposed

The passage proposes a strength that is almost exactly the inverse of what its original audience — and ours — would instinctively call strength.

**a) Strength as non-reactivity.** "Is not easily provoked" and "thinketh no evil" mean that the person is no longer governed by others' behaviour. Whoever is ruled by insult, by slight, by the fear of being taken advantage of, is *not* free — others are pulling the strings. Love's strength is inner self-possession: it is not bent out of shape by pressure applied to it. This is why Paul can say "when I am weak, then am I strong" (2 Cor 12:10).

**b) Strength as endurance across time.** *Makrothumei* — "suffereth long" — is literally *long-tempered*. Envy, rage, and suspicion are quick, short-horizon responses; they purchase immediate relief at the cost of the relationship. Love's strength is temporal: it can absorb a long accumulation of injury without retaliating and without closing down. Strength here is measured not by force exerted but by damage sustained without deformation.

**c) Strength as non-dominating power.** "Vaunteth not itself, is not puffed up, seeketh not her own": love refuses the very instruments by which power ordinarily operates — display, ranking, self-advancement. The strength proposed is power relinquished rather than power exercised — the cruciform pattern Paul unfolds fully in 2 Corinthians 10–13 and Philippians 2, where authority is demonstrated *by* endurance of suffering rather than by domination.

**d) Strength great enough to be vulnerable.** This is the sharpest paradox. "Believeth all things, hopeth all things" — love grants the benefit of the doubt; it accepts the risk of being wronged, deceived, and disappointed. A self-enclosed, suspicious, self-protecting person is *safe*, but safe because weak: he cannot afford exposure. The strength Paul proposes is the capacity to remain open, to continue trusting and hoping, precisely where armour would be the natural and prudent response. In verse 7 the four verbs (*stegō, pisteuō, elpizō, hypomenō* — roughly: to roof over, to entrust, to expect, to stand under) describe a person who can take the weight of another's failure without collapse.

**e) Strength ordered toward truth rather than safety.** The two affirmations in verse 6 — "rejoiceth not in iniquity, but rejoiceth in the truth" — show that this is not naïveté. Love is not weak on wickedness; it takes no pleasure in exposed wrongdoing (it does not gloat), but its joy is located in truth, not in its own vindication. Its energy is directed outward and upward rather than inward.

---

## Summary

The flood of negations exists because (i) they are addressed to a specific congregation whose actual sins were pride, envy, litigation, and irascibility; (ii) each refused vice is a mechanism of self-inflation or self-protection, so negating them all defines love as self-emptying; and (iii) love is a discipline recognizable under pressure, best defined by what it forecloses rather than by what it feels.

The vision of strength proposed is therefore **not invulnerability but the power to remain unbent, unretaliating, and open**: strong enough not to be provoked, secure enough not to suspect, and free enough to entrust itself to another and to hope — a strength measured by endurance, relinquishment, and the courage of exposure rather than by display or dominance. The movement of the passage confirms it: it begins with refusal, passes through joy in the truth, and ends in hope — strength that culminates not in safety but in a venture.
