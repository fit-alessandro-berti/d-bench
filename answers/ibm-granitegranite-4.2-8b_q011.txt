The movement from **"cease to do evil"** (v. 16) to **"learn to do well"** (v. 17) in Isaiah 1:16–17 is grammatically and logically precise, and it directly addresses the question without imposing interpretation beyond the text. Here is a clear, concise answer grounded *only* in the wording and context of the passage:

1. **"Cease to do evil"** means *stop actively harming or pursuing what is unjust, corrupt, or wrong*. It is a call to stop *doing* evil deeds (e.g., oppressing the poor, exploiting the vulnerable, false worship). It is necessary but **not sufficient** on its own; stopping evil does not automatically mean *doing good* or *acting justly*. One can simply refrain from wrongdoing while continuing to be indifferent or passive toward injustice.

2. **"Learn to do well"** follows immediately and purposefully. The verb *learn* (Hebrew: *lifn'ah* – implying instruction, practice, acquisition of skill/discipline) indicates *active effort and acquisition*, not mere passive absence. It is not synonymous with "stop evil"; it moves *beyond* cessation into *positive, intentional action*. The phrase "do well" (Hebrew: *aseh tov*) refers to concrete, observable ethical behavior – not an abstract feeling.

3. **Why the move?** The text explicitly links the two: after commanding cessation (*cease to do evil*), it commands *learning* and *doing* good (*learn to do well*). The reason is logical: merely stopping evil does not guarantee justice or care for the vulnerable. The prophet anticipates that people might simply avoid overtly sinful acts while ignoring systemic or relational injustice (e.g., failing to relieve the oppressed, neglecting the fatherless). Therefore, the imperative shifts to *actively learning and practicing* the good: seeking judgment, relieving the oppressed, judging the fatherless, pleading for the widow. These are specific, actionable, justice-oriented tasks.

4. **What this suggests about goodness:**  
   * Goodness here is **not merely restraint or mere absence of evil**. Saying "cease to do evil" alone would allow (in principle) for passivity or indifference toward injustice (e.g., not helping the widow or fatherless while not committing a crime).  
   * Goodness here is **a practiced, intentional form of justice and care**. The verbs "seek judgment," "relieve the oppressed," "judge the fatherless," "plead for the widow" describe concrete, relational actions. The command "learn to do well" indicates that doing good requires learning, effort, and discipline (it is not assumed or automatic). It is not framed as a feeling ("love") but as observable ethical practice rooted in justice and care for specific vulnerable groups.  
   * Thus, the text implies that **true goodness involves active engagement with justice and the needs of others**, not just avoiding harm. "Learn" underscores that this is not innate or automatic for the people being rebuked; it requires instruction/practice. "Do well" specifies *what* that active good looks like in concrete terms (seeking fairness, relieving suffering, protecting the vulnerable).

**In simple terms:** The passage first says *stop doing evil* (stop harming). Then it says *learn and actively do good* (seek justice, help the oppressed, care for the fatherless and widow). This shift shows that merely stopping wrongdoing is insufficient; genuine goodness requires actively learning and practicing justice and care for others. Goodness is portrayed as a practiced, intentional form of justice and relational responsibility—not just restraint.

> **Key clarity:** The text uses "love" (in common theological interpretation) *not* in Isaiah 1:16–17 itself. It uses concrete verbs: *seek judgment, relieve the oppressed, judge the fatherless, plead for the widow*. Therefore, the answer stays precise: goodness here = actively practicing justice/care as described (learned, not assumed), not merely restraint. The phrase "practiced form of love and justice" is accurate *insofar as the text describes justice and care as the practiced good*; calling it "love" is a later theological interpretation. Stick to the text's language: *learn to do well* → *seek judgment, relieve the oppressed, etc.* = practiced justice/care. Avoid overstatement; the text directly names the actions.

**Final concise answer for the question:**  
The text moves from "cease to do evil" to "learn to do well" because merely stopping evil does not guarantee justice or care for the vulnerable; people might simply avoid overt sin while ignoring oppression. Thus, goodness is portrayed not as mere restraint or passive absence of evil, but as a *practiced, intentional form of justice and care* – requiring learning and active steps like seeking judgment, relieving the oppressed, judging the fatherless, and pleading for the widow. The word "learn" shows it is an active discipline, not automatic. Goodness here is defined by concrete, relational action rooted in justice.
