The anger is real — but it's only the surface.

> "he was angry, and would not go in" v. 28

All the others you name are there in him: envy, wounded fairness, pride in obedience. But they are all branches. The root is deeper: **he is uncomfortable with mercy itself because he has never understood sonship as grace.**

That's why Jesus told the story. Remember who it's for:

> "Then drew near unto him all the publicans and sinners for to hear him. And the Pharisees and scribes murmured, saying, This man receiveth sinners, and eateth with them." Luke 15:1-2

The younger son is the tax collectors and sinners. The elder son is the murmuring Pharisees. Jesus deliberately leaves the ending open to invite *them* to come in.

Look at what the elder says:

**1. He relates like a slave, not a son.**
"Lo, these many years do I serve thee" v.29. The word is *douleuo* — to slave away. He doesn't say "I have loved being your son." He says "I have worked for you."

And the Father answers with the wound exposed: "Son [ *teknon*, my child ], thou art ever with me, *and all that I have is thine*." v.31

He was never deprived. The kid, the fellowship, the inheritance — it was all already his. He lived like a hired hand in his father's house and never enjoyed it, because he was trying to *earn* what was already freely his. That's not devotion, that's transaction.

**2. He relates by record-keeping, not relationship.**
"neither transgressed I at any time thy commandment" v.29.

Only a self-righteous person can say that with a straight face. He's keeping score. In his accounting, obedience should purchase celebration: I obeyed, therefore you owe me a kid-goat for me and my friends.

When the Father operates by a completely different accounting — by grace, not wages — it feels like a violation. That's the wounded fairness: "thou... hast killed for *him* the fatted calf." v.30

It's the same complaint as the workers in Matthew 20 and Jonah in Jonah 4. If God is fair the way I define fair, He cannot be that merciful.

**3. He refuses brotherhood.**
Notice he won't say "my brother." He says "this *thy* son, which hath devoured thy living with harlots." v.30

He distances himself from both the brother and the father. The Father gently corrects him: "for *this thy brother* was dead, and is alive again." v.32

You cannot share the Father's house while refusing the Father's family.

**4. Therefore he cannot rejoice.**
"It was meet that we should make merry, and be glad" v.32. *Meet* means morally necessary — there is a divine ought to joy over repentance.

The Father went out to both sons. He ran to the younger in v.20, he comes out and entreats — *parekalei*, pleads with — the elder in v.28. Same pursuing love. One responded in repentance. The other stood outside in his goodness.

That is the deepest problem: the elder is as lost as the younger, only lost at home. The younger was lost in rebellion; the elder is lost in his own obedience.

Pride in obedience made him feel entitled. Entitlement made mercy look unjust. And when mercy looks unjust, you get angry at a merciful father rather than grateful to be his son.

The question Jesus leaves us with is not, "Did you break the rules like the younger son?" It's, "Will you come inside when grace you didn't earn is given to someone you think doesn't deserve it?"
