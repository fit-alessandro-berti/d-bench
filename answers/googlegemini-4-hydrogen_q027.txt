Luke actually tells us at the very start of the passage what the parable is aimed at: Jesus spoke it to those who **“trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and despised others”** (v. 9). 

Looking at the five options you listed, they aren’t mutually exclusive—two are the **root diagnosis**, two are the **symptoms/mechanisms**, and one is a **subtle theological error** underneath it all.

---

### 1. What exactly condemns the Pharisee?

* **Pride (specifically, self-trust) — *Core root:***
  Verse 9 explicitly identifies the primary flaw: trusting in oneself for righteousness. Even though the Pharisee begins with a pious formula (*“God, I thank thee…”*), the center of gravity in his prayer is entirely himself (*“I am not… I fast… I give tithes”*). The phrase *“prayed thus with himself”* (v. 11) underscores this self-enclosure: his prayer barely leaves his own ego.
* **Contempt — *Core root:***
  The second half of verse 9 names contempt (*“despised others”*). His sense of standing before God depends on looking down on other people—first broad categories (*“extortioners, unjust, adulterers”*), and then a specific human being standing nearby (*“or even as this publican”*).
* **Comparison — *The mechanism:***
  Comparison is how he maintains his pride. Instead of measuring himself vertically against God’s holiness, he measures himself horizontally against people he considers morally inferior. On a horizontal scale, anyone who fasts twice a week and tithes rigorously can easily feel justified.
* **Virtue as self-display — *Nuanced: self-justification rather than mere applause-seeking:***
  Elsewhere in the Gospels, Jesus condemns performing piety to be seen by *other people* (e.g., Matthew 6:1–18). Here, however, the Pharisee is praying *“with himself”*—an internal or private address to God. His flaw isn't necessarily putting on a show for a human crowd; it is **weaponizing genuine virtue as a spiritual resume**. Fasting and tithing are good things in themselves, yet he uses them as evidence of his superiority and as leverage before God.
* **False doctrine — *Not explicit heresy, but a fatal functional error:***
  On paper, the Pharisee’s theology sounds orthodox: he believes in God, thanks God, avoids gross sin, and goes beyond the Law’s minimum requirements for fasting and tithing. What condemns him isn't a wrong abstract creed, but a **functional denial of grace**. By treating his righteousness as something he produces and possesses in himself, he approaches God not as a supplicant in need of mercy, but as a creditor presenting a record of merit.

In short: **Pride (self-reliance) and contempt** are what condemn him; **comparison** and **the misuse of virtue** are how they express themselves.

---

### 2. Why is the tax collector’s posture treated as spiritually truer?

The tax collector (publican) is not praised because his past life was good—as a collaborator with Roman taxation, he likely *was* guilty of the kind of extortion the Pharisee condemned. His posture is treated as spiritually truer for four reasons:

1. **Honesty before reality:**
   He makes no excuses, offers no mitigating virtues, and hides behind no religious credentials. He calls himself simply *“a sinner”* (in the Greek text, *the* sinner—as though he isn't thinking about anyone else's guilt at all). Truthful self-knowledge is the starting point of spiritual clarity.
2. **A vertical rather than horizontal orientation:**
   Unlike the Pharisee, the tax collector does not glance sideways to compare himself with anyone else—not even to resent the Pharisee. Standing *“afar off,”* refusing to lift his eyes to heaven, and striking his chest, he relates solely to God (*coram Deo*). Before an infinitely holy God, the distance between a devout Pharisee and a corrupt tax collector shrinks to insignificance; both stand in need of grace.
3. **Dependence on mercy rather than merit:**
   His entire prayer is six words in Greek: *“God, be merciful to me a sinner.”* The verb used for “be merciful” (*hilasthēti*) carries the overtone of atonement—appealing to God’s forgiving mercy rather than one's own performance. He recognizes that righteousness is not a status one achieves and presents to God, but a gift one receives from God.
4. **Alignment with the actual terms of justification:**
   In Luke’s Gospel (and biblical theology more broadly), a person is set right with God (*“justified”*, v. 14) not by claiming moral self-sufficiency, but by recognizing spiritual bankruptcy and casting oneself on God’s mercy. The Pharisee asks for nothing, assumes he already possesses righteousness, and goes home unchanged. The tax collector brings nothing, asks for mercy, and goes home justified.

The irony at the heart of the parable is that **the Pharisee’s real sins are invisible to him because they are disguised as religion**, whereas **the tax collector’s real sins strip away all illusions**, leaving him open to the truth of who God is and who he is before God.
