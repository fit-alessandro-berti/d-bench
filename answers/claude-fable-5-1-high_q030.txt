1 Corinthians 13:4–7 — Why love is described by what it refuses, and what kind of strength this proposes

1. The shape of the passage: a portrait built largely out of "nots"

Paul's description of love (agapē, rendered "charity" in the KJV) contains fifteen verbs in the Greek. Only a handful are positive: love is patient (makrothymei), is kind (chrēsteuetai), rejoices with the truth, and then the closing quartet—bears, believes, hopes, endures all things. The centre of the passage, however, is a run of eight negations: love does not envy, does not boast, is not puffed up, does not behave indecently, does not seek its own, is not provoked, does not keep a reckoning of evil, does not rejoice at wrongdoing.

That is a strange way to praise anything. If you were asked to describe a great virtue you would normally list its achievements. Paul instead lists the things love declines to do. There are several reasons for this, some rooted in the situation of the Corinthian church, some in the nature of love itself, and some in a deliberate argument about what real strength looks like.

2. The negations are a mirror held up to Corinth

The passage is not an abstract hymn dropped into the letter; it is the hinge between chapters 12 and 14, which deal with the Corinthians' quarrels over spiritual gifts. Nearly every negation in 13:4–7 names a fault that Paul has already charged them with elsewhere in the letter:

- "envieth not" — the letter opens with jealousy and strife (3:3), factions claiming Paul, Apollos, Cephas (1:12).
- "vaunteth not itself, is not puffed up" — "puffed up" (physioutai) is one of Paul's signature words for Corinth (4:6, 4:18–19, 5:2, 8:1: "knowledge puffeth up, but charity edifieth").
- "doth not behave itself unseemly" — the disorder at the Lord's Supper (11:17–22) and in worship (14:40, "let all things be done decently and in order"; the same root, aschēmonei/euschēmonōs).
- "seeketh not her own" — Paul's refrain about eating meat, using one's rights, and refusing to insist on one's freedom at the expense of the weak (8:9–13; 10:24, "let no man seek his own, but every man another's wealth"; 10:33).
- "is not easily provoked" — the lawsuits between believers (6:1–8).
- "thinketh no evil" — literally "does not reckon up (logizetai) the evil", the language of accounting, of keeping a ledger of wrongs; precisely what a litigious, factional community does.
- "rejoiceth not in iniquity" — the church that was proud of tolerating flagrant sin (5:1–2, 5:6).

So the first answer to "why so many negations?" is pastoral and rhetorical: Paul is describing love in exactly the places where the Corinthians lack it. Each "not" is a quiet indictment. The portrait is negative because the disease it is treating is a set of very specific positive vices—self-assertion, competitive display, touchiness, suspicion. Love is painted as the photographic negative of the community's actual behaviour.

3. The negations name the ways the self intrudes on love

Look at what the eight negations have in common. Envy, boasting, inflation, indecency, self-seeking, irritability, score-keeping, and gloating over wrong are all ways the ego asserts itself against another person:

- Envy resents that another has what I lack.
- Boasting and being puffed up demand that others see me as larger than I am.
- Unseemly behaviour puts my impulse ahead of the dignity of the situation and the people in it.
- Seeking one's own makes my advantage the measure of every choice.
- Being provoked treats every slight as an attack on my honour that must be answered.
- Reckoning evil keeps the account open so that I can collect.
- Rejoicing in iniquity enjoys another's fall because it flatters me by comparison.

Every one of these is a movement of the self toward the centre. Love, by contrast, is the self moving out of the centre so that the other can stand there. Paul cannot describe that movement purely in positive terms, because its essence is a refusal: love is what happens when the reflexes of self-protection and self-promotion are not obeyed. The negations are not a lack of content; they are the content. To say love "envieth not" is to say that love has already freed itself from the comparison that makes envy possible.

There is also a logical point here. Pride, envy, irritability and suspicion are the natural, default responses of a threatened or ambitious self. Nobody needs to be taught them. They arise automatically. Love, therefore, appears in human life first as an interruption—as something that stops a reaction that would otherwise have happened. The kind word is possible only because the sharp one was withheld. Patience is visible only where retaliation was available. Hence the grammar of "not."

4. Why these four in particular: pride, envy, irritability, suspicion

The question rightly picks out four families. It is worth seeing how they fit together as a single anatomy of the unloving self.

Pride (vaunting, being puffed up) is the root: the self inflated beyond its real size. In Corinth this attached itself to knowledge and to spectacular gifts—tongues, prophecy—which is why the chapter begins by saying that tongues, prophecy, knowledge, even martyrdom, are "nothing" without love.

Envy is pride's shadow. The self that wants to be large cannot bear another's largeness. The Corinthian competition over gifts (12:14–26, the body with members that despise or envy one another) is exactly this.

Irritability ("easily provoked", paroxynetai) is pride's defensive edge. The inflated self is easily punctured, and reacts to each puncture as an outrage. Touchiness is pride's nervous system.

Suspicion ("thinketh evil", the kept ledger; and by implication the opposite of "believeth all things") is pride's memory. It stores injuries, assumes the worst motive, and reads others through the record of their offences.

These four together describe a self that is swollen, comparative, defensive, and retentive. Paul's love is the opposite on each count: it is small enough to be kind, generous enough not to compare, secure enough not to be provoked, and free enough not to keep accounts.

5. The vision of strength: endurance rather than dominance

Now to the second part of the question. Ancient culture—Greek and Roman, and Corinth was both—admired a fairly clear picture of strength: the man who asserts himself, defends his honour, answers insult, outshines rivals, and secures his own. Boasting was not a vice in that world; it was the ordinary way honour was maintained. Being "easily provoked" was expected of anyone with standing. Seeking one's own was simple prudence.

Paul's portrait inverts this. The passage is framed by two words of endurance. It opens with makrothymei—"suffereth long", literally to be long-tempered, to hold one's anger far off—and closes with hypomenei, "endureth all things", the word for standing firm under a load. Between these, the negations describe a self that does not need to win. The strength on offer is not the power to impose but the power to remain: to stay kind under provocation, to stay generous in the presence of another's success, to stay open-handed when one could grasp, to stay trusting when suspicion would be safer.

Several features of this strength stand out.

(a) It is strength as self-possession rather than self-assertion. The person who is not provoked is not weaker than the person who retaliates; he has more control, not less. "Suffereth long" is not passivity; it is holding a great deal of pressure without letting it discharge. The negations describe a will strong enough to govern its own reflexes. Envy, boasting, irritability and suspicion are, on Paul's reading, not signs of vigour but of a self that is being pushed around by its own appetites and fears.

(b) It is strength that does not depend on comparison. Vaunting and envy both need an audience and a rival. Love needs neither. It can be kind without being seen, and glad at another's good without loss. That is a more stable footing than honour, which is always in competition and can always be taken away. Paul's love is strong the way a foundation is strong—by not needing to be noticed.

(c) It is strength that can afford to be vulnerable. "Believeth all things, hopeth all things" is the boldest line in the passage. Suspicion feels like strength because it protects; trust feels like weakness because it exposes. Paul reverses the valuation. Only a very secure self can afford to give others the benefit of the doubt, to assume good faith, to hope for the person who has disappointed you. The suspicious person is actually the frightened one. Love's willingness to be taken advantage of is not naivety; it is the confidence of someone whose standing does not depend on winning every exchange. (Paul says something similar in 6:7: better to be wronged and defrauded than to go to law against a brother.)

(d) It is strength measured by what it can carry, not by what it can seize. The final four verbs—bears, believes, hopes, endures—all describe capacity. "Beareth all things" (stegei) means to cover or to hold up under, like a roof. The strong person in this vision is the one who can take the weight of other people's faults, delays, and failures without collapsing into resentment. This is the strength of a load-bearing wall, not of a battering ram.

6. The theological ground: this is a portrait of God's own love

Paul is not offering a self-help ideal or a Stoic exercise in detachment. Behind the description stands a specific model. Elsewhere Paul says God "is kind" and "long-suffering" toward sinners (Romans 2:4); that God does not reckon sins against those in Christ (2 Corinthians 5:19, using the same verb logizomai as "thinketh no evil"); that Christ "pleased not himself" (Romans 15:3) and "made himself of no reputation" (Philippians 2:7)—the exact opposite of being puffed up. The cross is, for Paul, the supreme example of strength that looks like weakness: "the weakness of God is stronger than men" (1 Corinthians 1:25), a theme announced at the very start of this same letter. The love of chapter 13 is therefore a description of how God has treated the Corinthians, held up as the pattern for how they should treat each other. The negations describe what God has refused to do to them—he has not kept the ledger, not sought his own, not been provoked—and the vision of strength is Christ's.

This also explains why the passage ends by saying that love "never faileth" while prophecy, tongues and knowledge pass away (13:8). The showy gifts the Corinthians prized are powers of the present age. Love is the only thing that survives into the age to come, and so it is, in the most literal sense, the strongest thing there is: it outlasts everything else.

7. Summary

The passage is full of negations because love, in real human life, shows itself chiefly as a refusal: the refusal to let the self take the centre. Envy, boasting, irritability and suspicion are the automatic movements of a threatened, comparative ego, and Paul lists them because they were precisely the sins of the Corinthian church and because they are the standing enemies of love everywhere. Against the classical ideal of strength as self-assertion and defence of honour, Paul proposes a strength that consists in endurance, self-possession, generosity without rivalry, and the security to trust and hope even at the risk of being wronged. It is the strength to bear weight rather than to throw it, modelled on a God who has "not reckoned" evil to his people and on a Christ whose power was revealed in a cross. In that vision the person who does not need to win is the strongest person in the room.
