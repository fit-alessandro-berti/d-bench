Luke 6:37 is not saying that moral judgments never matter. If it were, the command to forgive would make no sense: forgiveness presupposes that a wrong has been done. The verse is not abolishing moral discernment; it is forbidding a posture of final, merciless condemnation.

### Discernment vs. the appetite to condemn

The difference is between two ways of relating to wrongdoing.

**Discernment**  
- Sees and names wrong as wrong.  
- Distinguishes between the person and the act.  
- Seeks truth, justice, protection, correction, and restoration.  
- Recognizes its own limits and its own need for mercy.  
- Leaves final judgment to God.  

**The appetite to condemn**  
- Moves from “that action is wrong” to “that person is worthless.”  
- Collapses a person’s identity into their worst act.  
- Feeds on superiority, resentment, or vengeance.  
- Claims final knowledge of another’s heart.  
- Rejects mercy and the possibility of repentance.

For example: “That was a cruel lie” is discernment. “He is a liar beyond hope” is condemnation.

### Why this is not moral relativism

The verse exists within a moral universe, not outside one. Jesus does not say, “Nothing is wrong.” He says, “Forgive.” Forgiveness is itself a moral response to a real wrong. If moral judgments did not matter, there would be nothing to forgive.

The warning in Luke 6:37 is about the *spirit* in which we judge. Just before it, Jesus says, “Be merciful, just as your Father is merciful” (Luke 6:36). Just after, he says, “For with the measure you use, it will be measured back to you” (Luke 6:38). That is not an invitation to moral indifference; it is a warning against merciless judgment.

### Scripture holds both together

Jesus also says, “Judge with right judgment” (John 7:24), and he commands correction of a brother who sins (Luke 17:3). James writes that God alone is lawgiver and judge (James 4:12). So the New Testament calls for moral discernment, but it forbids usurping God’s place as final judge over a person.

In other words, moral judgment matters greatly. But it must be exercised with humility, truthfulness, and mercy. The opposite of condemnation is not indifference. The opposite of condemnation is forgiveness—which still takes evil seriously, but refuses to let the appetite to condemn have the last word.
