

1 John 4:20 treats love of the visible brother as the test of love for the invisible God because the verse is concerned with the *reality* of love, not merely with religious claims or feelings.

The logic is not that loving people is more important than loving God. Rather, it is that the same love which is directed toward God must necessarily express itself toward those God loves. If a person claims to love God but hates, despises, resents, or ignores his brother, that claim is exposed as hollow.

Several things are going on in this verse.

### 1. Visible love is the appointed test of invisible love

God is unseen. A person can easily claim to love God in the abstract, in worship, in emotion, or in religious language. But because God is invisible, that claim is not directly verifiable in the same way.

The brother, however, is visible. He is concrete. He is present. He can be served or neglected, forgiven or resented, honored or despised. So the visible brother becomes the test case: Does this person actually possess the love he claims to have toward God?

John’s point is that love is not merely an interior sentiment. It is shown in action. As he says earlier in the same letter:

> “My little children, let us not love in word, neither in tongue; but in deed and in truth.”  
> —1 John 3:18

If love remains only in words or feelings but fails in concrete relationships, it is not the love John is talking about.

### 2. To love God is to love what belongs to God

The brother is not a neutral person. In the biblical view, human beings are made in God’s image. In the Johannine context especially, “brother” likely refers to a fellow believer, a member of God’s family. Therefore, to hate a brother is to hate someone who belongs to God, someone loved by God, someone bearing God’s image or God’s family likeness.

So the verse assumes a simple spiritual principle: you cannot truly love the Father while hating His children.

If someone says, “I love the king,” but despises the king’s children, that claim is contradicted by his attitude. How much more with God, whose nature is love?

John has just said:

> “Beloved, let us love one another: for love is of God; and every one that loveth is born of God, and knoweth God. He that loveth not knoweth not God; for God is love.”  
> —1 John 4:7–8

Love is not merely something God commands; it is something that flows from who God is. If God’s love truly dwells in someone, it will move outward toward others.

### 3. The visible neighbor prevents love of God from becoming fantasy

There is a danger in religion: people can cultivate a strong sense of devotion to an invisible God while avoiding the costly, humbling, everyday work of loving visible people.

It is easier to feel love for God in prayer than to forgive a brother who has wronged you.  
It is easier to speak of heavenly love than to show patience, generosity, and humility in ordinary relationships.  
It is easier to have zeal for God than to bear with the weaknesses of the people God has placed near you.

So the visible brother exposes whether our love for God is real or merely imaginative. The neighbor is the test because the neighbor cannot be spiritualized away. He is right there. His need, his fault, his presence, his offense — all of these reveal what is actually in the heart.

In that sense, love of the visible brother protects love of the invisible God from becoming self-serving fantasy.

### 4. The self-deception exposed is the separation of devotion from obedience

The verse exposes a very common form of spiritual self-deception: the belief that one can have a right relationship with God while living in hatred, bitterness, contempt, or indifference toward others.

The deceived person may say, “I love God,” while in practice he refuses to love the people God commands him to love. He may be sincere in his feelings, but his life contradicts his confession.

This self-deception can take several forms:

- **Mistaking religious emotion for love of God.**  
  One may feel deeply moved in worship yet harbor unforgiveness or hatred toward a brother.

- **Mistaking doctrinal correctness for love.**  
  One may defend truth while lacking the love that belongs to truth.

- **Mistaking spiritual language for spiritual reality.**  
  One may say, “I love God,” but fail to show kindness, patience, or mercy to actual people.

- **Using devotion to God as an excuse to avoid loving people.**  
  One may claim to be focused on “higher things” while neglecting concrete responsibility toward neighbor or brother.

- **Making God into an abstraction.**  
  If God is only an invisible object of religious feeling, then one can love “God” without ever being challenged by the visible person in front of us. But the true God is known partly through love of those He made and loves.

John therefore says bluntly:

> “He is a liar.”

That is a strong phrase. It does not necessarily mean the person is consciously lying in a calculated way. It can mean that his life gives the lie to his confession. He claims one thing but demonstrates another. His visible hatred or lovelessness contradicts his invisible claim.

### 5. The verse assumes that love is one reality

John is not contrasting two unrelated duties, as though love for God and love for brother were separate compartments. In Johannine theology, they belong together. The love of God is not complete if it remains only vertical. It must become horizontal.

This matches Jesus’ teaching that the whole law is summed up in love for God and love for neighbor:

> “Thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thy heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy mind. This is the first and great commandment. And the second is like unto it, Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself. On these two commandments hang all the law and the prophets.”  
> —Matthew 22:37–40

The second commandment is “like” the first because it reveals and embodies it. Love for God is not merely a feeling toward an unseen object; it is a life shaped by God’s own love.

### In short

The verse treats love of the visible neighbor as the test of love for the invisible God because visible love is the tangible proof of invisible grace. God is unseen, but His image-bearers are seen. His children are seen. His command to love is lived out in visible relationships.

The self-deception being exposed is the illusion that one can love God while hating or rejecting the people God loves. It is the deception of substituting religious feeling for real obedience, spiritual language for practical love, and devotion to God for contempt toward one’s brother.

The verse teaches that authentic love of God cannot remain hidden and abstract. It must become visible in how we love others.
