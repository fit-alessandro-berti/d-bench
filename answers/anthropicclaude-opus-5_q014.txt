**The grammar tips the scale toward training.**

Verse 21 does not say "where your treasure is, there *is* your heart." It says *there will be* (ἔσται) — a future. The heart is described as arriving somewhere, later, at a destination already chosen for it. That small tense does a lot of work. If treasure merely *revealed* the heart, the sentence would be a diagnostic present, and the sensible command would run the other way: *fix your heart on God, and your treasure will follow*. But Jesus commands the treasuring — an act of hands, ledgers, deeds — and *promises* the heart as consequence. The "for" (γάρ) that opens v. 21 is not offering evidence for a diagnosis; it is supplying the motive for obeying an imperative.

So the saying is not primarily a test you take but a mechanism you're warned about. Your heart is portable, and it migrates toward whatever you have stockpiled.

Of course the diagnostic reading isn't false — it's just downstream. "Where your treasure *is*" presumes you already have one, chosen by a heart that already wanted something. What we have is a loop: the heart selects a treasure, the treasure deepens the heart's attachment, the deepened heart selects more of the same. Jesus intervenes in the loop at the only point where you have real leverage. You cannot obey a command to love God more by staring hard at your affections. You *can* obey a command about where you put your money. The hand is the accessible end of the heart.

**Possession recruits attention.**

Notice that the argument against earthly treasure is not that material things are evil, nor even simply that they don't last. It is more specific: *moth, rust, thief*. Three agents of loss — one that eats fabric, one that eats metal, one that walks in and takes. What they have in common is that they make ownership into a job. A thing that can be eaten must be checked; a thing that can be stolen must be guarded. Vulnerable property does not sit quietly in a box; it conscripts your vigilance. Wealth in this world comes with an unpaid night watchman's post, and you are it.

That is how possession gets hold of attention. And attention is the soil love grows in. You come to love what you keep looking at, thinking about, calculating over, defending. Anxiety is a counterfeit form of devotion, and the two are hard to tell apart from the inside — both keep the object constantly in mind. Hence the darker edge of the promise: if your treasure is in something moths eat, then *your heart is in the moth-eaten thing*. It will share its fate. You have stored yourself in a house with a hole in the wall.

**Jesus makes this chain explicit in what follows.**

The next verses are not a change of subject. Treasure (6:19–21) → the eye (6:22–23) → the master (6:24) → anxiety about food and clothing (6:25–34). That is precisely the sequence of possession, attention, love, and care. The eye passage is about attention as such — the organ that either floods the whole person with light or darkens it — and in Semitic idiom the "evil eye" is the stingy eye, the "single" or generous eye its opposite. Attention and generosity turn out to be one topic. Then comes allegiance: two masters is impossible, because the heart is single, and it will end up wherever the one treasure is. Then comes anxiety, which is what having your treasure exposed to weather feels like from the inside.

The vocabulary shift is worth noticing too. Verses 19–20 speak of *treasures*, plural, scattered accumulations. Verse 21 says *your treasure*, singular, and *your heart*, singular. The plural hoards consolidate into one center of gravity, and you get only one.

**And the mechanism of transfer is giving.**

How does one actually "lay up treasure in heaven"? Matthew has already told us in this same chapter: alms in secret, prayer in secret, fasting in secret, "and your Father who sees in secret shall reward you." And later, plainly: "sell what you have and give to the poor, and you shall have treasure in heaven" (19:21). Heavenly treasure is not a separate portfolio one funds with spare piety; it is accumulated *by divesting the earthly one*. You send your goods ahead to where your Father is, and the heart follows its stake — which is exactly what hearts do, and exactly why the command works.

One last thing, easy to miss: the phrase *for yourselves* is retained in both halves. "Lay not up **for yourselves**... but lay up **for yourselves**." This is not a demand that you stop caring about your own good. It is a claim that you have been storing your good in a defective vault. Jesus argues like a financial adviser comparing security arrangements — and then, at the end, discloses what the deposit actually is. You thought you were investing money. You were investing your heart.
