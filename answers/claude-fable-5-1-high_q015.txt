Matthew 7:3–5: the mote and the beam

1. Why the image is so sharp

The saying works because it is, at bottom, a piece of visual comedy that turns out to be a piece of moral anatomy. Several features combine to give it its edge.

(a) Grotesque disproportion. The Greek karphos (KJV "mote") is a speck, a splinter, a bit of chaff or sawdust—something small enough to lodge in an eye. Dokos ("beam") is a roof-timber, a rafter, the kind of log that holds up a house. The contrast is not between a small fault and a somewhat larger one; it is between something barely visible and something that could not possibly be missed. Jesus uses this kind of hyperbole elsewhere (a camel through a needle's eye, straining out a gnat while swallowing a camel), and it works the same way here: the physical absurdity of a man with a plank protruding from his face makes the moral absurdity undeniable. You cannot picture the scene without laughing, and the laughter is the argument.

(b) The organ chosen is the eye. This is the stroke of genius. The eye is the instrument of seeing, and therefore the instrument of judging. A fault located anywhere else on the body—a lame leg, a diseased hand—would not affect one's ability to assess another person. A fault in the eye corrupts perception itself. So the image says more than "you have bigger faults than your brother." It says that the faculty you are using to detect his fault is exactly the faculty that is compromised in you. The judgment is unreliable at its source. The censorious man does not merely have a flaw; he has a flaw in the very organ of censure.

(c) The beam is invisible precisely to its owner. No one can see his own eye. Whatever is in it is, by definition, out of his line of sight, while it distorts everything he does see. This makes the picture an exact model of self-deception: the fault you cannot perceive in yourself is the one that most shapes how you perceive others. The hypocrite is not lying so much as blind—and blind in a way that feels to him like clear sight.

(d) The verbs. The man "beholds" (blepeis—simply sees, notices) the speck in his brother, but does not "consider" (katanoeis—attend to, take careful note of) the beam in himself. Attention is the issue. He has plenty of eyesight for the neighbour and none for himself. The disproportion is not only of size but of scrutiny.

(e) The dramatization. Verse 4 moves from description to direct speech: "Let me pull out the mote out of thine eye." We hear the tone—officious, solicitous, faintly superior—and we see the speaker leaning in to perform a delicate operation while a rafter swings from his own face. Removing a speck from someone's eye is intimate, close-range work; it requires steady hands, gentleness, and above all clear vision. The one thing the operation demands is the one thing the would-be surgeon lacks. He would put the brother's eye out, or knock him flat with the beam, before he ever got near the speck.

(f) The word "hypocrite." Hypokritēs is a stage term: an actor, one who plays a part behind a mask. The man is cast as a physician of eyes while being the blindest person present. The sharpness lies in the gap between the role assumed and the condition actual. This is exactly what hypocrisy is—not merely inconsistency, but the performance of a competence or virtue one does not possess.

(g) "Thy brother." The person under scrutiny is kin, not enemy. The relationship presupposed is one in which correction would be natural and welcome, which makes the abuse of it more pointed. And the second-person singular ("thou," "thine") aims the saying at the individual hearer. The reader who enjoys the satire of the plank-faced busybody discovers that he is the one being addressed.

There was a rabbinic proverb of similar shape (R. Tarfon laments that no one in his generation can accept reproof: told "take the chip from between your eyes," a man answers "take the beam from between yours"). In that form the saying is a cynical observation about how people deflect criticism. Jesus takes the same image and turns it from a retort into a discipline: the beam is not a debating point you throw back at your critic, it is something you must actually deal with in yourself.

2. How the passage criticizes hypocrisy without forbidding correction

It is easy to read verses 1–5 ("Judge not...") as a blanket prohibition on moral judgment. The text itself does not allow that reading, for several reasons.

(a) The mote is real. Jesus never says the brother's eye is clear. The speck is there, it is a genuine impairment, and removing it is a genuine good. The passage does not relativize the brother's fault or pretend that faults are matters of opinion. What it attacks is not the perception that something is wrong but the posture of the one perceiving it.

(b) The structure is "first... then," not "never." Verse 5 is an instruction in sequence: "first cast out the beam out of thine own eye; and then shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote out of thy brother's eye." The removal of the brother's mote is retained as the goal and stated as the outcome of the whole process. Self-examination is not the destination; it is the prerequisite. The saying regulates the order and the conditions of correction, not its legitimacy.

(c) The purpose of self-correction is clear sight for the sake of another. "Then shalt thou see clearly" (diablepseis) is the hinge. Dealing with one's own beam is not commended as private moral hygiene or as a retreat from responsibility for others. It is commended because it restores the vision needed to help. The passage thus rejects two opposite errors at once: the censorious man who corrects others without examining himself, and the quietist who uses "who am I to judge?" as an excuse to leave the brother with a speck in his eye. Both fail the brother. Only the one who has cleared his own vision can actually do him the service.

(d) What is condemned is a specific vice, named precisely. The target is the "hypocrite"—not the person who notices faults, but the person who plays the physician while himself in need of surgery, who exempts himself from the standard he applies to others. Verse 2 sets the frame: "with what measure ye mete, it shall be measured to you again." The problem is not having a measure but using one measure for yourself and another for your neighbour. The remedy is to apply the same measure inward first. That does not abolish measuring; it makes it honest.

(e) The surrounding context requires discernment. Immediately afterward Jesus says not to give what is holy to dogs or cast pearls before swine (7:6), warns against false prophets who must be known "by their fruits" (7:15–20), and elsewhere tells the disciples that when a brother sins they are to go and tell him his fault (18:15). None of that is possible without evaluation of others' conduct. "Judge not" cannot mean "make no moral assessments"; it means do not set yourself up as a condemning judge who stands above the process rather than within it. Paul draws the same balance: restore the one who is overtaken in a fault, "in the spirit of meekness; considering thyself" (Gal. 6:1).

(f) The correction envisaged is an act of care, not of superiority. The physical image chosen—taking a speck out of someone's eye—is a kindness one does for a friend at close range, face to face. The passage therefore reframes moral correction as service rather than verdict. The hypocrite's correction fails not because correction is wrong but because it is performed as a display of his own soundness, and because, blinded as he is, it will hurt rather than heal. The corrected version of the same act, done after the beam is out, is gentle, accurate, and welcome.

(g) There is a further, subtler point. What is the beam? The passage does not name it, and the omission is deliberate: the hearer must look for his own. But the shape of the saying suggests one candidate. The disproportion between how eagerly the man sees his brother's speck and how little he notices his own condition is itself a fault—the censorious, self-exempting habit of mind. Very often the beam is the very fault-finding zeal that discovers the mote. A person who takes verse 5 seriously will therefore find that the process of removing the beam changes the spirit in which he approaches the mote at all. He will come to the brother not as prosecutor but as someone who knows what it is to have had something in his eye.

In sum: the image is sharp because it locates the fault in the organ of judgment itself, magnifies it to absurdity, and stages it as a scene we cannot help laughing at until we realize we are in it. And the passage escapes the charge of forbidding all correction because its grammar is temporal ("first... then"), its goal is explicitly the brother's healing, and its condemnation falls not on those who see faults but on those who pretend to a clarity they have not yet earned.
