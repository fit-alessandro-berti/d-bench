Philippians praises **power that does not need to defend its rank**. Christ does not have to prove his equality with God by holding others beneath him. He takes the place of a servant and remains obedient even to the cross. The point is not that he stops being great in order to become humble, but that his humility shows what his greatness is like.

Open domination is easier to understand: it assumes that everyone wants to come out on top. Even resisting a tyrant can leave that assumption intact—we may simply want someone else on top. But when someone who truly has power freely takes the lowest place, it unsettles our sense of what makes the highest place worth having. What we call strength may turn out to be fear of losing status, and what we dismiss as weakness may be the freedom not to cling to it.

That is why “Let this mind be in you” is such a demanding invitation. It asks not only whether we admire Christ’s humility, but what we do with the power we already have.
