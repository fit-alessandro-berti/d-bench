In Isaiah 55:6-7, "way" and "thoughts" are a form of Hebrew poetic parallelism meant to describe the whole person.

> Let the wicked forsake his way, and the unrighteous man his thoughts

Most interpreters understand the two terms as complementary:

**1. "Way" *derek* = outward conduct**
This is the visible path of life - habits, practices, actions, the direction a person is walking. To forsake his way is to abandon a lived pattern, not just a single act.

**2. "Thoughts" *machashebet* = inward imagination**
This word is often translated as plans, purposes, schemes, or reasoning. It's not a fleeting thought, but the inner logic, self-justification, desires, and worldview that *produces* the way.

Together they imply:

* Sin and wrongdoing are not only behavioral but rooted in the mind and imagination. The text assumes conduct flows from inward planning.
* Therefore repentance - described here as "let him return" *shuv*, the classic Hebrew word for turning back - cannot be only external reformation or only private intention. It is a turning of both life and mind.
* It also resists a purely ritual idea of return. It's not just changing where you go, but how you think, what you dwell on, what you consider good and true.

That is why the promise that follows is so expansive:

> and he will have mercy upon him; and to our God, for he will abundantly pardon.

Literally, "he will multiply to pardon."

In the context of Isaiah 55, which begins with an open invitation to come without price, v.6-7 presents urgency - "while he may be found... while he is near" - but also breadth. The two categories are paired as:

*the wicked* and *the unrighteous man*

Both the outwardly destructive and the inwardly scheming are addressed.

For Jewish and Christian readers, the implication has traditionally been:

1. God sees and cares about both spheres, the public and the private.
2. No one is beyond return, because mercy is offered to both kind of failure.
3. Forgiveness corresponds to the whole need - if both way and thought need turning, then pardon is offered as abundantly complete.

In short, the verse portrays change as holistic: a new direction lived out, springing from a new mind renewed within, met by a mercy described as more than sufficient for both.
