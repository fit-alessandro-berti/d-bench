Romans 12:20–21 defines victory over evil **not as defeating the enemy by superior force, retaliation, or humiliation**, but as refusing to let evil reproduce itself through you. In context, Paul has just said, “Avenge not yourselves… Vengeance is mine; I will repay, saith the Lord” (v. 19). Then he commands concrete love: feed the hungry enemy, give the thirsty enemy drink. The summary is v. 21: “Be not overcome of evil, but overcome evil with good.”

So victory is **moral and spiritual**: evil is overcome when it fails to make you become evil in return. Good interrupts the cycle. It does not deny that evil is evil; it refuses to be conquered by it.

The phrase “heap coals of fire on his head” is often interpreted as the enemy’s conscience burning with shame, possibly leading to repentance. Others see it as leaving the enemy to God’s judgment. Either way, Paul’s point is not “be nice so you can secretly punish them.” That would be manipulation and revenge in disguise. The command itself is not manipulative because it seeks the enemy’s real good and leaves the outcome to God. If someone uses kindness as a guilt-trip technique, that is a misuse of the command, not its meaning.

Is the command weak? No. Retaliation often looks strong but is actually weak because it lets the enemy dictate your response. Passive resignation is also weak. But feeding and caring for someone who has wronged you requires real strength: self-control, courage, faith, and love. It is cruciform strength—the kind of strength Jesus showed in overcoming evil through faithful love rather than evil.

So Romans 12:20–21 presents a genuinely strong command. It does not guarantee that the enemy will repent, and it does not cancel God’s justice. But it frees the disciple from bitterness and revenge, trusts God to judge rightly, and aims at reconciliation where possible. Victory over evil means evil loses its power to make you like itself. Good—God’s kind of good—overcomes it.
