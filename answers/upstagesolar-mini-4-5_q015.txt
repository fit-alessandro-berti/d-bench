This is one of the sharpest images in the Gospels, and its sharpness lies in a set of deliberate contrasts that turn a proverb into a theological argument.

**The image's sharpness: scale reversal and its consequences**

The core move is a *scale inversion*. The "mote" (a splinter) and the "beam" (a great timber) are not just different sizes—they belong to different categories of object. A mote is something you notice reflexively, something that genuinely obstructs sight in its narrow domain. A beam is so massive that it doesn't just obstruct; it makes clear sight impossible for the person carrying it. That produces the passage's cruelest and funniest irony: the man who boasts he can extract a speck from his neighbor's eye is so blinded by his own timber that he is, quite literally, unable to *see* the speck he means to remove. The metaphor seals itself—the hypocrite's judgment is not merely wrong but perceptually impossible. He is judging with a corrupted instrument and cannot even know it.

There's a second layer: the beam doesn't just impair the critic, it *entangles* him. A log in the eye means the man walking around to remove splinters is hobbled, muttering, mistaking specks for beams. The passage is saying something about the phenomenology of hypocrisy: the hypocrite doesn't just hold a wrong opinion, he is functionally incapacitated by it.

**The target: not the judgment, but the self-certifying judgment**

Crucially, what gets condemned is not "having opinions" or "noticing others' faults." The passage targets a specific posture: the *manner* in which the correction is offered. Three markers of it:

1. **Absence of self-examination.** The hypocrite does the sight-seeing before the self-seeing. His eye is fixed on the brother's eye while his own is a window to a beam.
2. **The unsanctioned intervention.** Notice the grammar: he *says* "Let me pull out the mote"—proposing an act of surgery he has not earned and is not equipped to perform. Hypocrisy in this passage is anticipatory: he intends the surgery before qualifying for it.
3. **The label "hypocrite" (hupokritēs)** —originally a stage-player, an actor in a mask. The charge is not that he is evil but that he is *performing* virtue while the mask conceals the beam. The mask is the offense, not the content of the judgment.

**How moral correction survives**

The passage is careful not to abolish the duty to see and remove the mote—otherwise it would be a counsel of passivity. Several features preserve the obligation:

- **The imperative is doubled and sequenced, not negated.** "First... and then" —the correction is *retained*, just reordered. The moral work doesn't disappear; it gets a precondition.
- **"Clearly see" (diōpōsa)** implies a real act of seeing will follow. The purpose of the beam's removal is not self-improvement for its own sake but the restoration of *vision adequate to the task*—and the task is still the brother's eye. You cannot cast out a mote you cannot see.
- **The remedy is prescribed before the duty is discharged.** The sequence ("first cast out... and then shalt thou see clearly") implies that after the beam is out, the clearing of the mote is both possible and right. There is an implicit, unstated "well done" for the one who sees clearly afterward.
- **The correction is re-described as restorative, not prosecutorial.** The man who has cast out his beam can see the mote *as a mote*—small, specific, correctable. The hypocrite saw a beam where there was a speck; the clear-sighted person sees a speck where there was a beam. The difference is not that one judges and the other doesn't, but that one can now judge *accurately and gently*, at the right scale.

**The deeper critique: it exposes the engine of judgment**

The passage's sharpest edge is that it attacks the *structure* of a certain kind of moral confidence: the assumption that seeing another's fault is equivalent to being fit to address it, and that the address can precede the cure. The beam is a figure for the unexamined self—the sin, passion, or self-righteousness that keeps one from seeing clearly. The "beam" is not necessarily a single gross sin; it can be the blind spot, the pride of the corrector himself. That's what makes the image humbling: the beam is *ours too*, and the hypocrite is blind precisely to it while insisting on the speck in someone else's eye.

So the passage does two things at once. It *delegitimizes a class of corrections*—those offered by the blinded, those offered before self-examination, those offered as performance. And it *re-licenses correction itself*—restored to its proper condition: the clear-eyed, the first-cleansed, the one who can then truly see the small thing and remove it. The image is sharp because it makes the hypocrite's blindness visible, and it makes the remedy visible with it: you must first get the log out before the speck means anything.
