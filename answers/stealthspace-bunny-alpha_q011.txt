The movement is deliberate: **Isaiah calls not only for abandoning evil but for becoming practiced in good.** “Cease to do evil” is the negative turn—stop what harms you, others, and the relationship with God. “Learn to do well” is the positive turn: let a different way of life take root in its place. Repentance here is not simply the absence of wrongdoing; it is a reorientation of desires, habits, and responsibilities.

The Hebrew word behind “learn” carries the sense of **acquiring a skill through instruction and practice**. Isaiah is asking for something formed, not a momentary decision to be generous or fair. The rest of the verse shows what that practice involves:

- **“Seek judgment”**: actively pursue what is right, rather than wait for wrongdoing to trouble you.
- **“Relieve the oppressed”**: respond to injustice and deprivation, not merely avoid adding to them.
- **“Judge the fatherless, plead for the widow”**: defend people who lack power or a voice, including by taking their part in public disputes.

So the text places goodness beyond private restraint. Someone might cease doing obvious evil while remaining entirely indifferent to the suffering of others. Isaiah demands more: **a habit of loving neighbors in concrete, just actions**. Goodness is not simply refraining from harm; it is actively seeking justice, protecting the vulnerable, and giving voice to those overlooked.

The sequence therefore does not imply that stopping evil is unimportant. Rather, it makes clear that **stopping evil is not the finish line**. Isaiah envisions a people whose repentance changes both what they abandon and what they practice. One must cease doing wrong—and learn, day after day, to do right.
