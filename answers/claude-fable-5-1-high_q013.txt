Amos 5:21–24 is one of the harshest things God says about worship anywhere in Scripture, and its harshness is the point. The passage does not say "you have too much liturgy and too little justice; adjust the proportions." It says God hates, despises, refuses to smell, refuses to accept, refuses to look at, and refuses to listen to what Israel is offering him. To understand why the rejection is total rather than corrective, one has to see what the worship had become and what it was being used to do.

1. The setting: prosperous worship built on plundered people

Amos preaches in the northern kingdom under Jeroboam II, a time of military security and economic boom. The shrines at Bethel, Gilgal and Dan are busy and well funded. The same book tells us where the funding comes from. The wealthy "sell the righteous for silver, and the poor for a pair of shoes" (2:6), "tread upon the poor, and take from him burdens of wheat" (5:11), "afflict the just, take a bribe, and turn aside the poor in the gate from their right" (5:12), falsify weights and measures (8:5), and lounge on ivory couches eating "the lambs out of the flock, and the calves out of the midst of the stall" (6:4). Most tellingly, 2:8 pictures them lying down beside the altar on garments taken in pledge from debtors. The pledged cloak of a poor man is spread out in the sanctuary.

That is why the wording of 5:22 matters: "the peace offerings of your fat beasts." The fat beasts were fattened on wealth extracted from the powerless. The offering is not merely accompanied by injustice; it is made of injustice. The animal on the altar is the proceeds of the crime. When God says "your feasts," "your assemblies," "your songs," the possessive is doing work: these things are no longer his; they are theirs, an institution they own and operate.

2. Why the senses are shut, one by one

The verse works through God's faculties. He will not smell (the technical term for God's acceptance of a sacrifice, as in Genesis 8:21, and the very thing he threatens to withhold in Leviticus 26:31), he will not accept the offerings, he will not look on the peace offerings, he will not hear the songs. This is the language of a person turning his face away, not of a critic suggesting improvements. What the worshippers experience as melody, God hears as "noise." The sensory inversion tells us that the worshippers' own perception of their piety is exactly the thing that is false. They enjoy it; God is repelled by it.

Balance would be the right prescription if worship and justice were two independent goods and Israel had simply overinvested in one. But that is not the diagnosis. In Israel's own law, worship was never a separate good sitting beside justice. The feasts celebrated the Exodus, God's liberation of slaves; they were commanded to include the Levite, the stranger, the fatherless and the widow (Deuteronomy 16:11, 14); the sabbath rested servants and animals; tithes fed the poor. Justice was the content of the worship, not a competitor to it. So a festival of liberation held by people who are currently enslaving their neighbours through debt is not an unbalanced act. It is self-refuting, a celebration that contradicts its own meaning. You cannot rebalance a contradiction; you can only expose it.

3. The fraud: several layers

The passage exposes a fraud with at least four layers.

(a) Worship as a bribe. The men who take bribes in the gate (5:12) are trying the same thing on God. Sacrifice costs something, so the offerer feels he has paid. The implicit theology is that God can be placated by volume and expense and then will leave one's business practices alone. This turns the covenant God into a patron who can be kept sweet, and the altar into a place where favour is purchased rather than where loyalty is expressed. God's refusal to "smell" is his refusal to take the payment. If he accepted a more modest offering alongside a bit more justice, he would be confirming the very idea that his favour is a commodity with a price. Only by declining the currency altogether can he show that it was never legal tender.

(b) Worship as insurance. The verses immediately before (5:18–20) address people who "desire the day of the LORD." They want God to come because they are certain he is on their side; the busy sanctuary is their proof. Amos tells them that day will be darkness, not light, like a man escaping a lion only to meet a bear. Their religion has become protective magic, a way of securing God against the consequences of how they live. A religion whose function is to guarantee immunity has to be broken, not moderated, because moderation would leave the guarantee in place.

(c) Worship as an anaesthetic for conscience. The "noise" of their songs is loud enough to drown out the cry of the poor that God hears in 2:7 and 8:4. Religious fervour becomes the mechanism by which the oppressor reassures himself that he is a good man. Amos 4:4–5 is bitterly sarcastic on exactly this: "Come to Bethel and transgress... proclaim and publish the free offerings: for this liketh you." They love their worship because it makes them feel righteous. The people are not only defrauding God and the poor; they are defrauding themselves, and the fraud on themselves is the one that makes the others sustainable.

(d) A false God. The deepest layer is theological. A God who is pleased by such worship would be a God indifferent to the widow in the gate, in other words not the God who brought Israel out of Egypt (2:10; 3:1). To worship the true God as though he were that kind of God is to misrepresent him publicly, to bear false witness about his character in his own house. Verse 25 sharpens this: "Did ye bring unto me sacrifices and offerings in the wilderness forty years?" The relationship existed before the elaborate cult and never depended on it. Israel has taken a means and made it the thing itself, and then used it to hide from the end it was meant to serve.

4. The positive demand and its images

The "but" of verse 24 is the hinge. What God wants is that "judgment (mishpat) run down as waters, and righteousness (tsedaqah) as a mighty stream." Mishpat is concrete: verdicts in the gate, fair scales, the debtor's cloak returned. Tsedaqah is the right ordering of relationships that makes such verdicts habitual. The "mighty stream" is the perennial wadi, one that does not dry up in summer, as opposed to the seasonal torrents that roar after rain and then vanish. This is a precise contrast with Israel's religion. Their piety is like a seasonal wadi: loud and full at festival time, dry the rest of the year. Their justice is the same, a spasm at the feast and then nothing. God asks for the opposite pattern: justice that is quiet, continuous and unstoppable. Notice too that their offering is "noise" while justice "rolls down." One is sound without substance; the other is substance that moves.

5. Why sharpness rather than balance is the only honest response

Consider the analogy of a man who mistreats his wife and brings her flowers. She does not want a better ratio of flowers to mistreatment; the flowers themselves have become an insult, because they are an attempt to buy back goodwill without changing anything. Her refusal of the flowers is not a rejection of flowers in principle. It is a refusal to let the gift do the work the giver wants it to do, namely to make repentance unnecessary. Amos's God is in that position. He is not abolishing worship; the whole prophetic tradition (Hosea 6:6, Isaiah 1:11–17, Micah 6:6–8, Jeremiah 7:21–23, Psalm 51:16–17) makes the same move without ever concluding that sacrifice is evil. He is refusing to let worship function as a substitute for, and a cover over, the very thing worship exists to express.

Had he asked only for balance, he would have accepted the framework in which piety and injustice can be traded against each other on a single ledger. That framework is the fraud. The fraud is exposed only by declaring the offering worthless as offered: not a lesser good, not a good in need of supplement, but an abomination, because it takes what belongs to the poor, dresses it as devotion, and presents it to a God whose first concern is the poor. The sharpness is God refusing to be an accomplice.

So the passage rejects worship so violently because the worship is not a deficient version of the right thing but a counterfeit of it, and counterfeits are not improved by being issued in smaller quantities. The moral fraud is the attempt to obtain divine favour and security under false pretences, paid for with the goods of the oppressed. The spiritual fraud is the self-deception that lets religiously fervent people feel righteous while their neighbours cry out, and the theological fraud is the portrait of a God who does not care. Amos tears all three away at once, and then tells them what real worship would look like: a river of fairness that never runs dry.
