Amos 5:21–24 is not saying that God is indifferent to worship, nor that He simply wants “less worship and more justice,” as if the two could be balanced like items on a scale. It is a covenant lawsuit against worship that has become false. The people are maintaining feasts, assemblies, sacrifices, songs, and music while practicing economic exploitation, legal corruption, and violence against the poor and vulnerable (see Amos 2:6–8; 4:1; 5:11–12). Their worship is an attempt to secure God’s favor without covenant loyalty.

So the sharpness is deliberate: God rejects the worship *as offered*. The problem is not a scheduling imbalance but a contradiction. Worship that praises God while trampling on the people God cares about is not partial worship; it is counterfeit worship. A call for “better balance” would imply that some acceptable level of injustice could coexist with praise. Amos instead says, in effect, “Stop the music; let justice flow.” The rhetorical force is meant to shatter false security.

The fraud being exposed is both moral and spiritual:

1. **Liturgical cover-up:** Public religion is being used as a cloak for injustice. Sacrifices, songs, and solemn assemblies become a way of managing appearances and perhaps easing conscience while the social order remains unjust.

2. **Transactional religion:** The people treat worship as a mechanism to guarantee blessing and divine protection, as though God can be bought off or manipulated by ritual. They want the benefits of covenant—election, prosperity, security—without covenant obedience.

3. **False worship of a false god:** They are honoring a God of their own construction—one who accepts their injustice and values ceremony above covenant faithfulness. True worship of YHWH cannot be separated from justice, mercy, and right relations with neighbor.

4. **False security:** They assume that because the temple, feasts, and offerings continue, God must be with them. Amos announces that God is not impressed by noise and ritual when the cries of the oppressed are ignored.

The last verse gives the positive alternative: “Let justice roll down like waters, and righteousness like a mighty stream.” The Hebrew terms point toward social justice, right judgment, and covenant faithfulness—not merely private piety. Worship becomes true when it is joined to a life and community shaped by God’s justice. Until then, the songs are noise and the offerings are offensive. God is not against worship; He is against worship that lies.
