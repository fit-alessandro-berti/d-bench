Why Enemy-Love Is Hard to Take Seriously — and What It Actually Is

1. Why the command resists us

Enemy-love is difficult to take seriously for reasons that run deeper than mere moral laziness. Three stand out.

First, it collides with our intuitions about justice. An enemy, by definition, is someone who has wronged us or wills our harm. Every natural instinct — and much respectable moral philosophy — says that response should be proportionate to desert: friends deserve warmth, enemies deserve resistance or at least withheld goodwill. To love the persecutor can feel not merely difficult but *wrong*, as if it cheapened the wound, betrayed the victims, or erased the moral difference between cruelty and kindness. The command seems to threaten the very machinery by which we hold wrongdoers accountable.

Second, it collides with our psychology. Enmity is not an opinion we hold; it is an emotion lodged in the body — fear, humiliation, the memory of injury. Resentment has a protective logic: it keeps us vigilant against those who have hurt us. Asking someone to love an enemy feels like asking them to disarm in the middle of a battle. And there is a social cost too: groups often cohere around shared enemies, so the person who blesses "them" risks being read as a traitor by "us."

Third, it is easy to dismiss because of how it has been misused. The command has been quoted to tell the abused to stay with abusers, the oppressed to stop resisting oppressors. That history makes thoughtful people suspicious: is this teaching anything more than a spiritualized demand for passivity, issued most conveniently by the comfortable to the afflicted?

2. Is it sentimental?

The sentimental reading — enemy-love as a warm feeling toward everyone, a refusal to acknowledge that real evil exists — does not survive contact with the text. Jesus's instructions are concrete actions, not moods: *bless* them that curse you, *do good* to them that hate you, *pray* for them which persecute you. The verbs are practical and costly. Nothing in the passage asks you to feel affection for your persecutor, to pretend the persecution is not happening, or to call cruelty by a nicer name. Indeed, the passage presupposes clear-eyed moral judgment: it names the evil and the unjust as exactly that, even while noting that God sends sun and rain on them anyway.

Sentimentality, as Flannery O'Connor and others have observed, is actually the *opposite* of this: it is emotion detached from cost. Sentimental "love" of enemies would be loving them in the abstract, at a distance, where it demands nothing. What Matthew 5 commands is love at close range, toward the specific person whose hand is against you — which is about as unsentimental as an ethic can get.

3. Is it strategic?

There is a genuinely strategic case for enemy-love, and it deserves respect. Gandhi and Martin Luther King Jr. demonstrated that refusing to hate one's oppressor can be politically potent: it breaks cycles of retaliation, denies the enemy the moral cover of a symmetrical fight, unsettles the persecutor's conscience, and wins over onlookers. King's formulation — "returning hate for hate multiplies hate... darkness cannot drive out darkness" — is partly a practical observation about how conflicts escalate or de-escalate. Enemy-love, on this reading, is the only exit from the feud; retaliation merely recruits you into your enemy's logic.

But the strategic reading, taken alone, fails the text in two ways. First, strategy is conditional: if hating worked better, the strategist would hate. Yet Jesus grounds the command not in effectiveness but in the character of God — "that ye may be the children of your Father." The reason given is theological and ontological, not tactical. Second, strategic love is instrumental love, love *in order to* win, and instrumentalized love has a way of curdling back into a subtler form of combat: I bless you as a technique for defeating you. The passage asks for something that persists even when it visibly fails — when the enemy is not softened, the crowd is not moved, and the cross still comes.

4. The deeper claim: moral strength grounded in reality

The best reading is the third: enemy-love as a demanding form of moral strength — but with a crucial qualification the passage itself supplies. It is not strength as self-generated heroism; it is strength that comes from seeing the world a certain way.

Consider what the command actually requires. To love an enemy you must (a) refuse to let their treatment of you dictate your character — which is a radical form of freedom; (b) continue to regard them as a human being and potential child of the same Father, resisting the almost gravitational pull toward dehumanizing those who hurt us; and (c) absorb an injury without passing it on, ending a chain of harm in your own person rather than transmitting it. Each of these is an act of power, not weakness. Retaliation is easy; any creature can do it reflexively. Non-retaliation that is not cowardice — that stands its ground, names the evil, and still wills the enemy's good — requires more self-possession than vengeance ever does. This is why the command can only be given to people it treats as strong: it assumes you have a self secure enough to give away.

The qualification is verse 45. Jesus does not say "love your enemies by gritting your teeth." He points to the sun and the rain: the Father already sustains the evil and the good alike, indiscriminately, every day. Enemy-love is therefore presented not as an unnatural feat but as *alignment with how reality is already being run*. The disciple is asked to join a generosity that is prior to them and larger than them. God's providence is not sentimental (it does not pretend the unjust are just), and it is not strategic (the rain is not a negotiating tactic); it is simply the settled character of the Giver. To love enemies is to become the kind of person — a "child" resembling the parent — whose goodness is likewise unconditioned by the worthiness of its recipients.

This also answers the justice objection. Enemy-love does not abolish moral judgment; it relocates vengeance. One can oppose an enemy's actions, restrain them, testify against them, and refuse to cooperate with their evil — Jesus himself rebukes, denounces, and overturns tables — while still refusing to will their damnation, and while praying for their transformation rather than merely their defeat. Love of enemies is compatible with resisting enemies; it is incompatible only with hating them. Bonhoeffer, who joined the resistance against Hitler while writing that the follower of Jesus must love his enemies, embodied exactly this tension: opposition without hatred, resistance without the corruption of the resister.

5. Conclusion

Enemy-love is hard to take seriously because it offends our sense of desert, contradicts our protective instincts, and has been counterfeited both by sentimentalists and by manipulators. But rightly read, it is neither sentimentality (it demands costly action toward real evildoers, seen clearly) nor mere strategy (it persists when it doesn't pay, because its ground is God's character, not its results). It is a deeper form of moral strength — arguably the deepest, because it is the only response to evil that neither surrenders to the enemy nor becomes like him. Its difficulty is not a flaw in the command but its point: sun-and-rain generosity toward the undeserving is precisely what distinguishes the children of the Father from those who love only the people who love them back. The command is difficult because it asks us to be perfect as the Father is perfect (5:48) — not flawless, but complete: whole enough, and free enough, to love without first checking whether the beloved has earned it.
