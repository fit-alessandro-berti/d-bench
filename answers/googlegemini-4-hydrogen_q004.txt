### 1. What kind of trust is being asked for?

The trust commanded in Proverbs 3:5–6 is not mere mental agreement with a set of ideas, nor is it passive wishfulness. It is **total, relational, and practical reliance on God**. Several features of the text bring this out:

* **Whole-person allegiance (*“with all thine heart”*):** In biblical usage, the “heart” is not just the seat of emotion; it is the center of the intellect, will, desires, and decision-making. Trusting “with all thine heart” means undivided loyalty—not trusting God with spiritual matters while reserving career, relationships, or crises for self-reliance.
* **Shifting your ultimate support (*“lean not”*):** The imagery of *leaning* refers to putting your weight on something for stability. God is meant to be the load-bearing foundation of your life, rather than your own grasp of how things work.
* **Active, everyday consultation (*“in all thy ways acknowledge him”*):** The word “acknowledge” carries the sense of *knowing* and *recognizing* God personally across every sphere of life (“all thy ways”). It asks for an ongoing posture of consulting God’s character and instruction before and during action—not merely nodding toward him in emergencies.
* **Confident dependence on his guidance (*“he shall direct thy paths”*):** The trust asked for assumes that God sees the whole road where we only see a few steps ahead, and that submitting to his wisdom leads to a genuinely straight, clear, and well-ordered life.

### 2. Humble self-distrust vs. anti-intellectualism

At a glance, *“lean not unto thine own understanding”* can sound like a call to stop thinking. Yet in the very next chapter, Proverbs commands: *“Get wisdom, get understanding”* (Proverbs 4:7), and a few verses after 3:5–6 it blesses the person who *“getteth understanding”* (3:13). The book of Proverbs is itself a manual of careful observation, logic, foresight, and mental discipline.

The distinction, therefore, lies not in *whether* you use your mind, but in *what status* you give your mind:

| **Anti-intellectualism** | **Humble self-distrust** |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Rejects reason and inquiry.** Treats study, critical thinking, evidence, and careful planning as enemies of faith. | **Recognizes the limits of reason.** Uses the mind diligently while acknowledging that human insight is finite, fallible, and prone to blind spots. |
| **Turns off the mind.** Confuses ignorance or thoughtlessness with spirituality. | **Dethrones intellectual autonomy.** Refuses to make *“my own understanding”* the final, self-sufficient court of appeal. |
| **Avoids counsel and learning.** Dismisses expertise, observation, and hard questions. | **Seeks wisdom outside oneself.** Actively seeks God’s wisdom and wise counsel (Prov. 11:14; 15:22) precisely because one’s private perspective is incomplete. |
| **Suspicious of “understanding” itself.** Views thinking as a threat. | **Suspicious of *self-reliance* and pride.** The warning is clarified in verse 7: *“Be not wise in thine own eyes.”* |

In short:
* **Anti-intellectualism** says, *“Do not think; just obey blindly.”*
* **Humble self-distrust** says, *“Think carefully, learn deeply, and gather wisdom—but remember you are a creature, not God. Your perspective is partial, your motives can deceive you, and your own judgment is too fragile a foundation to bear the weight of your life.”*

Proverbs 3:5–6 does not ask you to abandon your mind; it asks you to stop leaning your whole weight on your own autonomous perspective as if you were all-knowing, and instead anchor your thinking and acting in the wisdom of God.
