Love of enemies is so difficult to take seriously not because it is too beautiful, but because we suspect it is not true to how the world *works*.

Jesus in Matthew 5:44-45 is not giving private spiritual advice. He is preaching to an oppressed people living under Roman occupation. “Love your enemies” would have sounded then much like it does now: either impossibly idealistic or morally irresponsible.

There are three ways people usually hear it, and only one does justice to what he actually said.

### 1. Why we instinctively dismiss it

We don’t dismiss it because we’ve tried it and it failed. We dismiss it because it violates three deep intuitions:

**1. Justice:** Love feels like acquittal. If I love the person who harmed me, am I saying what they did was okay? That it doesn’t need to be answered for?

**2. Identity:** Enemies define us. My group knows who *we* are because we know who *they* are. To bless those who curse me threatens the clarity of tribe, party, nation, even victimhood.

**3. Survival:** Evolutionarily, psychologically, reciprocity keeps us safe. Love your friends, neutralize your enemies. An unconditional ethic feels like disarmament in a world that hasn’t disarmed.

So we relegate it to poetry. A nice sentiment for an innocent world that doesn’t exist.

But Jesus’ wording won’t let us do that. Note the verbs: *love, bless, do good, pray for*. This is not a feeling. In Greek, it’s *agapate* — not *philia* (affection) or *eros* (desire), but *agape*: active, willed goodwill toward the other. He is not commanding warm feelings, approval, trust, or even reconciliation. He is commanding *action on behalf of their good* when everything in you wants to withhold it.

That alone rules out the first misreading:

### 2. Sentimental? No.

If enemy-love were sentimental, it would mean:

> Pretend you don’t have enemies. Minimize the harm. Think nice thoughts. Be nice to be liked by God.

That is not what the text does. Sentimentality needs to deny that the enemy is *really* an enemy — “he’s not that bad, deep down everyone is nice.”

Jesus does the opposite. He names the categories with brutal realism: *those who curse you, those who hate you, those who despitefully use you and persecute you.* He takes it for granted that enmity is real, vicious, and ongoing. He never says “pretend it doesn’t hurt.”

Sentimental love collapses when face-to-face with real evil because it has no content other than niceness. It cannot say to a persecutor: “What you do is unjust, and I will not retaliate in kind.” It confuses love with permissiveness, and therefore enables abuse. 

If that were what Jesus meant, Nietzsche was right to call it slave morality — a weakling’s fantasy.

Jesus means something harder: to will the good of the person whose actions you must still call evil.

### 3. Strategic? Partly, but not primarily.

There *is* a strategic wisdom here. Modern psychology, nonviolent resistance, even game theory confirm it:

* Hate locks you into mimesis — you become what you oppose. As Martin Luther King Jr., preaching directly from this passage, said: “Hate cannot drive out hate; only love can do that.”
* Praying for an enemy — even in a secular sense, holding them in mind as a human with a history — breaks rumination and de-escalates the cycle.
* “Do good to them that hate you” disarms the tit-for-tat logic that makes every conflict permanent.

In that sense, enemy-love is incredibly practical. It’s the only strategy that has ever actually broken blood feuds.

But to reduce Matthew 5:44 to strategy — “love your enemies because it works” — is too small. Strategy is still conditional: I will be kind *in order to* change you, pacify you, win. If you don’t change, my calculation fails and I am licensed to hate again.

Jesus’ reason is not consequentialist. He gives a theological reason:

> “*That ye may be the children of your Father* which is in heaven: for he maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good…”

In other words: do this not because it is useful, but because it is *who God is*. And it is who you are meant to become.

### 4. A deeper form of moral strength

The third reading is the only one that fits the second verse.

Sun and rain in the ancient world were not poetry — they were life and death. And God gives them indiscriminately. This is what theologians call common grace: God does not distribute existence on the basis of merit. He gives Being itself even to those who misuse it.

To love your enemy, then, is not to be sentimental about evil, or clever about outcomes. It is to refuse to let the enemy’s evil determine your moral identity.

Hate says: *You have defined me. I will be a reaction to you.*
Sentimentality says: *You are not really evil. I will pretend.*
Strategic niceness says: *I will be kind to manipulate you.*

Moral strength says: *You are really wrong, and have real power to harm me, and I will not let that wrong become the law of my heart. I will act from a different source.*

That is why Jesus links it to “perfection” — *teleios* — in the next verse. It does not mean flawlessness; it means wholeness, maturity, an end-goal. A person who can only love the lovable is incomplete, conditioned by others. A person who can will the good even for the enemy has become free — his action originates in himself, grounded in God, not in the stimulus.

This is not weakness. It is the hardest discipline there is. Anyone can hate. Anyone can be nice when it costs nothing. It takes a formed character to:

* seek justice without dehumanizing the unjust
* tell the truth without delighting in destruction
* protect boundaries without closing the heart

Love does not mean trust. Love does not mean proximity. Love does not mean no consequences. You can love an enemy and still oppose them in court, in war, in protest. What you cannot do is enjoy their damnation.

That is the test King gave his movement: Can you oppose a segregationist and still be willing to be his neighbor tomorrow? If not, you have won a power struggle, not a moral victory.

---

Enemy-love is difficult to take seriously precisely because we hear “love” as private emotion in a sentimental culture. Jesus meant it as public power — the decision to break the mirror, to stop letting evil reproduce itself through you.

It fails the sentimental test because it is too honest about enemies.
It exceeds the strategic test because it doesn’t depend on success.
It endures as moral strength because it answers hate not with its opposite, but with something *else entirely*: a freedom the enemy cannot give you and cannot take away.
