The moral danger in Matthew 6:1 lies **not in visibility itself, but in the craving to be seen**. The verse explicitly ties the condemnation to intention, not location or circumstance.

### 1. The Text Points to Motive, Not Merely Exposure
The Greek behind “to be seen of them” uses a purpose clause: ὅπως φανῶσιν αὐτοῖς (“so that they may appear to them”). Jesus isn’t warning against doing good where people might notice; he’s warning against doing good *in order* to be noticed. The sin is instrumentalization: turning an act of love or obedience into a vehicle for personal reputation.

This is reinforced by what follows in verses 2–4, where Jesus contrasts those who “sound a trumpet” or pray on street corners to gain public admiration with those who give, pray, and fast in secret. The contrast is never between public vs. private per se, but between **human applause** and **divine approval**.

### 2. The Deeper Moral Danger: Displaced Worship
Public goodness becomes spiritually hazardous when the craving to be seen replaces the love of God and neighbor as the driving end. Several corruptions occur:
- **Virtue becomes performance**: Almsgiving stops being mercy and becomes theater.
- **Obedience becomes transaction**: Giving is no longer worship but a bid for social capital.
- **Love becomes selfish**: The recipient ceases to be a person to be served and becomes an audience member.

In Jesus’ critique of the “hypocrites” (ὑποκριταί, originally “actors”), the tragedy is that these outwardly righteous acts mask inward idolatry. Human praise substitutes for God’s blessing, resulting in the stark warning: “you have received your reward in full” (Matt 6:2, 5, 16).

### 3. Visibility Itself Is Not Sinful
Biblical ethics does not condemn public virtue. When done with pure motives, public good can:
- Encourage communal imitation (1 Cor 11:1; Phil 4:9)
- Model transparency in stewardship (2 Cor 8:21)
- Witness to God’s character in the world (Matt 5:16)

What Jesus rejects is not the eye of the beholder, but the heart’s misplaced allegiance. A deed hidden out of prudence or simplicity retains its moral value; a deed displayed out of pride forfeits its spiritual reward, regardless of how generous it appears outwardly.

### In Short
Matthew 6:1 identifies the moral danger as **the orientation of the will**. Visibility becomes spiritually toxic only when it is pursued as an end. The verse calls believers to examine not merely where they act, but why: are we serving God through our neighbor, or using our neighbor to serve ourselves?
