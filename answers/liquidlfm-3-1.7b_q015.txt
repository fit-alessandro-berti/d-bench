**Matthew 7:3‑5 (NIV)**  
> “And why beholdest thou the mote that is in thy brother’s eye, but considerest not the beam that is in thine own eye? … Thou hypocrite, first cast out the beam out of thine own eye; and then shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote out of thy brother’s eye.”

---

### 1. What makes the image **so sharp**?

| Element | Meaning | Why it stands out |
|---------|---------|-------------------|
| **“Mote”** – a tiny speck of dust or a grain of sand | Something very small that can be seen from the outside. | Its minuscule size makes it easy to spot when you turn your attention outward. |
| **“Beam”** – a flash of light shining inside your own eye | A powerful, blinding source of light that overwhelms the rest of the vision. | The beam is *inside* you, where no one else sees it, and it blinds you to everything else. |

**Sharpness comes from the stark contrast between the two images.**  

- **Scale & Brightness:** A mote is faint and hidden behind walls; a beam is intense and unavoidable.  
- **Location:** One is external (in another person’s eye), the other is internal (within your own).  
- **Visual effect:** Seeing the mote means you’re looking at *other people’s* problems; seeing the beam means you’re staring at *your own* failures or pride.  

Because the metaphor paints a vivid picture of **self‑focus**, it forces the listener to ask: *Am I really looking at my brother’s brokenness, or am I fixated on my own light?* The sudden realization of blindness is what makes the teaching “sharp” – it cuts through complacency instantly.

---

### 2. How does the passage **criticize hypocrisy** without banning moral correction?

1. **Labeling the speaker a “hypocrite.”**  
   In the Old Testament, a hypocrite is someone who praises righteousness or loves God but does not live up to it. Here, the speaker tells us: *“You claim to want to help, but you are a hypocrite.”*

2. **The logical sequence of actions**  
   - **Step 1:** *First* “cast out the beam out of thine own eye.”  
     → This means you must **purify yourself**—examine your own heart, sin, pride, or selfish desires.  
   - **Step 2:** After you’ve cleared your own light, you’ll “see clearly” and can then “cast out the mote out of thy brother’s eye,” i.e., genuinely help others.

3. **Why this isn’t a prohibition on doing good**  
   - The text **does not tell us to stay silent**. It merely states a necessary condition: *you must be free from your own blindness before you can effectively reach out to others.*  
   - It acknowledges that many people *want* to help but remain self‑centered. By demanding first‑hand self‑scrutiny, it guards against **hypocrisy**—the act of pretending to care while still letting your own “beam” block the view of others’ needs.  
   - Once you’ve “cleared the beam,” you gain the clarity to **actively remove the mote**—to bring justice, mercy, or restoration into the life of your neighbor.

In short, the parable **condemns self‑righteousness** (being blinded by your own light) while simultaneously **encouraging compassionate action** once that self‑examination is complete. It therefore offers a balanced path: *first, examine yourself; then, serve others.*

---

### TL;DR

- The **mote vs. beam** image is sharp because it contrasts a tiny, externally visible flaw with a blinding, internally produced flaw.  
- It exposes **self‑absorption**: if you stare at your own eye, you miss everyone else’s problems.  
- Calling the listener a **hypocrite** points out that he pretends to want to help but is actually blinded by his own shortcomings.  
- The passage **doesn’t stop you from helping**; it simply insists you must **first cast out your own beam**—i.e., undergo honest self‑reflection—so that you can later “see clearly” and truly remove the “mote” from others.
