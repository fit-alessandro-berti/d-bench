While several of the options you list accurately describe symptoms in the text, the elder brother’s **deepest problem is a fractured understanding of sonship and grace** that makes him unable to receive or celebrate unmerited mercy. Here’s how the text unravels that core issue:

### 🔍 Surface Symptoms vs. Root Cause
- **Anger, envy, wounded fairness, and pride in obedience** are all present, but they function as *symptoms*, not the root. They emerge from a deeper malfunction: **a transactional, merit-based relationship with his father**.
- His complaint hinges on accounting: *“I’ve served you faithfully… yet you never gave me a kid.”* He positions himself as a dutiful employee rather than a secured heir. Notice his distancing language: *“this thy son”* (v. 30), not *“my brother.”* He has emotionally and relationally withdrawn from the family system.
- When the father replies, *“Son, you are always with me, and all that is mine is yours”* (v. 31), he exposes the elder brother’s blindness: he already possesses everything true sonship entails, yet lives as though he must earn his standing.

### 💡 The Deepest Problem: Inability to Comprehend Unmerited Mercy
His discomfort with mercy itself is the closest single phrase, but only when properly framed: **he resents a love that operates outside human desert**. Merit-based ethics cannot coexist with grace without producing either guilt (for the “failed” one) or resentment (for the “faithful” one). The elder brother’s obedience has become self-justifying. He doesn’t trust the father’s goodness apart from performance, and thus cannot join the celebration when grace bypasses calculation.

### 📖 Context Confirms the Diagnosis
Jesus tells this parable in direct response to Pharisees and scribes who *“grumble, saying, ‘This man receives sinners and eats with them’”* (Luke 15:2). The elder brother embodies religious self-righteousness: outwardly obedient, inwardly alienated, and fundamentally threatened by divine grace extended to the unworthy. His tragedy isn’t rebellion; it’s faithful estrangement.

### ✅ In Summary
| Option | Role in the Text |
|--------|------------------|
| Anger / Envy / Wounded fairness | Emotional responses to perceived injustice |
| Pride in obedience | The engine behind his transactional worldview |
| **Discomfort with mercy itself** | ⭐ Closest to the root, when understood as **resistance to grace that shatters merit-based identity** |

**Deepest problem:** A broken relational ontology. He does not know himself as a beloved son nor the father as a gracious host. Until he exchanges his ledger for his inheritance, his service becomes prison, his fidelity becomes isolation, and the feast remains forever just beyond his door.
