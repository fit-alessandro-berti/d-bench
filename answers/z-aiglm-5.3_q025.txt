## The grammar answers the question before the theology does

The verse doesn't forbid doing alms "before men" — it forbids doing them "before men, **to be seen of them**." The Greek is purposive: *pros to theathēnai autois*, "with the aim of being beheld by them." Visibility is the circumstance; the aim is the danger. If being seen were itself the sin, Matthew 5:16 — barely a page earlier — would be unintelligible: "Let your light so shine **before men**, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father which is in heaven." Jesus commands visible goodness there. What he forbids in 6:1 is goodness done *in order to be beheld*.

The word choice sharpens this. *Theathēnai* comes from *theaomai*, the root of "theater" — not neutral visibility but spectacle. And the performers in the following verses are "hypocrites" (*hypokritai*, stage actors). The whole unit (almsgiving, prayer, fasting — the three pillars of Jewish piety) runs on theatrical imagery: audience, performance, applause, payment.

## The reward logic: two audiences, two currencies

"Otherwise ye have no reward of your Father" is not a punishment but an accounting. In 6:2, the hypocrites "have their reward" — *apechousin ton misthon autōn*, commercial receipt language meaning "paid in full." You chose your audience, and the audience paid. Human praise and divine reward are two different currencies, and the act can be cashed in only one. The transaction closes the moment the applause comes.

## The deeper point: the craving reconstitutes the act itself

Here the usual framing — "good act, impure motive" — doesn't go far enough. An act's identity is partly fixed by its intended recipient. Alms are directed toward the poor and, as *dikaiosynē* / *tsedaqah*, toward God. When the intended recipient becomes the crowd's admiration, the act changes *kind*: giving becomes advertising, and the poor man becomes a stage prop. So the act isn't stained from the outside by a bad motive; it has been reconstituted as a different act entirely. That's precisely why it can claim no reward from the Father — nothing in it was ever addressed to him.

## But visibility is not treated as neutral either

Jesus plainly regards public goodness as *hazardous*, which is why he prescribes secrecy — "in secret," the left hand not knowing — not as an absolute rule but as a discipline. The gaze of others tends to colonize the imagination; we begin performing for whoever is watching. And note the limit of the remedy: one can grow proud of one's own hiddenness — vainglory attaching itself to secrecy itself, a point the desert fathers (Cassian, Evagrius) pressed hard. If the problem were visibility, secrecy would solve it. It doesn't fully, which proves the problem is the heart's appetite, and visibility merely its most reliable occasion.

## The final turn: there is no unobserved act

Even "in secret" is immediately qualified: the Father "*seeth in secret*" (6:4, 6, 18). The verse offers no genuine privacy — only a change of audience. Every act of righteousness is performed before some gaze. So the moral question is never *seen or unseen* but *seen by whom, and done for whom*.

**In short:** the danger is the craving, not the visibility — but visibility is dangerous precisely because it is the natural habitat of the craving, and because the craving, once in charge, quietly converts the gift into a performance.
