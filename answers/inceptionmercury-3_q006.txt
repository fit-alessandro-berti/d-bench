In Mark 8:34–35, Jesus is talking to people who had left their ordinary lives to follow him. He has just told them he is going to Jerusalem to suffer, be rejected, and die. Peter objects. Jesus then turns to everyone and says: if you want to follow me, deny yourself, take up your cross, and follow me. The “self” Jesus is talking about is therefore not the self as God made it. It is the **false self**—the ego-centered self that tries to be its own center, its own Lord, its own savior.

### What self is being denied?

The self being denied is the one that says:

- “My life is mine to do with as I please.”
- “My reputation, my safety, my plans are the most important things.”
- “I must defend my ego at all costs.”

This is the self that is turned in on itself. In Christian language, it is the old self, the self-will, the ego that wants to preserve, advance, and justify itself. It is not the denial of your dignity, your humanity, or your God-given identity. It is not “self-hatred” or the abuse of people who are told to erase themselves.

When Jesus says “deny yourself,” the Greek word implies *disown, repudiate, refuse to acknowledge as the ruling reality of your life*. It is like saying: stop treating your ego as your master. Let the center of your life be something greater than yourself—Christ and his gospel.

The paradox is in the next verse:

> “For whosoever will save his life shall lose it; but whosoever shall lose his life for my sake and the gospel's, the same shall save it.”

“Life” here is the Greek word *psyche*, which also means “soul” or “self.” The person who clings to their life, identity, plans, and reputation will lose them. The person who lets go of that possessive grip for Jesus’ sake finds his or her true self.

So the self being denied is not your “true self” but your *self-generated false self*—the one built on comparison, control, achievement, and fear.

### Why does this sound liberating to some?

To many people, the ego is a prison. It demands constant effort: protecting your image, comparing yourself with others, worrying about what people think, trying to secure your own happiness, and ultimately fearing death. That is exhausting.

Jesus’ call to deny yourself is liberating because it says: you do not have to save yourself. You can stop trying to be your own Messiah. When you surrender the impossible project of self-justification, you are freed to love, to serve, to receive grace, and to find your life in something far bigger than your own ego.

This is why the gospel is good news. The people who hear it as liberation are often those who are tired of themselves—tired of their anxiety, ambition, shame, or self-obsession. They hear that self-loss is not the end but the doorway to real life.

### Why is it offensive to others?

To modern ears, “deny yourself” sounds like the opposite of mental health. We are told to affirm ourselves, believe in ourselves, put ourselves first, and never let anyone make us feel small. In that cultural context, Jesus’ words seem dangerous, even abusive.

For many, the offense is not just philosophical but personal. People who have been taught to deny themselves by abusers or manipulators may hear this verse as a weapon. It sounds like: you don’t matter; disappear; accept your suffering. When “self-denial” is used to silence victims or make people submissive, it becomes a horrible distortion of the gospel.

There is also a deeper offense. Jesus’ message says that you cannot save yourself—you must die to your own self-rule and trust in him. That offends pride. It offends the modern belief that self-fulfillment is the highest good. And it offends anyone who thinks the “cross” is unnecessary. Why should anyone have to lose their life to find it? Why can’t I just have it now, on my own terms?

In the New Testament, this is called the “offense of the cross.” To one culture it looks like foolishness; to another it looks like a scandal. A crucified Messiah is not what people expected or wanted.

### The bottom line

The self Jesus tells us to deny is the **self that sits on the throne**. It is not the self as God’s beloved creature. It is the self as idol. Jesus invites us to get off the throne and become who we were always meant to be: people who love, who give, who lose themselves in a true story, and in doing so—mysteriously—find themselves.
