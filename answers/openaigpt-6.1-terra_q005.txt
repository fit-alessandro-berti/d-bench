Enemy-love is difficult to take seriously because it asks us to resist a powerful moral instinct: when someone harms us, withholding goodwill can feel like the least they deserve. Love may seem to excuse the wrong, betray its victims, or expose us to further harm. Anger can also be an appropriate response to injustice, so the command can sound like a demand to suppress it.

But Jesus’ instruction need not mean feeling affection for an enemy or pretending the harm does not matter. In the passage, love takes practical forms: **blessing, doing good, and praying**. It means refusing to treat another person’s humanity as extinguished by what they have done.

That makes it more than sentiment. Sentiment is inexpensive when it requires no action; enemy-love can be costly precisely because the injury is real. Nor does it require removing boundaries: one can seek justice, resist abuse, protect oneself and others, and still refuse cruelty or revenge. Forgiveness and reconciliation are not the same thing, and reconciliation may be unsafe or impossible.

Enemy-love can be strategic: it may interrupt cycles of retaliation or make change possible. But its value cannot depend on those outcomes. An enemy may remain hostile. The passage grounds the command instead in God’s generosity toward both “the just and the unjust.” The aim is to resemble that generosity, not merely to win someone over.

So enemy-love is best understood as a **deeper moral strength**: the disciplined choice to oppose wrongdoing without surrendering to hatred. It is difficult not because it asks us to call evil good, but because it asks us to seek the good even of those who do evil.
