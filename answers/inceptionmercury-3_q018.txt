The juxtaposition in Ephesians 4:31–32 is deliberate: bitterness and forgiveness are two opposite ways of dealing with a wrong. Paul is not just telling us to feel better; he is calling us to a decision.

**Bitterness in the text**  
The Greek word for bitterness is *pikria*, and in the New Testament it describes a deep, resentful sourness—a heart that has held onto an offense until it becomes a settled state. It is not merely a passing feeling. Paul commands that it be “put away” (literally, “lifted off” or “removed”), which suggests a decisive act of severance. So the text treats bitterness as:

- a **feeling** that has been nursed into something more,
- a **habit** of rehearsing the hurt,
- a **chosen posture** of refusing to let go,
- and a **contagion**, because it spills out into wrath, anger, clamor, and evil speaking, and can poison the whole community. Hebrews 12:15 warns of a “root of bitterness” that defiles many.

**Forgiveness as the opposite**  
Forgiveness is not the absence of bitterness; it is the active release of it. Paul puts kindness and tenderheartedness right before forgiveness because forgiveness is not cold legalistic pardon—it is compassion in action. And then he gives the key: “even as God for Christ’s sake hath forgiven you.”

That changes everything. We forgive not because the other person deserves it, but because we have been forgiven by God in Christ. Bitterness says, “You owe me.” Forgiveness says, “I have been forgiven an unpayable debt, so I can release yours.”

**Why they are set directly against each other**  
Because bitterness is unforgiveness grown stale. It is what happens when we refuse to receive and pass on grace. The text forces a choice: you cannot be bitter and forgiving at the same time. So Paul says, *put away* bitterness, and *be* kind, tenderhearted, forgiving. The ground of that command is God’s own forgiveness in Christ—the measure, the motive, and the model.

So yes, bitterness can be a feeling, a habit, a chosen posture, and something contagious. But above all, in this passage, it is the opposite of grace. And the only cure for it is to forgive, not because the offense is small, but because God’s forgiveness of us in Christ is infinitely greater.
