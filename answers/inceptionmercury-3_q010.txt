The power being praised here is not the power of domination, control, or self-assertion. It is the power of self-emptying love.

Paul says Christ was “in the form of God” — equal with God, possessing divine glory and prerogatives. Yet he did not treat that equality as something to be jealously held or exploited. Instead, he “emptied himself”: he took the form of a servant, was born in human likeness, and humbled himself to the point of death on a cross. This is a power that looks entirely different from what we usually call power. It is the power to let go, to give, to become vulnerable for the sake of others. It is not the power that takes; it is the power that spends itself.

That is why humility joined to greatness feels unsettling. Open domination is at least coherent and predictable: the strong suppress the weak, and everyone knows where they stand. Humility joined to greatness is a paradox. It claims that the highest reality is self-giving, and that the Lord of all is the one who washes feet. It makes power look like weakness, yet it exposes our ordinary obsession with status, force, and control as a lie.

It also unsettles because it refuses to leave us as spectators. If Christ, being equal with God, could humble himself, then our ambitions to be first—our need to be recognized, vindicated, and in control—are revealed as something far smaller than what we were made for. The passage invites us not merely to admire this power, but to learn it. And that is frightening, because it means letting go of the very things we use to protect ourselves.

The power praised here, then, is the power of love that does not need to dominate—and it unsettles us because it calls us to the same kind of life.
