Enemy-love is difficult to take seriously because it runs against nearly every natural instinct we have about justice, safety, honor, and self-preservation. When someone harms us, our immediate moral reflex is to categorize them as “deserving” of punishment, contempt, or at least distance. To love an enemy feels like betraying oneself, condoning evil, or pretending the injury did not matter. That is why the command can sound either impossibly noble or dangerously naive.

But in Matthew 5:44–45, enemy-love is neither mere sentimentality nor mere strategy. It is a deeper form of moral strength rooted in the character of God.

### 1. It is difficult because it refuses to reduce the enemy to the injury

Hatred simplifies the world. It says, “You are what you did to me.” Enemy-love complicates that reduction. It insists that the person who curses, hates, or persecutes is still a human being under God’s providence. That does not excuse the wrongdoing, but it denies the injury the final word.

This is one reason the passage speaks of the sun and rain:

> “He maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good, and sendeth rain on the just and on the unjust.”

God’s generosity is not distributed according to simple desert. The enemy still receives sunlight. The persecutor still receives rain. To love an enemy is to see them, in some sense, as God sees them: not as beyond the reach of goodness, but as still existing within the space of God’s mercy.

That is a hard thing to accept, because we usually want moral reality to be tidier: good people receive blessing, bad people receive judgment, and we get to stand safely on the good side. Enemy-love unsettles that.

### 2. It is not sentimental

Sentimentality would mean pretending that harm does not hurt, that injustice is not real, or that affection can be manufactured where trust has been destroyed. But biblical enemy-love does not require us to feel warm affection toward those who hurt us.

The love commanded here is closer to willed goodwill than to emotional fondness. It says: do not let hatred govern your actions. Do not seek revenge. Do not make cruelty your guiding principle. Pray for them. Do good where possible. Refuse to become the mirror image of the one who wronged you.

This kind of love can coexist with grief, anger, lament, and even resistance. Jesus does not tell his disciples to approve of evil; he tells them not to be mastered by it.

In fact, enemy-love is often most powerful precisely when affection is absent. It is strongest when someone says, “I will not return evil for evil, even though I have every natural desire to do so.”

### 3. It may have strategic effects, but it is not mainly a strategy

There is a sense in which enemy-love can be strategically powerful. It can disarm hostility, expose injustice, break cycles of retaliation, and create the possibility of reconciliation. We see this in figures like Jesus himself, or later witnesses such as Martin Luther King Jr., who spoke of love as a force capable of transforming enemies into neighbors.

But if enemy-love is treated only as a strategy, it becomes fragile. What if it does not work? What if the enemy remains hostile? What if love leads to suffering rather than victory?

Jesus does not present enemy-love primarily as a technique for defeating opponents. He presents it as a way of being children of God:

> “That ye may be the children of your Father which is in heaven.”

The motive is not first, “This will make your enemy change,” but rather, “This reflects the character of God.” The disciple loves the enemy because God is generous to the undeserving. The point is not simply social effectiveness but participation in divine mercy.

That is why enemy-love remains serious even when it appears politically ineffective. It may not always convert the enemy. Sometimes it leads to the cross. But it still reveals what God is like.

### 4. It is a deeper form of moral strength

Enemy-love is often mistaken for weakness because it refuses retaliation. But refusal of retaliation can require extraordinary strength. It takes more discipline to absorb wrong without perpetuating it than to strike back. It takes more courage to pray for a persecutor than to curse him. It takes more moral imagination to seek the redemption of an enemy than to settle for their destruction.

This is why enemy-love is not a soft virtue. It is one of the most demanding forms of love. It requires:

- the strength to tell the truth without becoming cruel;
- the humility to admit that we too live by mercy;
- the restraint to resist making ourselves the center of moral reality;
- the faith to believe that goodness is not powerless;
- the courage to endure loss rather than become hateful.

Enemy-love does not mean allowing oneself to be abused endlessly. It does not mean abandoning justice, surrendering discernment, or pretending that evil is good. One can love an enemy and still oppose them, resist them, expose them, or protect others from them. The difference is that the heart is not ruled by vengeance.

### 5. The hardest part: it reveals that we all need mercy

Enemy-love is difficult because it forces us to confront our own need for mercy. If God sends rain on the unjust, then we too live under that rain. We are not merely the innocent victims of other people’s evil; we are also people who have failed, harmed, and needed patience we did not deserve.

To love the enemy is to say, “I will not place myself in a separate moral universe from you. I too live by grace.”

That is why the command is so serious. It is not a decoration added to ordinary morality. It is a radical reorientation of moral life around the character of God.

### In short

Enemy-love is hard to take seriously because it looks like weakness in a world that worships power. It can be dismissed as sentimental if we confuse love with feeling, or as merely strategic if we reduce it to a method for social success.

But in Matthew 5, enemy-love is something deeper: a form of moral strength that imitates the generosity of God. It refuses to let hatred have the final say, insists on the humanity of the enemy, and entrusts final judgment to God. It is difficult precisely because it is not easy. It is costly. And, for Christian faith, it is ultimately grounded in the belief that God loved us while we were still enemies.
