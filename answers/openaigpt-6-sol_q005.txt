Enemy-love is hard to take seriously because it seems to ask the injured person to surrender the one thing they still control: their judgment of the wrong. Anger can feel like fidelity to justice. Loving the person who caused harm can therefore sound like excusing the harm—or inviting more of it.

But Jesus’ examples are not sentimental feelings. Blessing, doing good, and praying are actions a person can choose even while feeling anger or grief. Nor is this merely a strategy for winning an enemy over; the enemy may never change. The reason Jesus gives is God’s own generosity: sun and rain fall on the just and the unjust alike.

So enemy-love is a form of moral strength: refusing to let another person’s wrongdoing dictate the kind of person you become. It can coexist with naming the wrong, seeking justice, and setting firm boundaries. It rejects revenge without pretending there was no injury.
