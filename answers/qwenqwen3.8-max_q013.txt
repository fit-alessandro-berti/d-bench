The passage is not rejecting worship as such. It is rejecting worship that has become a mask for injustice.

The sharpness comes from the nature of the problem. Israel’s sin is not merely that its priorities are slightly out of balance — a bit too much ritual and not enough ethics. The problem is deeper: its worship has become false. It has turned into a religious performance that gives people the feeling of being right with God while they continue to violate the basic demands of justice and covenant loyalty.

In Amos, the historical setting matters. The prophet is speaking into a society marked by wealth, religious activity, and oppression of the poor. People are keeping festivals, offering sacrifices, singing sacred songs, and maintaining the outward forms of devotion. Yet at the same time, the vulnerable are being crushed, the poor exploited, and justice perverted. The worship is elaborate, but it is disconnected from righteousness.

So God says, in effect: **“Do not imagine that all this religion impresses me.”**

The fraud being exposed is this: **the attempt to substitute ritual for obedience.**

Israel is trying to offer God what God does not ultimately want — ceremonies — while withholding what God does want — justice, mercy, and righteousness. They are treating God as if he were a deity who can be appeased by offerings, flattered by music, and satisfied with religious festivals, even while his people are living unjustly.

This is a profound spiritual deception because it makes disobedience feel holy. It allows people to say, “We are faithful; we worship, we sacrifice, we sing,” while ignoring the moral demands of the covenant. The worship becomes a way of covering oppression rather than confronting it.

That is why the language is so violent:

- **“I hate, I despise your feast days.”**
- **“I will not accept your offerings.”**
- **“Take away from me the noise of your songs.”**

The repetition intensifies the rejection. God refuses every major category of Israel’s religious life: festival, sacrifice, and song. The message is not that these things are inherently evil, but that they become detestable when offered by people who refuse to do justice.

The final verse gives the positive demand:

> “Let judgment run down as waters, and righteousness as a mighty stream.”

The contrast is striking. Instead of controlled, formal, ritual religion, God wants justice to flow like water — natural, abundant, continuous, life-giving. Justice is not supposed to be occasional or decorative. It is supposed to be as constant as a stream.

The moral fraud exposed is therefore **religious formalism**: the belief that external acts of devotion can compensate for internal corruption and social injustice.

The spiritual fraud is even deeper: it is the attempt to manipulate God through worship. The people act as if God can be bought off with sacrifices or pacified with sacred music. But God cannot be bribed. He does not accept worship that is divorced from righteousness.

In short, the passage rejects the worship sharply because it is not merely defective; it is deceitful. It presents sin as devotion. It offers God ceremony while refusing him obedience. It uses religion to create a false sense of security.

Amos’s point is not: “Worship less and be a little more ethical.”  
His point is: **“If your worship does not lead to justice, it is not true worship at all.”**
