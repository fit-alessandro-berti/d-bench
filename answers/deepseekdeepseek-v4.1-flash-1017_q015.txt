The image is sharp because it is so concrete, visual, and absurd. One man is worried about a tiny splinter in another person’s eye while a wooden beam sticks out of his own. He offers to perform delicate eye surgery while he himself is blinded. The contrast in scale—splinter vs. plank—exposes the disproportion of hypocrisy: tiny faults in others become huge, while massive faults in oneself are overlooked. And because the eye is the organ of sight, the image also says that sin distorts moral vision. The would-be corrector cannot see clearly enough to correct.

The irony is heightened by the language of “behold” and “consider”: the hypocrite carefully inspects the other’s small fault but fails to “consider” the obvious obstruction in himself. The word “hypocrite” then names the divide between the role he is playing—wise healer/judge—and the reality—a blind man needing healing first.

But the passage does **not** forbid moral correction. The decisive phrase is “**first** cast out the beam out of thine own eye; and **then** shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote out of thy brother’s eye.” The goal of removing the splinter remains. Jesus is not saying, “Never help your brother with his fault.” He is saying that correction must come from a different posture: not self-righteous superiority, but repentance, humility, self-examination, and restored sight. The passage condemns hypocritical, censorious judgment that ignores one’s own sin while managing others. It does not condemn careful moral discernment or loving correction; it purifies them. Proper correction happens only after the corrector has first allowed God’s truth to judge himself. Then he can see clearly—and help his brother rather than merely display his own supposed righteousness.
