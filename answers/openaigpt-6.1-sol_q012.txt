The passage is **severe about human nature, but its severity serves a realistic warning about self-deception**. It is not simply saying, “People are terrible.” It is saying, “People are not transparent to themselves—and God sees what they cannot, or will not, see.”

### What Jeremiah is diagnosing

In biblical usage, the **“heart”** is not merely the seat of feelings. It includes thought, desire, judgment, and will. So the problem is deeper than strong emotions: the very faculty by which we explain and evaluate ourselves can also mislead us.

The KJV’s “desperately wicked” translates language also rendered **“desperately sick”** or “incurably sick.” That gives the verse the character of a diagnosis, not merely an insult.

The surrounding verses contrast trusting in human strength with trusting in the Lord (17:5–8). Verse 9 challenges confidence in our own inward judgment; verse 10 answers “Who can know it?” with God’s searching knowledge. “Reins” means kidneys, an ancient image for the deepest inward life.

Thus, the passage is pessimistic about **unaided moral self-confidence**, not a claim that every human impulse is equally evil or that honest self-examination is impossible.

### How to talk about motives

If the heart is an unreliable witness, we should neither accept its testimony unquestioningly nor refuse to hear it altogether. We should treat our account of our motives as **important but revisable evidence**.

That suggests several habits:

- **Speak provisionally.** “I think I wanted to help, though I may also have wanted recognition” is more credible than “My motives were completely pure.”
- **Allow for mixed motives.** Love, fear, pride, duty, and resentment can coexist in the same act.
- **Look at patterns and fruit.** Verse 10 links God’s judgment to a person’s “ways” and “the fruit of his doings.” Claimed good intentions do not cancel harmful conduct.
- **Welcome correction.** Ask whether trustworthy people see a pattern your preferred explanation overlooks.
- **Avoid claiming privileged access to others’ hearts.** You can identify manipulation, dishonesty, or harm without pretending to know every inward motive.

Crucially, uncertainty about motives does **not** eliminate moral responsibility. “I cannot fully understand why I did it” is not the same as “I cannot know whether it was wrong.” We can acknowledge harm, repair it, and change our conduct before achieving a complete account of ourselves.

### Humility, not endless suspicion

The passage should not turn self-examination into an impossible demand to prove that every motive is pure. Nor should it become a weapon: “Your heart is deceitful, so you must accept my interpretation of you.” The person making that accusation has a heart too.

A fitting posture is:

> “My intentions matter, but my account of them is not infallible. I will test it against my actions, their effects, wise correction, and what God requires.”

That is neither complacency nor despair. It is **moral humility joined to accountability**: we do not know ourselves perfectly, but we can still speak honestly, receive correction, and act faithfully.
