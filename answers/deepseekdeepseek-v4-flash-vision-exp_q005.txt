“Love your enemies” is one of the most dangerous sentences ever spoken. It sounds like a beautiful dream—until the enemy is real: a tyrant, an abuser, a betrayer, someone who has cost you everything. Then it seems impossible, even morally irresponsible. Why is it so hard to take seriously?

Because it goes against every survival instinct we have. The basic moral arithmetic of the world is simple: reward friends, punish enemies. Reciprocity is the language of ethics; retribution is the language of justice. Jesus breaks that grammar. He does not say “tolerate your enemies” or “ignore them” or “be polite to them.” He says “love them.” And by “love” he does not mean a warm feeling—he means blessing them when they curse you, doing good when they hate you, praying for them when they use you and persecute you. That is not a sentiment. That is a battlefield command.

So the first reason we fail to take enemy-love seriously is that it sounds weak. We imagine a person who does nothing while being attacked, who smiles when insulted, who calls evil “misunderstanding.” But the Sermon on the Mount is not that. Jesus loved enemies by confronting them, speaking truth to them, and finally allowing himself to be killed by them. The command is not passivity. It is active, costly, self-emptying goodwill toward those who have no right to it.

Is it sentimental? No. Sentimentality is cheap feeling without action. Jesus gives three concrete actions: bless, do good, pray. You can bless someone without liking them. You can do good to someone who revolts you. You can pray for someone whose name you would rather forget. That is not a mood; it is a disciplined choice. The Greek word for love here, *agapē*, is not a flutter of emotional affection. It is a deliberate, faithful pursuit of another’s true good, especially when they are opposed to you. Sentimentality would say, “I love everyone,” and do nothing. Jesus says, “Love your enemies,” and means hard, redemptive action.

Is it strategic? In one sense, there is a strategic dimension: evil is not overcome by evil; hatred does not defeat hatred; violence does not heal the wound it creates. To love an enemy can break the cycle of revenge. It can create a space where repentance becomes possible. That is why Martin Luther King Jr. could take Jesus’ command into the streets and use nonviolent resistance as a public strategy against injustice. It is not naive. It is the only strategy that aims at the real conversion of the enemy rather than their destruction.

But Jesus does not frame enemy-love as a technique. He does not say, “Love your enemies so that you will win them over.” He says, “Love your enemies … that ye may be the children of your Father which is in heaven.” The motivation is not success. It is resemblance. God makes his sun rise on the evil and on the good; he sends rain on the just and on the unjust. God does not love only the lovable. He does not bless only the blessable. He does not withhold the sun from sinners. That is who he is. And Jesus is saying: if you want to be a child of that Father, you must share his character. Enemy-love is not a method for achieving a result; it is the result of having God’s life in you.

So the deeper answer is that enemy-love is a deeper form of moral strength. It is stronger than hatred, because hatred is a kind of enslavement. When you hate your enemy, you let your enemy define you. Your thoughts, your plans, your identity become shaped by them. But when you bless them, you refuse to let them have that power. You absorb the injury without passing it on. That takes far more strength than retaliation.

Retaliation is easy. It is natural. Any fool can hit back. But to pray for those who misuse you, to seek their good rather than their ruin, to stand in the face of evil and still hold the image of God in your heart for them—that is the power of the cross. Jesus did not love his enemies because he felt warm toward them. He loved them because he was stronger than the hatred they gave him. He absorbed it and answered it with forgiveness. That is not weakness. That is the deepest moral strength there is.

And it is difficult, maybe most difficult, because we cannot do it on our own. Our hearts need to be changed. We need to be loved first by a God who was not sentimental, who acted in history, and whose strategy—surprising, costly, never coercive—was to reconcile enemies by dying for them. When we see that, enemy-love stops being a beautiful impossible ideal. It becomes the only serious way to live in the world as a child of God.

Take it seriously, then. Not because it is easy. It is not. But because it is the power that refuses to let evil have the last word.
