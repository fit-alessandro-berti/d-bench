Bitterness is called a **root** because the passage treats it not as a single impulsive act but as a latent force that can grow. A “root” is buried. It may begin in a small grievance, lie unseen in the soul, and only later “spring up” into open trouble. That language shifts attention from the surface outburst to the hidden origin.

This implies several things:

1. **Hidden moral growth.** Bitterness can develop in secret. A man may seem outwardly calm or virtuous while a resentment is forming beneath the surface, like a root spreading beneath living soil. Hence the instruction to look “diligently”: one must watch for beginnings, not merely for visible fruits.

2. **Delayed damage.** A root takes time. It may appear harmless, even trivial, but because it has depth, it can emerge later as something far larger than its first cause. The injury is not in the moment of anger alone; it is in what that anger becomes through growth.

3. **One resentment infecting many.** “Springing up” suggests a shoot or bough that rises up and troubles not only the host but the surrounding body. The “thereby many be defiled” means one person’s bitterness can spread into relationships, fellowship, and communal grace, like a root whose branches disturb others. One man’s private resentment becomes a public source of contamination.

Thus the image warns that bitterness is not finally an act but a **seed-like origin**; if cut out early, it may prevent the larger moral defilement, but if allowed to root itself, it springs up and makes “many” impure.
