The passage praises a **self-giving, other-centered power**—not power asserted for its own sake, but power that takes the form of a servant and empties itself even to death on a cross. This is the classic biblical image of *kenosis* (self-emptying): the one who is “in the form of God” does not grasp equality with God as a possession to be defended but “made himself of no reputation,” taking “the form of a servant.”

**Why this power feels more unsettling than open domination**

1. **It denies the usual grammar of power.** Open domination is predictable and often invites resistance or at least fear. Humble power refuses the game entirely: it does not coerce, does not use superiority as leverage, and does not demand acknowledgment. That makes it harder to rationalize or oppose.

2. **It exposes the hypocrisy and insecurity of domination.** Domination needs fear, status, or superiority to work. When someone rises through self-giving—especially through suffering and sacrifice—it strips away the excuse that power must be cruel to be real. It puts the dominator in an awkward position: either imitate the humility or feel the shame of refusing it.

3. **It is hard to counterfeit.** Open domination can be performed; humble service is costly and cannot be faked in the same way. A power that goes down to death on a cross is not merely nice or polite—it is a scandalous inversion of worldly status. That inversion unsettles because it undermines the entire scoreboard of status, competition, and self-promotion.

4. **It creates a debt and a claim.** Domination demands submission; humble power, by contrast, offers worth and forgiveness, which can feel more unsettling because it cannot be paid back with mere obedience. It invites love, gratitude, and transformation—and that is harder to dismiss.

In short, the power praised here is **powerful precisely because it is self-emptying**. It feels more unsettling than domination because domination is a familiar form of threat, whereas this power exposes the emptiness of every status struggle and calls people into a different kind of greatness—one that is found in humility, service, and faithful obedience.
