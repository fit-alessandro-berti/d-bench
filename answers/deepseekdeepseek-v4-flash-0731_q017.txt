Light is hated because light reveals. And to be revealed is threatening when we are hiding something.

In John’s gospel, “light” means more than information. It means truth, holiness, God’s presence, and ultimately Jesus Himself, who said, “I am the light of the world” (John 8:12). When light comes, things become visible: not only the world as it really is, but us as we really are. That is why people who love evil love darkness. Darkness is not merely the absence of light; it is the attempt to hide from being seen.

The relation between truth, exposure, and shame works like this:

- **Truth** says: this is what is real. This is who you are. This is what you did.
- **Exposure** says: that truth is now visible. You cannot control the story anymore. You are seen.
- **Shame** says: being seen like this is unbearable. You are bad. You must hide.
- **Desire for darkness** says: if I stay hidden, maybe it doesn’t count. Maybe I don’t have to face it.

So the hatred of light is not because light is bad. It is because light forces a choice: either admit what is true, or reject the truth to protect yourself. That choice feels like a kind of death, especially when our deeds are evil. The person whose life is built on lies, self-deception, or guilt cannot endure being clearly seen.

John 3:19 says “this is the condemnation”: light came into the world, but people loved darkness rather than light. Notice that the condemnation is not that God condemns them from a distance; it is that they choose hiddenness when light is offered. Their judgment is their own refusal to come to the light. They prefer the false safety of darkness to the liberating pain of being known.

There is also a deep human problem behind this: we want to be our own gods. We want to be the ones who define what is good and evil, who we are, and what our lives mean. If we come into the light, we surrender that control. We allow our deeds to be measured by something outside ourselves. That is humiliating to the ego. So we hide. We construct a self-image, we manage what others see, and we call that “freedom.” But it is a prison.

What about the person who “does truth” and comes to the light? This does not mean a sinless person. It means a person whose deepest desire is to live honestly before God. They do not run from exposure, because they know that whatever the light reveals, God already knows. They are not trusting in their own goodness but in God’s mercy. They can even let their failures be seen, because their identity is not built on hiding. They want their true story told, because ultimately their story is God’s story: their deeds, however imperfect, are “wrought in God.” The light does not destroy them; it reveals that God has been at work in them all along.

Why would light be hated? Because we are afraid of being known. Shame whispers that if we are truly seen, we will be rejected. But the light that comes in Jesus is not a cold spotlight. It is the light of the One who says, “I did not come to condemn the world, but to save the world” (John 3:17). The light exposes us, yes—but it also offers healing. The only shame that can be healed is the shame we stop hiding.
