Isaiah 55:6–7 deliberately pairs two objects of forsaking — the wicked man's "way" (Hebrew derek) and the unrighteous man's "thoughts" (machashavot) — and this pairing is the key to the verse's whole theology of repentance.

1. Why both "way" and "thoughts"?

"Way" in Hebrew idiom is a person's habitual course of life: the pattern of conduct, the visible track one walks — deeds, practices, lifestyle, the observable direction of the life. "Thoughts" (machashavot) is broader than passing mental events; it denotes plans, purposes, devices, schemes — the inward workshop where intentions are formed, the imagination that designs what the hands will later do. The word can even carry the nuance of contrivance or calculation (as in Genesis 6:5, where "every imagination of the thoughts of his heart was only evil continually").

The prophet names both because sin lives in both places, and because the two stand in a causal relationship: the way is the fruit, the thoughts are the root. Conduct is thought made visible; a man walks outwardly the road he has first traveled inwardly. If repentance addressed only the "way," it would be behavior modification — pruning branches while the root remains. A person can reform conduct out of fear, social pressure, or self-interest while the inner life continues to plot, covet, resent, and scheme. Isaiah's demand cuts deeper: the plans themselves, the cherished projects and self-devised purposes, must be abandoned.

There is also a structural clue in the immediate context. Verses 8–9 continue: "For my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways, saith the Lord." The same two words recur. The problem, then, is not merely that the sinner does bad things; it is that his entire framework — both his course of action and his scheme of thinking — runs on a different axis from God's. Repentance is therefore not a minor course correction but an exchange of ways and thoughts: forsaking one's own and, implicitly, taking up God's, which are "higher than your ways... as the heavens are higher than the earth." The double vocabulary of v. 7 is deliberately mirrored in v. 8 to show that repentance means realignment with the divine mind, not just the divine rulebook.

Note too the parallelism within v. 7: "let the wicked forsake his way, and the unrighteous man his thoughts." Hebrew parallelism here is not mere repetition; the second line intensifies and interiorizes the first. The movement is from outside to inside — from the road the feet walk to the counsel the heart keeps.

2. What this implies about outward conduct

Outward reformation is genuinely required — "forsake" is a strong word meaning to abandon, leave behind, quit. Repentance is not merely feeling sorry or thinking differently while continuing in the same practices. The "way" must actually be left. Biblical repentance (shuv, "return," which appears explicitly: "let him return unto the Lord") is a turning with the feet, a change of direction that shows up in observable life. So the verse forbids a purely internal, sentimental repentance that never alters behavior.

3. What this implies about inward imagination

But the verse equally forbids a purely external repentance. God's claim extends to the hidden interior — the plans, fantasies, ambitions, grudges, and self-justifying reasonings that no human observer can audit. This teaches that:

- Sin is culpable at the level of intention and imagination, not only at the level of act. This anticipates the teaching later made explicit by Jesus (Matthew 5:21–28), that anger and lust in the heart already violate the commandments against murder and adultery.
- God's scrutiny reaches the heart. Only a God who "looketh on the heart" (1 Samuel 16:7) could meaningfully command the forsaking of thoughts. The demand itself presupposes divine omniscience.
- True conversion is a renovation of the mind, not a cosmetic repair of manners. The unrighteous man's "thoughts" include his whole way of construing the world — his estimate of what is good, profitable, and desirable. To repent is to have that estimate overturned.

4. What this implies about the possibility of mercy

Here is the wonder of the passage: precisely this radical, double-depth repentance is met not with grudging probation but with superabundant pardon. Two promises answer the two forsakings: "he will have mercy upon him" (racham — the tender, visceral compassion of a parent), and "he will abundantly pardon" — literally in Hebrew, "he will multiply to pardon" (yarbeh lisloach). The verb "multiply" answers the multiplicity of sins in way and thought alike: however layered and habitual the sin, the pardon out-multiplies it.

Several implications follow:

- Mercy is available, but on a schedule of grace: "while he may be found... while he is near." The invitation is urgent because the season of accessibility is not indefinite. Repentance is possible now; presumption that it will always be possible is itself one of the "thoughts" to be forsaken.
- The demand for total repentance does not make mercy harder to obtain; it defines what returning actually is. God does not pardon a person into continuing rebellion; he pardons the one who returns. Yet the returning is not a meritorious payment — the surrounding chapter has already offered wine and milk "without money and without price" (55:1). The forsaking is the empty-handed turning of the beggar toward the free feast, not the currency that buys it.
- The fact that God pardons "abundantly" answers the secret despair of the person who knows his own thoughts. Anyone who honestly surveys not only his deeds but his inner life may conclude he is beyond forgiveness. Verses 8–9 respond directly to that despair: "my thoughts are not your thoughts." Man's thought is that great sin exhausts mercy; God's thought is higher. Human forgiveness is measured, conditional, and quickly exhausted; divine pardon multiplies. The very verses that expose the depth of sin (way and thought) guarantee the greater depth of grace.
- Mercy reaches as deep as the repentance it commands. Because God requires the thoughts to be forsaken, he also pardons the sins of thought — the whole hidden history of the imagination is covered, not merely the public record of conduct.

In sum: Isaiah names both "way" and "thoughts" because sin is a whole-person disorder — conduct flowing from a corrupted inner counsel — and therefore repentance must be a whole-person return: the feet leaving the old road and the mind abandoning its old designs. The demand is total, yet it is embedded in an invitation, not an ultimatum: God is near, findable, tender in compassion, and lavish in pardon. The completeness of the required turning does not narrow the door of mercy; it describes the door, and the promise "he will abundantly pardon" assures the penitent that no accumulation of evil ways or evil thoughts can outrun the multiplying forgiveness of God.
