The image is sharp because it turns a familiar moral scene—“you have a speck in your eye”—into a reversal of power. The small thing the speaker notices in the brother becomes almost irrelevant next to the large thing hidden in his own eye. The contrast of scale—“mote”/“beam”—is not decorative; it makes self-knowledge suddenly urgent. “Beholdest” and “considerest” also tighten the focus: the failure is not merely moral weakness but a failure of attention. And “hypocrite” sharpens the charge: the person is not simply judging badly; he is performing a role of moral seriousness while blind to his own condition—like an actor stepping onto a stage while his own light is off. The passage’s force comes from concision and inwardness: it does not merely say “reprove gently”; it says the person who critiques another may be in a worse position to see clearly.

Yet it does not forbid correction. It reorders it. The passage assumes that a brother can still be helped—“Let me pull out the mote…”—but only after the larger obstacle is removed. So the point is not “never speak” but “not while your own sight is obstructed.” Hypocrisy is the failure to see that the critic’s judgment is always tethered to his own condition. Correction is legitimate, even required, but it is conditional: first cast out the beam, then see clearly enough to help. The critique is therefore not a silence but a posture of humility and readiness: honest enough toward self to make moral speech with others credible. In that sense the image warns against inward invisibility to one’s own need, not against discernment toward others.
