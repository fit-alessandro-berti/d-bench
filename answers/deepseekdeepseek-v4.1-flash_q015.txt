This image is sharp because it is deliberately exaggerated, visual, and ironic. The contrast is not merely “small sin” vs. “big sin”; it is a tiny splinter (“mote”) vs. a huge wooden beam or plank (“beam”) lodged in one’s eye. The picture is absurd: a person half-blinded by a massive plank is offering to perform delicate eye surgery on someone else. Jesus uses rhetorical questions (“why beholdest thou… but considerest not…?”; “how wilt thou say…?”) to expose the self-deception, then lands the blow with “Thou hypocrite.” The would-be corrector is play-acting as a healer while ignoring his own obvious condition.

The passage criticizes hypocrisy specifically: scrutinizing another’s small fault while refusing to “consider” one’s own greater fault. It is not condemning moral discernment or the desire to help a “brother.” The fault is not that the brother has a speck; it is that the corrector has a beam and acts as if he does not.

And the final clause shows that correction is not forbidden altogether: “first cast out the beam out of thine own eye; and then shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote out of thy brother’s eye.” The word “then” is crucial. Jesus does not say, “Never touch your brother’s eye.” He says, in effect: Deal with your own sin first; let your own vision be healed; then you will be able to help your brother clearly and humbly.

So the passage forbids hypocritical, self-righteous correction—not correction itself. It relocates moral correction after self-examination and repentance. The goal is still to remove the speck, but only from a position of restored sight, humility, and love.
