Luke actually gives us the interpretive key before the parable even begins: Jesus told this story to those who **“trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and despised others”** (v. 9). 

Rather than choosing just one item from your list, the parable shows how these flaws form a single, interconnected pathology. Here is a breakdown of what condemns the Pharisee, and why the tax collector’s posture reflects true spiritual reality.

---

### 1. What Exactly Condemns the Pharisee?

**The Root: Self-Trust (Spiritual Pride)**
The foundational sin is *autarkeia*—spiritual self-sufficiency. The Pharisee approaches God not as a needy creature, but as a moral peer presenting a favorable audit. Notice that he asks God for *nothing*. There is no confession, no petition, and no sense of dependence. Because his hands are already full of his own accomplishments, he cannot receive anything from God.

**The Symptom: Comparison and Contempt**
When righteousness is self-generated, it becomes a competitive scarce resource. Because the Pharisee measures himself horizontally (against other humans) rather than vertically (against the holiness of God), he *needs* others to be wicked in order to feel righteous. Comparison inevitably curdles into contempt (“...or even as this publican”). He uses the tax collector as a dark backdrop to make his own virtue shine brighter.

**The Distortion of Virtue: Self-Enclosure rather than Public Display**
Interestingly, unlike the hypocrites in Matthew 6 who pray on street corners to be seen by crowds, this Pharisee’s flaw isn’t necessarily public theatrics. The Greek phrasing in verse 11 (*pros heauton*) can be translated either as he “prayed silently to himself” or “prayed *concerning* himself.” Even his genuine virtues—fasting beyond what the Law required, tithing meticulously—have become a closed feedback loop. God is merely a prop in a conversation the Pharisee is having with his own ego. 

**What about False Doctrine?**
Superficially, the Pharisee’s doctrine is orthodox! He begins with, *“God, I thank thee...”*—formally acknowledging that his moral success comes from God’s grace. This makes the parable deeply unsettling: **functional self-righteousness can hide behind orthodox gratitude.** His "false doctrine" isn't an explicit heresy; it is a practical misunderstanding of the Temple itself. He treats the house of mercy as a hall of merit.

---

### 2. Why is the Tax Collector’s Posture Spiritually Truer?

The tax collector (likely a genuinely corrupt collaborator with Rome) is not justified because sin is good, or because self-loathing is a virtue in itself. His posture is treated as spiritually truer because it aligns with **reality** in three ways:

**1. Vertical Honesty (No Comparison)**
In the original Greek, the tax collector does not ask for mercy on “*a* sinner,” as the KJV translates it, but on “***the*** sinner” (*ho hamartōlos*). Just as the Pharisee puts himself in a category of one at the top, the tax collector puts himself in a category of one at the bottom. He isn't looking sideways at anyone else’s sins to excuse his own. Standing before a holy God, his own brokenness is the only thing in view.

**2. He Understands Where He Is**
Both men "went up into the temple," the center of sacrifice and atonement. The Pharisee ignores the entire theological architecture of the building—the altar, the blood, the need for reconciliation. 

Conversely, when the tax collector says, *“God be merciful to me,”* he uses the Greek verb *hilasthēti*—which isn't just a plea for leniency, but a specific liturgical cry meaning **"make atonement for me"** or **"be propitiated toward me."** Possibly looking from afar toward the altar of sacrifice, the tax collector is the only one of the two actually using the Temple for what God designed it for.

**3. The Alignment of Body and Soul**
Everything about the tax collector—keeping his distance, refusing to look up, striking his chest (a gesture of deep grief usually reserved for funerals)—shows a man without pretense. He brings his actual self to the actual God. 

### Summary
The Pharisee is condemned not because he kept the Law, nor because his theology was technically wrong on paper, but because **he used his virtue to insulate himself from needing God and loving his neighbor.** 

The tax collector goes home "justified" (declared right with God) because grace can only fill an empty vessel. By telling the absolute truth about his bankruptcy, he positioned himself in the only place where divine mercy actually operates.
