Luke supplies the answer in the introduction: Jesus tells the parable to people who **“trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and despised others”** (v. 9). The Pharisee’s problem is not that fasting, tithing, or avoiding wrongdoing is inherently bad. It is what he makes of them.

His prayer turns his religious practice into a basis for confidence in himself. Though he begins, “God, I thank thee,” the substance of the prayer is a comparison: *I am not like those people; I do these things.* His virtues become credentials, and the tax collector becomes a foil against which he declares himself righteous. So the passage condemns **self-righteousness expressed through comparison and contempt**. It is a false understanding of righteousness, though the parable is not chiefly a debate over a formal doctrine: the Pharisee behaves as though his standing before God rests on his own record.

The tax collector’s posture is treated as truer not because lowered eyes or a bowed body earn acceptance, but because his words and gestures express an honest recognition of his need: **“God be merciful to me, a sinner.”** He does not excuse himself, claim superiority, or present a record of good works. He asks God for mercy. The word translated “be merciful” can carry the sense of “make atonement” or “be propitious,” so his plea is not merely for sympathy but for God’s forgiving action.

The contrast, then, is not simply between a bad man and a good man, or between religious practice and no religious practice. It is between **reliance on one’s own righteousness, which breeds contempt for others, and dependence on God’s mercy**. The tax collector’s humility is not a meritorious performance; it is the truthful posture of someone who knows he needs mercy. That is why Jesus says he went home justified. The parable’s target is the self-trust and disdain named in verse 9—not Pharisees as a whole, nor the practice of virtue itself.
