Matthew 5:44–45 is difficult to take seriously not because it is vague, but because it is *too* clear. Jesus commands his disciples to love, bless, do good to, and pray for those who attack them. That is not a minor moral adjustment; it is a direct assault on the ordinary human economy of reciprocity, retaliation, and self-protection.

So why is it so hard to receive? And what kind of thing is it—sentimentality, strategy, or something stronger?

## Why enemy-love is hard to take seriously

Several things make it sound unrealistic or even offensive:

1. **It breaks our instinct for reciprocity.**  
   Loving those who love us is natural; Jesus says even “tax collectors” and “Gentiles” do that (Matt 5:46–47). Enemy-love asks for a love that is not triggered by worthiness, loveliness, or return.

2. **It seems to conflict with justice.**  
   We rightly sense that evil must be named, opposed, and sometimes restrained. “Bless them that curse you” sounds, to modern ears, like it might mean excusing abusers or abandoning justice.

3. **We confuse love with feeling.**  
   If love means warm affection, then “love your enemies” seems psychologically impossible. We cannot manufacture affection on command, so we dismiss the command as a pious slogan.

4. **It is costly and vulnerable.**  
   To bless and do good to an enemy is to give up the weapon of revenge and to risk further harm. That is frightening.

5. **It has often been misused.**  
   Too often, victims have been told to “forgive and forget” while oppression continues. That distortion makes people suspicious of enemy-love, and understandably so.

## Is it sentimental?

No—though it is often *reduced* to sentimentality.

Biblical love—*agape*—is not primarily a feeling. It is a settled orientation of the will toward the good of the other, expressed in action. Notice that Jesus does not command a feeling; he commands deeds: **bless, do good, pray.** Those are things you can do even when your emotions are not yet healed.

Enemy-love is therefore not “being nice,” not pretending the wrong did not happen, and not feeling affection for someone who has harmed you. It is entirely possible to love an enemy while still grieving the injury, feeling anger at the injustice, resisting the evil, and maintaining firm boundaries. What enemy-love forbids is hatred, dehumanization, vengeance, and the desire for the other person’s destruction.

Sentimentality avoids the cross. Enemy-love walks straight into it.

## Is it strategic?

It can be wise, but it is not *merely* strategic.

There is a genuine wisdom in not repaying evil with evil. Retaliation often escalates cycles of violence; kindness can disarm hostility and create space for repentance. Paul even says that feeding an enemy can “heap burning coals” on their head (Rom 12:20), and that we are to “overcome evil with good.”

But Jesus does not ground the command in effectiveness. He does not say, “Love your enemies because it works.” He says:

> “That ye may be the children of your Father which is in heaven…”

The basis is the character of God, not the likely outcome. God sends sun and rain on the evil and the good, the just and the unjust. That kindness does not always produce gratitude or repentance. Sometimes it simply manifests the generous, impartial goodness of the Creator.

If enemy-love were merely strategic, it would become a technique: kindness as a weapon, niceness as manipulation, love as a way of winning. And when the strategy failed—when the enemy remained hostile—the command would collapse. But Jesus gives it as a way of life rooted in God’s own life.

## It is a deeper form of moral and spiritual strength

Properly understood, enemy-love is neither soft sentiment nor cold calculation. It is a **cruciform moral strength**—the strength to love without guarantee.

What makes it strength?

- **It requires self-mastery.**  
  Anyone can lash out when hurt. It takes far more strength to absorb evil without transmitting it.

- **It refuses to let evil set the terms.**  
  To return hate for hate is to be conquered by the other person’s sin (Rom 12:21). To return good for evil is to break the cycle and assert a different kind of power.

- **It recognizes the image of God in the enemy.**  
  The enemy is still a human being made for God. To love them is to will their true good—including their repentance and flourishing—even while firmly opposing what they do.

- **It entrusts justice to God.**  
  Enemy-love does not deny justice; it relocates ultimate judgment to the One who judges rightly (Rom 12:19). This is not weakness. It is the courage to refuse the role of executioner.

- **It mirrors the Father.**  
  God’s sun and rain are not sentimental. They are the steady, generous, impartial goodness of a God who is kind even to the ungrateful. To be “children of your Father” is to bear that family likeness.

The clearest picture is the cross. Jesus does not love his enemies as a tactic. He loves them while they are killing him: “Father, forgive them.” That is not helplessness. It is the power of God to absorb evil and overcome it with love.

## What enemy-love is *not*

Taking enemy-love seriously does **not** mean:

- becoming a doormat;
- enabling abuse;
- refusing to protect the vulnerable;
- abandoning the pursuit of justice;
- pretending reconciliation exists where there is no repentance.

Enemy-love is fully compatible with resisting evil, setting boundaries, demanding accountability, and defending others. What it excludes is hatred, vengeance, and the inner poison of resentment.

In practice, enemy-love often begins where the heart is cold: with prayer. You may not be able to feel love, but you can pray, “Lord, bless them; change them; and change me.” Prayer is the act that begins to reorient the will and reminds us that the enemy belongs to God, not to us.

## The deepest reason it is possible

Enemy-love is difficult because it is supernatural. It is not the natural human response to harm. But the New Testament gives the ground for it:

> “While we were enemies, we were reconciled to God through the death of his Son” (Rom 5:10).

We who have received undeserved love can begin, by grace, to extend it.

So enemy-love is not sentimentality, and it is not merely strategy. It is the deepest moral strength: the God-given power to love without calculation, to overcome evil with good, and to leave the results to the Father “who maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good, and sendeth rain on the just and on the unjust.”
