**Why they are set against each other**

Bitterness and forgiveness are opposite responses to the same thing: a real injury. Both presuppose that someone actually wronged you. Bitterness keeps the debt on the books, and forgiveness cancels it. The Greek word for "forgiving" here is *charizomenoi*, from *charis* (grace), so it literally means "gracing one another." That makes the contrast with malice sharp: one holds a claim against the other person, the other gives a gift.

The grounding clause matters too: "even as God for Christ's sake hath forgiven you." For Paul, bitterness in a forgiven person is not just unpleasant but incoherent. It lives off a mercy it refuses to pass on, which is the logic of the unforgiving servant in Matthew 18.

**What kind of thing bitterness is**

I think the text supports all four of your options, arranged almost as stages.

- **Feeling:** *Pikria* is a taste metaphor, something experienced inwardly. It heads the list, and many interpreters read the list as a progression from inner resentment, to flare-ups of wrath (*thymos*), to settled anger (*orgē*), to shouting, to slander, with malice as the underlying ill-will. On that reading, bitterness is the root and the rest are its fruit.

- **Habit:** The passage sits inside the "put off the old man, put on the new" section (4:22–24), which is about a whole way of life. And "be ye kind" is more literally "*become* kind," a present-tense, ongoing process. Paul is describing a character being unlearned and another being formed.

- **Chosen posture:** It is commanded, which means it is not treated as mere emotional weather. Verse 26 is important here: "Be ye angry, and sin not: let not the sun go down upon your wrath." Anger itself is permitted. Bitterness is anger kept overnight, and the keeping is where responsibility enters. But the verb "be put away" is passive: "let it be removed from you." You are responsible for it, yet it is not removed by willpower alone. That fits verse 30, where the Spirit is the one grieved by it.

- **Contagion:** The whole passage is communal: "we are members one of another" (4:25), and "from you" is plural. The list moves from the heart into the mouth, where it becomes other people's problem. Verse 27 warns that unresolved anger "gives place to the devil," a foothold in the community. Hebrews 12:15 makes the point explicit, speaking of a "root of bitterness" by which "many be defiled."

**In short**

The text treats bitterness less as a single kind of thing than as something with a life cycle. It begins as a feeling, becomes a posture when it is kept, hardens into habit, and spreads through speech. That is probably why Paul says "*all* bitterness," meaning every stage and form of it.

It is also why the remedy is not merely "stop feeling that" but a counter-formation: become kind, tenderhearted, and forgiving. "Tenderhearted" is the fitting opposite, since bitterness hardens and forgiveness keeps the heart soft toward the person who hurt you.
