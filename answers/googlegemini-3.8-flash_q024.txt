In Ezekiel 36:26–27, God addresses the root cause of Israel’s repeated failure: the problem is not merely bad behavior, but a defective core. By contrasting a “heart of stone” with a “heart of flesh,” the passage draws a profound distinction between mere behavioral modification and ontological renewal.

Here is an analysis of the two questions:

---

### 1. External Compliance vs. Receiving a “New Heart”

* **The Source of Motivation (Outside-in vs. Inside-out):**  
  **External compliance** is driven by external pressures—laws, fear of punishment, social expectation, or the raw effort of the will. The individual may perform the required actions, but their underlying desires remain unchanged, often resulting in resentment, hypocrisy, or exhaustion.  
  By contrast, receiving a **“new heart”** represents an interior transformation. The Hebrew concept of the *heart* (*lev*) encompasses the mind, will, and affections. A new heart means that a person’s desires are realigned with God's nature. Obedience ceases to be a grudging duty imposed from the outside; it becomes the natural overflow of what a person loves.

* **Agency and Power (“I will cause you”):**  
  Under external compliance, the burden of execution falls entirely on human willpower, which the history of Israel proved to be insufficient. In verse 27, God promises: *“I will put my spirit within you, and cause you to walk in my statutes.”* The dynamic shifts from human striving to divine enablement. God supplies the moral power to fulfill the very things He commands.

* **Durability:**  
  External compliance is fragile. When surveillance ceases, pressure fades, or temptation becomes severe, compliance collapses. The “new heart” provides an enduring, organic impulse toward righteousness because the law is no longer just written on tablets outside the person, but woven into their nature (anticipating Jeremiah 31:33).

---

### 2. Why Hardness is Described as a Material Condition

Ezekiel, writing to exiles whose nation had been crushed because of persistent rebellion, does not describe the human problem as a minor flaw, an intellectual misunderstanding, or a lack of education. He describes it as a **material pathology**—a “heart of stone”—for several specific reasons:

* **Incurability and the Need for Replacement (Not Reform):**  
  You cannot educate, persuade, or train a stone to beat. You cannot soften a boulder by speaking to it or commanding it to be flexible. By portraying the heart as stone, the text emphasizes that the human problem cannot be solved by moral self-improvement. The old heart cannot be repaired; **it must be surgically excised and replaced.** It requires an act of divine re-creation.

* **Total Unresponsiveness:**  
  Stone is cold, rigid, unfeeling, and impenetrable. A blow against stone yields no life; water runs off it without penetrating it. Spiritually, a stony heart is entirely numb to God’s warnings, His mercies, and His presence.  
  In contrast, **“flesh”** here does not refer to the sinful nature (as it often does in Paul’s epistles), but to living, warm, sensitive human tissue. A heart of flesh has a pulse; it can bleed, feel, and respond. It yields to touch and is receptive to divine instruction.

* **Human Inability (Total Dependence on God):**  
  No patient can perform an open-heart transplant on themselves. By framing hardness as a solid, alien substance lodged in the human chest (*“I will take away the stony heart out of your flesh”*), the text highlights human helplessness. The removal of the stone and the insertion of living tissue can only be performed by the Sovereign Creator. 

### Summary

Ezekiel 36 moves beyond the demand for external conformity to declare that humanity’s fundamental problem is **spiritual deadness, symbolized by an inert mineral within the chest.** True obedience is not the triumph of the human will over bad habits; it is the miraculous result of divine surgery, wherein God replaces an unfeeling, unresponsive core with a living heart animated by His own Spirit.
