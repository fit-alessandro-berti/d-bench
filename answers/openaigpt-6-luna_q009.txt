In Romans 12, victory over evil is **not defeating an enemy by becoming like the enemy**. Paul has just said not to repay evil for evil and, “as much as lieth in you,” to live peaceably. He then calls for active goodness: feed the hungry enemy, give the thirsty enemy drink. The final line names the aim: “overcome evil with good.”

That is not passivity. It takes restraint and courage to refuse retaliation while still acting for another person’s good. The response breaks the cycle in which injury produces revenge, and it leaves judgment to God rather than claiming vengeance for oneself.

The phrase “heap coals of fire on his head,” drawn from Proverbs 25:21–22, is interpreted in different ways. It may picture the enemy’s painful shame or awakening at being treated generously; some readers also hear an image of divine judgment. Either way, it should not turn kindness into a covert weapon. **If the goal is to feed someone in order to torment or humiliate them, the act has slipped back into revenge.** Paul’s command is to do good, not to disguise retaliation as goodness.

So the command is genuinely strong—though not a demand to enable abuse or abandon wise boundaries. It calls for mercy without surrendering moral clarity: oppose evil, but do not let evil dictate what kind of person you become.
