## The problem is the craving, but visibility is where it grows

**The grammar points to motive.** The key phrase is *"to be seen of them."* The Greek (*pros to theathēnai autois*) expresses purpose, and the verb shares its root with *theatron*, "theater." Jesus is not forbidding good deeds that happen to be seen. He is forbidding good deeds *staged in order to be seen*. The coming examples confirm this: the "hypocrites" (*hypokritai*, literally "stage actors") sound trumpets and pray on street corners.

**The broader context rules out visibility as the problem.** Just a few verses earlier, Jesus says:

> "Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father which is in heaven." (Matthew 5:16)

The two sayings don't contradict each other because they differ in *whose glory is sought*. In 5:16, others see and glorify the Father. In 6:1, others see and admire the giver. The same visible act can point either way.

## Why the craving is morally dangerous

1. **It changes what the act is.** Almsgiving is meant to be an act of mercy toward someone in need. Done for an audience, it becomes self-promotion, and the poor person becomes a prop in someone else's performance.

2. **It substitutes one reward for another.** Jesus says of the performers, "They have their reward" (6:2). The verb *apechousin* was a commercial term meaning "paid in full," as on a receipt. The danger isn't that approval is sinful to enjoy. It's that the performer has chosen the lesser payment and closed the account.

3. **It displaces God as the audience.** The phrase "your Father which is in heaven" frames the whole passage. The question is *before whom* you live. Performing for people means human opinion has quietly become the judge whose verdict matters.

4. **It hollows out the self.** Someone who habitually does good in order to be seen gradually becomes dependent on being seen. Their goodness can't survive without an audience, which suggests it was never quite goodness.

## Why Jesus still prescribes secrecy

If the problem is motive, why does Jesus tell people to give "in secret" (6:4)? Because visibility is the *occasion* for the craving even if it isn't the sin itself. Secrecy is a discipline that starves the desire for applause and tests whether the act can stand without it. It is practical wisdom about human weakness, not a rule that public generosity is always wrong.

## A subtler point

Jesus goes further: *"Let not thy left hand know what thy right hand doeth"* (6:3). This suggests the audience can be internal. A person can give in total secrecy and still perform for themselves, savoring their own virtue. The ideal isn't just hidden giving but self-forgetful giving, where attention rests on the need and on God rather than on one's own goodness.

## Summary

Visibility itself is morally neutral, and sometimes good (5:16). The danger lies in the craving to be seen, which turns mercy into theater, trades God's reward for human applause, and makes goodness depend on an audience. Secrecy is the remedy because it exposes and weakens that craving, but the deeper aim is a heart that no longer needs any spectator, including itself.
