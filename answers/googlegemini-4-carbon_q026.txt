In Matthew 12:34–35, Jesus dismantles the idea that speech is merely a surface-level behavior detached from a person’s core identity. Coming right after his metaphor of a tree being known by its fruit (v. 33) and right before his warning that humans will give an account for "every idle word" (v. 36), this passage establishes a direct, unbreakable pipeline between a person's inner nature and their outward communication.

### 1. Speech and the "Moral Stockpile"
Jesus uses two distinct metaphors in the Greek text to connect speech to the hidden life of the heart: **hydraulic overflow** and **vaulted inventory**.

*   **The Mechanics of Overflow ("Abundance"):** The word translated as "abundance" (*perisseuma*) refers to a surplus—what is left over when a container is filled past capacity. In ancient Jewish thought, the "heart" (*kardia*) was not merely the seat of emotion, but the command center of the intellect, will, and desires. Jesus suggests that a person’s internal meditations, cherished resentments, loves, and intentions accumulate until the heart reaches capacity. Speech is simply the spillover. You cannot forever contain what you continually cultivate; eventually, pressure builds, and the surplus exits through the mouth.
*   **The Moral Warehouse ("Treasure"):** In verse 35, Jesus shifts the metaphor to a "treasure" (*thēsauros*), which means a storehouse, vault, or treasury—not necessarily the treasure itself, but the chest or room where goods are stockpiled. Over a lifetime, every person acts as a collector, stocking their inner vault with either malice, pride, and deceit, or grace, truth, and goodwill. 
*   **The Principle of Supply:** By asking, *"How can ye, being evil, speak good things?"* Jesus points out a logistical impossibility. A merchant cannot pull gold coins out of a vault stocked entirely with counterfeits. While a person might manage a scripted, hypocritical performance for a moment, their unguarded, habitual, or pressured speech can only draw from the actual inventory available inside them.

### 2. Symptoms, Choices, or Revelations?
Rather than forcing us to choose one category, the grammar and context of Matthew 12 suggest that words function as **all three simultaneously**, depending on the angle from which you view the act of speaking.

**Words as Symptoms (The Involuntary Condition)**
The phrase *"out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaketh"* frames speech diagnostically, much like a symptom of an underlying health condition. Just as a fever naturally accompanies an infection, toxic speech naturally accompanies a corrupted heart. The Pharisees could not consistently speak "good things" because their internal condition made evil speech an inevitability. As a symptom, speech points backward to an existing internal state that precedes the conversation.

**Words as Choices (The Deliberate Agency)**
If words were *only* involuntary symptoms, people could plead innocence by claiming they couldn't help what spilled out of them. Verse 35 prevents this excuse by shifting to active agency: a person *"bringeth forth"* (*ekballō*—literally casts out, sends forth, or extracts) things from their treasury. Here, the speaker is pictured as a householder deliberately walking into their storeroom, selecting an item from the shelf, and handing it to the world. Furthermore, the stockpile itself was built through thousands of prior micro-choices regarding what thoughts to harbor. (This moral agency is confirmed in verses 36–37, where Jesus holds speakers legally accountable for their words—a judgment that only makes sense if choice is involved.)

**Words as Revelations (The Public Unmasking)**
Because another person's heart is an invisible, locked vault, there is no way for observers to inspect it directly. Words act as revelations—epistemological evidence that makes the invisible visible. When someone speaks, they unlock the vault and put a sample of their private inventory on public display. In the context of the passage, the Pharisees had just accused Jesus of casting out demons by the power of Satan (v. 24). Jesus argues that their slanderous accusation did not actually reveal anything about *Him*; rather, it served as an inadvertent self-revelation, exposing the hidden malice stockpiled in *their* own hearts.

### Summary
In this passage, **choice** stocks the vault and selects the words; **symptom** explains why the mouth inevitably defaults to the heart's true nature when under pressure; and **revelation** is the result, exposing a person's hidden moral inventory to the light of day.
