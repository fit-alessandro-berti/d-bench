**1.  Context and Core Claim**

In Matthew 12:34‑35 Jesus confronts the scribes and Pharisees, accusing them of “being vipers” who nevertheless “speak good things.”  The rhetorical question—*How can you, being evil, speak good things?*—sets up a simple but profound principle: **the words that come from a person’s heart are a direct outflow of that person’s moral stock‑pile**.  The next two verses spell out the mechanism:

> “For out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaketh.  
> A good man, out of the good treasure of the heart, brings forth good things; and an evil man, out of the evil treasure, brings forth evil things.”  

The passage therefore ties **speech** to an **inner reservoir** (the heart) that either holds “good treasure” or “evil treasure.”  What is spoken is not a random performance; it is the visible expression of an invisible moral inventory.

---

**2.  The Heart as a Hidden Moral Stock‑pile**

In biblical anthropology the *heart* (Hebrew *lev*, Greek *kardia*) is the seat of will, emotion, and moral decision‑making.  It is described as a *treasure* (Greek *θησαυρός*)—a hidden store of values, desires, and intentions.  Because it is concealed, the heart is often likened to a vault that only God can fully open (Psalm 139:23‑24).  When Jesus says that the mouth “speaks from the abundance of the heart,” He is saying that **what we utter is the overflow of whatever moral wealth (or poverty) lies inside us**.

---

**3.  Speech as a Symptom of Inner Condition**

A symptom is an outward sign that reveals an internal state.  The passage treats words exactly like a symptom:

* **Vipers’ venom** – The “generation of vipers” is a metaphor for a people whose very nature is poisonous.  Their evil hearts produce a toxic speech that looks “good” on the surface but is in fact harmful.  
* **Abundance of the heart** – Just as a fever signals illness, the volume and quality of our speech signal the moral condition of the heart.  A person who habitually speaks harshly is displaying the “evil treasure” within; a person who speaks graciously is showing the “good treasure.”

Thus, **words function as symptoms**: they betray the hidden moral stockpile that the speaker may be trying to conceal.

---

**4.  Speech as a Choice**

While symptoms are involuntary, the passage also implies a **volitional dimension**:

* **Deliberate selection** – The phrase “bringeth forth” (Greek *ἐκβάλλει*) suggests that the speaker *chooses* which words to let out.  An evil person does not *have* to utter evil; he *decides* to draw from his evil treasure.  Likewise, a good person *chooses* to let good things flow from his good treasure.  
* **Moral agency** – Jesus’ warning to the religious leaders is that they *choose* to perform a religious façade (“speaking good things”) while their hearts remain corrupt.  The choice to speak kindly or maliciously is a moral act that shapes the speech.

Hence, **speech is also a choice**, and the hidden moral stock‑pile is the raw material from which that choice is made.

---

**5.  Speech as a Revelation**

Because speech springs from the heart, it **reveals** what is concealed:

* **Exposing hypocrisy** – The scribes and Pharisees could talk eloquently about the law, yet their actions betray an “evil treasure.”  Their words, when examined in light of the passage, become a *revelation* of their true moral state.  
* **Discernment for others** – Jesus invites His listeners (and us) to look beneath the surface: “Judge not before the judgment seat of God” (Matt 7:1) is echoed here—only by reading the *fruit* of the heart (the speech) can we perceive the inner condition.

Thus, **words serve as a revelation**—they disclose the hidden moral stock‑pile to those who are willing to see.

---

**6.  Putting It Together: All Three Aspects Interwoven**

The passage does not limit speech to a single category; rather, it presents a **triadic relationship**:

| Aspect | How it appears in the text |
|--------|----------------------------|
| **Symptom** | The outward utterance mirrors the inner moral condition (the “abundance of the heart”). |
| **Choice** | The speaker deliberately draws from either a good or evil treasure, making a moral decision each time he speaks. |
| **Revelation** | The resulting words become a transparent sign that can be read by others to discern the speaker’s true character. |

In other words, **words are simultaneously symptoms, choices, and revelations**.  The hidden moral stock‑pile (the treasure) determines the *symptom* (the speech), the *choice* (which treasure to draw from) is exercised in the act of speaking, and the *revelation* (the public expression) allows observers to read the heart’s condition.

---

**7.  Practical Implications**

1. **Guard the heart** – Since speech is a symptom of inner morality, cultivating a “good treasure” (a heart filled with love, mercy, and truth) is the first step toward pure speech (cf. 2 Cor 13:5).  
2. **Be mindful of volition** – Recognize that we *choose* what to say; the same moral decision that leads to good deeds can lead to harmful words.  Disciplining the tongue (James 1:21) is a matter of aligning our choices with the good treasure.  
3. **Use speech wisely** – Because words reveal the heart, we should be careful not to assume a person’s goodness solely from eloquence.  Conversely, we can use honest, compassionate speech to help others discover the truth of the heart’s condition (e.g., evangelism that points to the need for inner transformation).

---

**8.  Conclusion**

Matthew 12:34‑35 teaches that **the mouth is a conduit for the heart’s hidden moral wealth**.  The passage therefore portrays speech as:

* **A symptom** of the inner moral condition,
* **A choice** made by the speaker from the treasure that resides there, and
* **A revelation** that can expose both the speaker’s and the listener’s true spiritual state.

Understanding this threefold link helps us see why Jesus warns the religious leaders—and, by extension, all of us—to examine the “treasure” within: only when the good treasure of the heart is cultivated will our words be a genuine fruit of goodness; when the evil treasure dominates, our speech becomes a dangerous symptom of corruption, and it inevitably reveals the truth of our moral stock‑pile.  In the Christian life, therefore, the discipline of the heart is inseparable from the purity of our speech.
