Paul puts bitterness and forgiveness directly across from each other because they are two opposite ways of living in the new humanity. In Ephesians 4, he is talking about “putting off the old self” (v. 22) and “putting on the new self” (v. 24). Bitterness belongs to the old self; kindness and forgiveness belong to the new self. They cannot share the same heart, and they cannot share the same church.

## What is bitterness in this text?

The Greek word used here is *pikria*, which literally refers to something sharp, sour, or poison-like. It is a settled, fermented resentment. It can begin as a feeling of hurt or injustice, but it becomes something more. In Ephesians 4:31, bitterness is not just an emotion like “I feel hurt right now.” Paul treats it as a posture, a habit, and a poison.

Notice the list in verse 31:

> “Let all bitterness, and wrath, and anger, and clamour, and evil speaking, be put away from you, with all malice.”

Bitterness is the root; the other words are its fruit:

- **Wrath** is a settled, chronic anger.
- **Anger** is an explosive flare-up of the same root.
- **Clamour** is the public shouting, the angry outcry that spills out.
- **Evil speaking** is slander and abusive talk.
- **Malice** is the underlying desire to hurt.

So Paul is saying: if you let bitterness live in you, it will not stay quiet. It will speak. It will shout. It will poison the way you talk and the way you treat other people. That is why the text treats bitterness as contagious. In Hebrews 12:15, it says:

> “Looking diligently… lest any root of bitterness springing up trouble you, and thereby many be defiled.”

A root of bitterness defiles many people. It spreads. It contaminates a home, a friendship, a congregation.

So yes: bitterness is a feeling, but the Bible treats it as more than a feeling. It is a chosen posture. It is a habit you feed. It is a contagious spiritual poison. And Paul commands the church to put it away.

## Why is forgiveness placed directly against it?

Verse 32 begins with a contrast:

> “And be ye kind one to another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, even as God for Christ’s sake hath forgiven you.”

Paul could have said simply, “Stop being bitter.” But he does not stop there. He says bitterness is not just to be suppressed; it is to be replaced. The cure for bitterness is not “try to feel happy.” The cure is forgiveness.

Forgiveness is the opposite of bitterness in this way:

- Bitterness says: “I will hold this against you. I will remember what you did. I will keep the record of your debt.”
- Forgiveness says: “I will release you. I will cover the debt. I will refuse to hold it over you.”

Bitterness locks you inside your hurt. Forgiveness sets you free from the need for revenge. Bitterness protects your wound. Forgiveness lets Christ bind your wound. Bitterness makes you hard and self-focused. Forgiveness makes you “tenderhearted” and “kind.”

The phrase “even as God for Christ’s sake hath forgiven you” is important. Paul is not saying, “If you forgive others, God will forgive you.” He says the opposite: because God has already forgiven you in Christ, you have the grace and the reason to forgive others. You are not forgiving others to earn God’s mercy; you are forgiving others because God’s mercy has already reached you.

God’s forgiveness to us is the pattern. God did not wait for us to feel sorry enough. He did not wait for us to make up for our wrongs. He acted “for Christ’s sake.” He gave His Son to take our debt. Now, because of what you have received, you are called to give others what you never owed them: grace.

## The practical lesson

The text is not asking you to pretend you were not hurt. Hurt is not the same as bitterness. God does not call grief sin. But He does call bitterness sin, because bitterness is what happens when we let hurt ferment and become a ruling posture.

Paul’s command is clear:

> “Let all bitterness… be put away from you.”

That is an action. You can do it. God’s grace makes it possible. Then:

> “Be kind one to another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another.”

That is also an action. It is a decision, and it becomes a habit, and the habit forms the heart. Bitterness and forgiveness are placed face to face because there is no middle ground between them. You cannot nurse bitterness and truly forgive. You cannot forgive and keep a bitter root alive.

Choose the new life. Choose the cross. Forgive as you have been forgiven.
