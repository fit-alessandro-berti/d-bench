In Romans 12:20–21, **victory over evil means refusing to let evil determine the character of your response—and actively answering it with good.** The enemy’s wrongdoing must not turn you into its reflection.

### What counts as victory?

Paul does not say, “Overcome your enemy,” but **“overcome evil.”** That distinction changes the goal. Victory is not merely making the other person suffer, submit, or lose. It is breaking the pattern in which injury produces retaliation, which produces further injury.

Feeding a hungry enemy is a concrete act of that resistance. You recognize a real human need without pretending the person’s hostility is right. Their wrongdoing does not get to dictate whether you do what is good.

### What about “coals of fire”?

Paul is quoting Proverbs 25:21–22. The image is debated. It is commonly understood as the burning shame or remorse that unexpected kindness can awaken; some interpreters connect it with divine judgment.

Either way, the surrounding passage rules out treating kindness as disguised revenge. Immediately before this, Paul says not to avenge yourselves but to leave judgment to God (Romans 12:19). Immediately afterward, he says to overcome evil **with good**.

So the command cannot coherently mean, “Be nice because that is the cleverest way to hurt them.” Kindness may expose wrongdoing and bring painful conviction, but **the enemy’s pain is not the good you are commanded to seek**.

### Weak, manipulative, or strong?

- **Not weak:** This is active care, not mere passivity. It requires the self-command to resist retaliation and the courage to treat an enemy as a person rather than merely as a threat.
- **Not manipulative:** Genuine goodness is not a technique for making someone feel indebted, ashamed, or compliant. If apparent generosity is only a weapon, it reproduces the hostility the passage tells us to overcome.
- **Genuinely strong:** It refuses both to excuse evil and to imitate it. It places moral integrity above the immediate satisfaction of revenge.

This does **not** require remaining in danger, granting an unrepentant person access to you, or abandoning accountability. Earlier, Paul qualifies the pursuit of peace: “If it be possible, as much as lieth in you” (12:18). You can seek protection and justice without making cruelty your answer to cruelty.

Nor does the passage promise that every enemy will repent. Their response is not under your control. **The victory begins when their evil fails to recruit you into doing evil—and your response instead makes goodness present where hostility would otherwise prevail.**
