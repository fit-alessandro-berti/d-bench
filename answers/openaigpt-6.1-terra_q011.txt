The movement from “cease to do evil” to “learn to do well” suggests that goodness is more than restraint. Stopping harm matters, but it is not the whole of a just life.

Isaiah gives that positive goodness a concrete shape: **seek justice, relieve the oppressed, defend the fatherless, and plead for the widow**. These are active responsibilities toward people who are vulnerable, not merely rules about what to avoid.

The word *learn* is significant too. Doing good is something people must cultivate through attention and practice. In the passage’s wider context, Isaiah challenges a people whose religious observance coexists with injustice. Genuine cleansing, then, is shown not simply by worship or by refraining from wrongdoing, but by a practiced commitment to justice and care for others.
