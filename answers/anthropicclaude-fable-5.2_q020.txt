## Why a root, not an act

An act is an event. It happens, it can be named, and it ends. A root is a living condition that produces things. By choosing this image, the writer points past individual bitter words or deeds to the source underneath that keeps generating them. You can apologize for an act, but a root must be dug out.

The phrase echoes Deuteronomy 29:18, where Moses warns of "a root that beareth gall and wormwood": a person whose heart has quietly turned from God while he tells himself, "I shall have peace." So "bitterness" here is broader than resentment in the modern sense. It is a poisonous inner disposition that yields bitter fruit. Resentment still fits naturally, though. The chapter has just discussed enduring hardship and discipline (12:5–11), which are the conditions in which people grow bitter toward God and each other.

## Hidden moral growth

Roots grow underground and are established before anything shows. The image suggests that:

- **The inner life comes before visible behavior.** By the time bitterness shows in speech or conduct, it has already been fed for a long time by rehearsed grievances and nursed injuries.
- **Self-deception is easy.** Like the man in Deuteronomy, a person can look peaceful on the surface while something very different grows beneath.
- **Surface correction is not enough.** If you cut a weed at ground level, it comes back. Managing behavior without addressing the heart leaves the source intact.

## Delayed damage

The root is "springing up": it grows upward and emerges later. Its harmlessness today says nothing about tomorrow. The next verses give the example of Esau, whose contempt for his birthright seemed trivial in the moment and whose loss became clear only afterward, "though he sought it carefully with tears." The time to deal with a root is when it is small and seems insignificant, which is when one is least inclined to bother.

## Spread to others

The verse moves from "any man" to "many be defiled." Bitterness does not stay private:

- A bitter person retells grievances, recruits sympathizers, and teaches others to see through the same lens.
- Suspicion and factions erode trust, so "follow peace with all men" becomes impossible.
- "Defiled" is the language of contamination, the opposite of the "holiness" in verse 14. Bitterness is pictured as spreading like an infection or an invasive plant.

## The prescribed response

Two details matter. First, "looking diligently" is plural and comes from the Greek word for oversight (*episkopountes*). The whole community is to watch over one another, because a root in one person is everyone's concern. Second, the warning sits beside "lest any man fail of the grace of God." Bitterness takes hold where grace is missed. A heart that stops receiving grace stops extending it, and in that soil the root grows.

So the image teaches that sin begins before it shows, costs more later than it appears to now, and never belongs only to the person who harbors it.
