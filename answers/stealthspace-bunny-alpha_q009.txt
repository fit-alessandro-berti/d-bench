**Romans 12:20–21 defines victory as refusing to let evil govern your response, and actively opposing it with good rather than retaliation.** It is neither passive acquiescence nor “winning” by hurting the other person.

### What victory means in context
Paul’s immediate point is that **personal vengeance belongs to God** (Romans 12:19). The believer should not become the instrument of the judgment he might deserve.

The enemy is a person, while evil is the wrongdoing and the retaliatory spirit it can awaken in us. Victory therefore means:

- **Refusing to be morally conquered by the wrong you received.**
- **Responding with concrete good:** feed someone who is hungry; give someone who is thirsty drink.
- **Trusting God to handle the ultimate outcome**, rather than making retaliation the basis of your goodness.

The enemy’s response does not determine whether you have obeyed. You are not promised that your kindness will produce repentance, gratitude, or even a pleasant encounter.

### Is the command weak?
No—its strength is **costly rather than coercive**. Giving goodness to someone who has harmed you requires resisting the natural impulse to retaliate. It breaks the cycle in which each injury generates another injury.

But that strength does not mean it is effortless, painless, or a promise of safety. Paul is not saying that wrongdoing carries no consequences, that reconciliation is always possible, or that believers must ignore genuine danger. Trusting God does not require surrendering appropriate boundaries or abandoning the call to pursue justice.

### Is it manipulative?
Not in its fundamental logic, although kindness can be twisted into manipulation. The crucial distinction is this:

> Is your goodness a way of refusing evil and honoring God—or a bargain designed to make the other person feel guilty and surrender to you?

The “coals of fire” image suggests that goodness can leave the wrongdoer exposed, troubled, or unable to evade the truth of what they have done. But Paul is not prescribing humiliation as a self-help technique. **We are responsible for our good; we are not responsible for manufacturing the other person’s repentance.**

So the command is genuinely strong: **it calls for goodness precisely where retaliation is tempting, and trusts God rather than taking justice into our own hands.** Its victory is not domination over the enemy; it is resistance to becoming like the evil you oppose.
