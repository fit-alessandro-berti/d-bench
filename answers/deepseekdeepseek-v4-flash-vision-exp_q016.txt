The elder brother’s deepest problem is not just anger or envy, though those are real. The root is **pride in his own obedience**, which makes him **uncomfortable with mercy itself**.

Notice how he speaks to his father:

> “Lo, these many years do I serve thee, neither transgressed I at any time thy commandment…”

That is not a son speaking; that is a hired worker. He has reduced his relationship with his father to **a contract**: *I behaved, so I deserve a reward.* His obedience was not joyful love; it was self-justifying performance.

So when the father welcomes the prodigal with grace, the elder brother sees it as an injustice. He calls him “this thy son,” not “my brother.” He cannot rejoice, because his whole identity is built on being better than the prodigal. If grace can save the prodigal, then the elder brother’s righteousness is not as special as he thought.

The father’s response exposes the real problem:

> “Son, thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine.”

The elder brother already had everything. He did not need a reward; he already possessed the father’s estate and the father’s presence. But he wanted it as a wage, not as a gift.

So the deepest problem is **self-righteousness**—a pride in his obedience that leaves him outside the feast of grace. He is just as lost as the younger brother, but lost in a different way: lost in the far country of “I deserve it.”
