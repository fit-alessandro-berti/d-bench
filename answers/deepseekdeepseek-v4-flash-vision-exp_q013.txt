The sharpness is intentional. Amos is not telling a slightly overzealous congregation, “Try harder to practice what you preach.” He is exposing worship that has become a lie. The people were not irreligious; they were extremely religious. They kept the festival calendar, brought offerings, and filled the temple with music. But in the courts and markets, they were crushing the poor, bribing judges, and turning away the needy (Amos 5:10–12). So God says: I hate it. I despise it. I will not accept it. Take it away. Stop singing.

Why so sharp? Because in God’s covenant, justice is not one item on a list that should be balanced alongside worship. True worship already includes justice. And when worship is disconnected from justice, it stops being worship. It becomes noise.

The fraud exposed is a sacred hypocrisy: using religious activity as a shield for injustice. The worshippers were offering sacrifices, as if bribing God to leave their corrupt lives alone. They were singing about God while robbing the poor. They were saying, “We are Your people,” but God says, “Those are *your* feast days, not Mine.” Their religion was not a bridge to God—it was a mask.

In verse 21, God says, “I will not smell in your solemn assemblies.” That is a deliberate image taken from sacrifice: the smoke of offerings was supposed to rise as a “pleasing aroma” before God. But God says, “I won’t even breathe it in.” He doesn’t want the smoke of their incense; it stinks to Him, because it smells like injustice.

Verse 23 is startling: “Take away from Me the noise of your songs.” God would rather have silence than beautiful music that hides an ugly life. They did not need better song balance; they needed repentance. Their music was not worship; it was noise.

The moral and spiritual fraud is this:

- **Worship used as compensation.** They thought sacrifice and songs could pay God back for their unjust dealings. But God cannot be bribed with liturgy.
- **Worship used as a substitute for obedience.** Religion feels holy, but if it doesn’t produce justice in the city, it is counterfeit.
- **Worship used as self-deception.** They could gather at festivals and feel blessed, while their own sin was sitting at the gate, crying out against them. It was cheap grace.
- **Worship used as violence.** The “sound of many songs” could drown out the noise of the poor. God says, stop making noise. You are not honoring Me with your music; you are covering your crimes with it.

Then comes the famous verse 24:

> “But let justice run down as waters, and righteousness as a mighty stream.”

God is not saying, “Keep your worship but add some justice.” He is saying, “Let justice itself become your worship.” The word for “justice” is *mishpat*—the act of fair judgment, especially in the courts and gates where widows, orphans, and the poor were being denied their rights. The word for “righteousness” is *tsedaqah*—covenant faithfulness, right relationships, living as God’s people in the world. And the image is not a gentle trickle. It is a flood, a torrent, a mighty stream that sweeps through the marketplace and the courts and changes everything.

God hates false religion because false religion mocks His name. The sharp rejection is not a rejection of worship as such. It is a rejection of worship without justice. The passage exposes the fraud at the center of religious hypocrisy: pretending to love the God of the oppressed while helping oppress them.

So the answer is not “worship plus justice.” The answer is that true worship *is* a life of justice. “Take away the songs” is a judgment. “Let justice roll down” is the remedy. When justice and righteousness become the music of a community, that is a festival God will smell—and accept.
