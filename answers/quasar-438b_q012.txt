**Is the passage mainly pessimistic about human nature, or realistic about self-deception?**

It is **realistic about self-deception**. 

While the language is certainly pessimistic—"desperately wicked"—the passage’s core function is epistemological. Jeremiah is not saying that humans are irredeemably evil and should be abandoned. Rather, he is saying that the inward heart is a *bad witness*. It deceives not only others but also the person who carries it. Because the heart is "deceitful above all things," a person’s own report of their inner state is a false record. The pessimism is only the rhetorical shell; the inside is a realistic claim that self-knowledge is fundamentally compromised. 

Because the inward heart is not to be trusted, Jeremiah God does not despair. Instead, he moves judgment *outward*: he "tries the reins" and looks to the **ways** and the **fruit of the doings**. The passage is a teaching on method: when the inside lies, judge the outside.

**How should someone talk about motives if the heart itself is an unreliable witness?**

If the heart is an unreliable witness, then one cannot talk about motives by citing the heart. The statement "I did it because I felt..." is already a compromised report. Instead, motives should be spoken of in the way Psalm 17 itself prescribes:

*   **By the fruit of the doings.** State what was done and what came of it. A motive is known by what grew from it. To talk about a motive, point to the outcome. "Ask the tree by its fruit." If one's doings yielded generosity, the motive was generous; if they yielded wreckage, the motive was wrecking, no matter what sweet light shone in the dark of the soul.
*   **By the ways.** Psalm 17 says every man is judged "according to his ways." Talk about a motive as the pattern of a person’s actions, the steer they took, the habits they kept. A pattern of outward behavior is a more honest record of a motive than a glance inward. 
*   **With humility.** If you must speak about your own motive, do it with the awareness that your witness is compromised. Say: "I can name the action, and I can name what came of it. My heart may have lied to me about the why." State the outward act; let the motive be inferred. Ask not "what shines in my heart?" but "what was the fruit of my tree?"

In short, one should talk about motives *practically and outwardly*. A motive spoken from the inward heart is a guess; a motive read from the fruit of the doings is knowledge.
