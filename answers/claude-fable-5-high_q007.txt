Luke 6:37 is often quoted as if it dissolved morality itself — as if Jesus were saying that no one may ever call anything wrong, and that every moral evaluation is an act of arrogance. But that reading collapses the moment you set the verse back into its context. The same Jesus who says "judge not" also says, in the verses immediately following, that a tree is known by its fruit, that the blind cannot lead the blind, and that there are false prophets to be recognized and avoided. Elsewhere he tells his hearers to "judge righteous judgment" (John 7:24). The Sermon on the Plain, of which Luke 6:37 is a part, is itself saturated with moral evaluation: love your enemies, do good, lend expecting nothing, be merciful. None of that is intelligible unless moral distinctions are real and matter enormously. So whatever "judge not" means, it cannot mean "moral judgments never matter."

The key is to notice that the verse names three acts in parallel: judging, condemning, and forgiving. The structure tells us what kind of judging is in view. It is judging as the opposite of forgiving — judging as sentencing, as the passing of final verdicts on persons. The Greek verbs support this: krinō and katadikazō carry a forensic flavor, the language of the courtroom. Jesus is not forbidding the perception that an act is wrong; he is forbidding us to seat ourselves on the bench that belongs to God, to move from "this deed is evil" to "this person is disposed of, written off, beyond mercy." The prohibition targets the verdict on the soul, not the assessment of the act.

This is where the distinction between discernment and the appetite to condemn does its work.

Discernment is a duty. It is the capacity to tell truth from falsehood, cruelty from kindness, wisdom from folly — and Scripture everywhere commands it. Without discernment there is no way to protect the vulnerable, no way to repent (since repentance requires recognizing that something was wrong), no way even to obey the command to forgive (since forgiveness presupposes a real wrong to forgive — you cannot forgive what you refuse to name as a fault). A person who "never judges" in this sense is not humble; he is merely useless to his neighbor and dishonest about the world. Notably, the command to forgive in this very verse quietly assumes discernment: it treats wrongs as real, identifiable things.

The appetite to condemn is something else entirely, and it can be distinguished from discernment along several lines:

1. Its object. Discernment evaluates acts, patterns, teachings, fruits. Condemnation targets persons — it wants to fix a final identity on someone: hypocrite, reprobate, irredeemable. Discernment says "that was a lie"; condemnation says "he is a liar and nothing more, forever."

2. Its motive and pleasure. Discernment is ordered toward some good: protection, correction, restoration, one's own repentance. The appetite to condemn is ordered toward the self. It enjoys the verdict. Condemning another is one of the cheapest ways to feel righteous: I purchase my own elevation with someone else's disgrace. This is why Jesus pairs the saying with the image of the speck and the beam (Luke 6:41–42). The condemning eye is drawn outward precisely so that it need never turn inward; the harshness toward the other is doing psychological work, exempting me from examination. The beam is not incidental to the judging — it is often its cause.

3. Its posture toward the future. Discernment leaves the story open: it names the wrong and hopes for, works for, the person's turning. Condemnation forecloses the future; it wants the sentence to be final. But finality over a human life is exactly the prerogative Jesus reserves to God. Only God sees the whole heart, the whole history, the whole set of pressures and wounds; only God, therefore, can render the last verdict. To condemn is not merely to be unkind; it is to usurp an office.

4. Its epistemic honesty. Discernment knows what it can see (fruits, acts, words) and what it cannot (motives, struggles, the state of a soul before God). Condemnation pretends to knowledge it does not have — it reads hearts, assigns motives, and totals a person from a fragment.

The second half of the verse — "and ye shall not be judged... and ye shall not be condemned... and ye shall be forgiven" — supplies the reason, and it is a reason of reciprocity. Luke continues: "with what measure ye mete, it shall be measured to you again" (6:38). The measure you use will be used on you. This is not a bribe ("be nice so God will be nice"); it is a statement about what mercy is. Mercy is not a commodity one can hoard while denying it to others; it is a medium one lives in. The unmerciful heart clenches into a shape that cannot receive mercy — this is the parable of the unforgiving servant (Matthew 18) in a single line. A person consumed by the appetite to condemn has adopted condemnation as his native language, and he thereby places himself under its grammar. Conversely, the one who forgives has entered the economy in which forgiveness is the currency, and so can receive it.

Notice, too, that this reciprocity argument only works if moral judgments do matter. If nothing were really wrong, "forgive, and ye shall be forgiven" would be empty — there would be nothing to forgive and nothing to be forgiven of. The verse presupposes a moral universe dense with real wrongs; its whole point is about how to conduct oneself within such a universe. The command is not "pretend evil is not evil" but "renounce the pleasure of the verdict; hand the sentencing back to God; and in the space that opens up, forgive."

A practical way to state the difference: discernment moves toward the person with the truth in hand ("go and tell him his fault," "restore such a one in a spirit of meekness, considering thyself" — Galatians 6:1, which pairs correction with self-examination exactly as Luke 6 does). Condemnation moves away from or above the person with the truth as a weapon. The same true observation — "he lied" — can be the beginning of either. What distinguishes them is not the accuracy of the perception but the direction of the will: toward restoration or toward execution; toward the mirror or away from it.

So Luke 6:37 does not abolish moral judgment; it disciplines the judge. It says: you must see clearly, but you may not sentence; you must name the wrong, but you may not savor the naming; you must remember, before every evaluation of another, that you stand under the same measure, with a beam in your own eye and a need for the same mercy. The verse is not a retreat from morality but a deepening of it — for the refusal to condemn, offered by someone who sees the evil plainly and forgives anyway, is a far more serious moral act than either the shrug that judges nothing or the sneer that condemns everything.
