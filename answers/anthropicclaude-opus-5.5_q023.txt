## Why both "way" and "thoughts"?

**1. The two words cover the whole person.**
In Hebrew, *derek* ("way") is a person's path: habitual conduct, the direction of a life. *Machshevot* ("thoughts") means more than passing ideas. It usually means plans, schemes, devisings, what the heart works out before the feet carry it out. Compare Micah 2:1 ("woe to them that devise iniquity… upon their beds"), Proverbs 6:18 ("a heart that deviseth wicked imaginations"), and Genesis 6:5 ("every imagination of the thoughts of his heart"). Isaiah himself pairs the two in 59:7: "their thoughts are thoughts of iniquity; wasting and destruction are in their paths." Thoughts are the source; the way is where they lead. Repentance that addresses only one leaves the other intact.

**2. The pairing sets up verses 8–9.**
The next lines give the key: "For my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways… as the heavens are higher than the earth." God's *ways* and *thoughts* answer human ways and thoughts. The contrast is not only that God is holier. In context it is specifically that God's thoughts are *more merciful* than ours. The "for" links the lofty statement directly to "he will abundantly pardon." Human beings can hardly imagine forgiveness on that scale. So among the thoughts to be forsaken are our own assumptions about God: that he is like us, grudging, keeping score, unwilling to take someone back.

## What this implies

**About outward conduct:**
Changed behavior is necessary. The wicked must actually *leave* a path. But it is not enough on its own. Reform that leaves the inner scheming untouched is fragile or hypocritical, a way that will be walked again because the thoughts still map it.

**About inward imagination:**
The inner life is morally significant and, importantly, *changeable*. The command to "forsake" thoughts assumes a person is not simply trapped by what they imagine and desire. Thoughts can be abandoned the way a road can. At the same time, inward regret that never reaches the feet is incomplete. The text holds both together.

**About the possibility of mercy:**
Several details stress how open mercy is:
- The invitation goes to the *wicked* (*rasha*) and the "man of iniquity," not to the nearly righteous.
- The movement asked for is *return* (*shuv*): coming back to a person, "unto the Lord… to our God," not merely correcting a record.
- The response is *compassion* (*racham*, a word related to "womb") and pardon that is *multiplied* (*yarbeh lislo'ach*).
- The larger chapter frames all this as a free invitation: "Ho, every one that thirsteth… buy… without money and without price" (55:1).

Mercy is not earned by first achieving inner purity. The person forsakes and returns, and pardon meets them. The depth of the required change (even the thoughts!) could sound impossible. But it is matched by the height of God's thoughts, which are set on pardon.

**A note of urgency:**
"While he may be found… while he is near" keeps this from becoming presumption. The door is wide, but the call is to walk through it now. Fittingly, Jewish tradition reads this passage (55:6–56:8) as the prophetic reading on public fast days, occasions devoted precisely to turning.

## In short
Repentance involves both "way" and "thoughts" because sin lives in both what we do and what we devise. Genuine return reaches from the imagination to the footsteps. And the passage insists that this total turning is met by a mercy that surpasses human imagining, including our imagining of how far mercy can go.
