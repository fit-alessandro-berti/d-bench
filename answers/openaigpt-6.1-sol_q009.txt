In Romans 12:20–21, **victory over evil means refusing to let evil determine what you become—and answering it with concrete good.** Paul shifts the goal from defeating an enemy to defeating the pattern of hostility itself.

### What counts as victory?

“Be not overcome of evil” suggests that evil can conquer you even when you appear to win. If someone’s cruelty turns you cruel, or their hatred makes hatred your governing principle, you have begun to reproduce what you oppose.

“Overcome evil with good” is therefore more than passive restraint. Paul commands action: **feed the hungry enemy; give the thirsty enemy something to drink.** Their hostility does not erase their humanity or cancel your responsibility to respond to their need.

### What about the “coals of fire”?

Paul is quoting Proverbs 25:21–22. The image is difficult, and interpreters differ. A common reading is that unexpected kindness produces the painful shame that can lead an enemy to repentance. Others understand it in relation to divine judgment.

Either way, the surrounding passage matters: Paul has just forbidden personal vengeance and entrusted judgment to God (Romans 12:19). Reading the coals as a clever way to hurt someone through kindness would sit uneasily with that command—and with “Let love be without dissimulation,” or hypocrisy, earlier in the chapter (12:9).

**The good is to be genuine, not retaliation disguised as generosity.** It may expose wrongdoing, awaken conscience, or change the relationship, but it is not a technique for controlling another person’s response.

### Weak or genuinely strong?

It is strong because it requires the freedom not to be governed by provocation. Revenge can feel powerful while leaving your conduct controlled by your enemy. This command asks for disciplined courage: to oppose evil without adopting its methods.

That does **not** mean enabling abuse, abandoning justice, or restoring trust where there is no repentance. Paul qualifies the call to peace: “If it be possible, as much as lieth in you” (12:18). Boundaries and protection can coexist with refusing hatred and revenge.

So the passage defines victory neither as submission to evil nor as domination of the enemy. **It is remaining faithful to the good while actively seeking to break evil’s cycle—even when the enemy does not change.**
