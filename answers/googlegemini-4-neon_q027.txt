Luke does not leave us to guess at the root problem; he states it upfront in verse 9 before the parable even begins, and summarizes it in the concluding maxim of verse 14 (*“everyone who exalts himself will be humbled...”*). 

Looking closely at the text, the Pharisee is condemned not by any single isolated flaw from your list, but by **self-reliant pride**—which in turn corrupts his theology, fuels comparison and contempt, and twists genuine virtue into a spiritual resume. 

### 1. What exactly condemns the Pharisee?

Evaluating the five possibilities against the text reveals a sharp diagnosis:

*   **Pride (The Root):** Luke explicitly identifies the target audience as those who *“trusted in themselves that they were righteous”* (v. 9). Notice that the Pharisee’s prayer contains no confession, no petition, and no sense of need. He begins with “God, I thank thee,” which sounds pious, yet the text slyly notes that he *“prayed thus with himself”* (the Greek *pros heauton* can mean “to himself” or “standing by himself”). Either way, God is merely a polite backdrop for an inward monologue of self-congratulation. Five times in two sentences, the subject is “I.”
*   **Comparison and Contempt (The Fruit):** Because the Pharisee’s righteousness is self-manufactured, it requires a measuring stick. Rather than measuring himself against the holiness of God, he measures himself horizontally against other human beings—specifically using the tax collector as a moral foil. Luke explicitly pairs self-trust with *“despising others”* (literally, “treating others as nothing”). Contempt is the inevitable byproduct of a righteousness built on outperforming one’s neighbors.
*   **False Doctrine (Functional, not merely verbal):** Strictly speaking, the Pharisee isn’t a heretic in his words. He acknowledges God as the source of his moral advantage (“God, I thank thee...”), and his fasting and tithing actually exceed what the Mosaic Law required. His error is *functional* false doctrine: he treats righteousness as a ledger of moral achievements and religious performances that secures his standing before God. He believes he is justified by his record rather than by God’s mercy.
*   **Virtue as Self-Display (Inward rather than public):** Unlike the hypocrites in Matthew 6 who pray loudly on street corners to impress crowds, this Pharisee does not appear to be performing for human applause. He is performing *for himself and for God*. His condemnation isn’t that his virtues are fake—there is no hint in the text that he is lying about fasting, tithing, or avoiding adultery—but that he uses real virtues as credentials to establish his own superiority.

In short, the Pharisee is condemned for **self-referential righteousness**: turning devotion into a credential, God into an audience for his achievements, and his neighbor into a prop for his ego.

---

### 2. Why is the tax collector’s posture treated as spiritually truer?

The tax collector is not praised because being a tax collector or a sinner is somehow better than fasting and tithing. Rather, his posture is spiritually truer because it aligns with **reality**—specifically, the reality of who God is and who human beings are before Him.

*   **He uses the right standard (Vertical vs. Horizontal):** The Pharisee looks sideways at other sinners and concludes he is doing well. The tax collector looks upward—or rather, cannot even bear to look upward—and confronts the holiness of God. In the Greek, he does not merely call himself “a sinner,” he uses the definite article: *“be merciful to me, **the** sinner”* (*tō hamartōlō*). He is not comparing himself to anyone else in the temple; standing before God, he sees only his own moral bankruptcy.
*   **He understands the nature of justification:** The Pharisee brings a resume; the tax collector brings only his need. The Greek verb translated “be merciful” (*hilasthēti*) carries the liturgical weight of **atonement**—literally, *“be propitiated toward me”* or *“make atonement for me,”* echoing the temple sacrifices offered at the mercy seat. He recognizes that right standing with God cannot be constructed out of personal merit; it can only be received as an unearned gift of grace.
*   **His outward posture matches his inward truth:** Standing at a distance, keeping his eyes downcast, and beating his breast (a traditional gesture of deep grief and contrition) are not performative theatrics. They are the honest expression of someone who has dropped all pretense. While the Pharisee leaves the temple untouched because he believed he already possessed everything he needed, the tax collector leaves *justified*—declared right with God—precisely because he threw himself entirely on God’s mercy.

Ultimately, the parable argues that the greatest barrier to God is not moral failure, which can be repented of, but moral self-satisfaction, which leaves a person feeling no need for God at all.
