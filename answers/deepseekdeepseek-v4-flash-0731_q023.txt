The pairing of “way” and “thoughts” in Isaiah 55:6–7 points to the comprehensive nature of true repentance. God is not calling for a partial adjustment, but for a whole-person turning—from the outside in and from the inside out.

### 1. “Way” refers to outward conduct

In Hebrew, *derekh* means “way,” “path,” or “road.” It is the direction a person travels, and in biblical language it often represents the whole pattern of one’s behavior. To “forsake his way” means to turn from sinful habits, actions, and the lifestyle of the wicked. This is the visible part of repentance: changed behavior, honesty replacing deceit, purity replacing immorality, kindness replacing cruelty. The “way of the wicked” is the path of ungodliness, and repentance demands getting off that path.

### 2. “Thoughts” refers to inward imagination and intention

The second word, *machshevot*, is broader than isolated ideas. It means “thoughts,” “plans,” “inventions,” “designs,” or “purposes.” In Scripture, the heart is the fountain of action, and human thoughts are often described as deeply corrupted by sin (Genesis 6:5). To forsake “thoughts” means giving up not just evil deeds, but the inner world that drives them: pride, covetousness, rage, lust, self-justification, envy, resentment. It implies repentance at the level of the imagination, motives, and desires.

So why does the text name both? Because people can change their actions while their minds remain dark—like hypocrites who clean the outside of the cup while inside remains filthy (Matthew 23:25). Conversely, someone can entertain good intentions while continuing in harmful behavior—like the person who says “I repented in my heart” but refuses to make restitution or break with sin. Isaiah insists: repentance is not real unless it touches both branches of the tree. The roots must be transformed (thoughts), and the fruit must change (ways).

### 3. What this implies about repentance

Repentance is not merely regret; it is a **turn** at every level of the person. The Latin word for repentance is *paenitentia*, often understood as a change of mind, but the Hebrew *shuv* means “to return” or “to turn around.” To return to God means the entire direction of life reverses: the outward walk changes, and the inward world is reordered.

This also implies that sin is not simply a behavioral mistake; it has an inner source. When someone repents, they are not just “fixing behavior” or “trying harder.” They are renouncing the ungodly assumptions, self-centered plans, and unclean fantasies that once occupied their hearts. The Bible calls this the renewal of the mind (Romans 12:2). It is possible to stop certain sins and yet never repent, because the heart still rehearses and desires them. True repentance goes deeper—to the level of what we *love*, what we *imagine*, and what we *intend*.

### 4. What this implies about mercy

The final part of the verse shows why repentance is possible and why it is good news: “Let him return unto the Lord, and he will have mercy upon him; and to our God, for he will abundantly pardon.”

God’s mercy is not measured out stingily. He “abundantly pardons” (literally, “multiplies to forgive”). This is astonishing because the sins that require pardon include both wicked ways and evil thoughts—everything we have done and everything we have inwardly entertained. But God does not say, “Come back only if your way is clean or your thoughts are pure.” He says, *Return to me as you are, with your whole tangled life and your polluted heart, and I will be merciful.* Yet the call to forsake sin is still real: mercy is not a license to persist in sin, but the power that makes a real turning possible.

The urgency in verse 6—“while he may be found, while he is near”—adds gravity. God is always present everywhere, but He is not always graciously available to the hard-hearted. The invitation to seek the Lord is not open-ended. There is a day of grace, a season when the Word of the Lord is near and the Spirit is moving. But even here the emphasis is on the vastness of mercy: the very God who is “near” is eager to pardon. No one who turns to Him, leaving behind both actions and thoughts, will be turned away.

### In summary

Isaiah 55:6–7 teaches that true repentance involves:

- **Outward conduct** (“way”): changed behavior and lifestyle.
- **Inward imagination** (“thoughts”): changed mind, motives, plans, and desires.
- **Mercy**: because God is near and abundantly forgiving, the wicked and the unrighteous are invited to come back, no matter how deeply their sins have reached into their doing and dreaming.

The good news is that God does not wait for a perfect repentance. He invites broken sinners to forsake their path and their mental world, to return to Him, and to discover that He is more ready to pardon than we are to repent. That is the grace of the gospel: mercy is possible precisely because God has drawn near in Christ, and in Him there is full and abundant forgiveness.
