The image is sharp because its exaggeration makes hypocrisy both **ridiculous and dangerous**: someone with a great timber protruding from his own eye offers to remove a tiny speck from another’s. The would-be helper cannot see clearly, yet assumes the position of an expert.

Several features give the picture its force:

- **The contrast in scale:** a “mote” is a small speck; a “beam” is a substantial piece of wood. The discrepancy exposes how readily we magnify another person’s fault while overlooking a much greater fault in ourselves.
- **The contrast in attention:** “beholdest” versus “considerest not.” The problem is not merely having faults, but scrutinizing someone else while failing to examine oneself.
- **The intimacy of the proposed correction:** removing something from an eye requires clear sight, care, and precision. A morally blind person’s intervention may injure the person he claims to help.

The passage does **not** end by saying, “Leave your brother’s eye alone.” It says, **“first”** remove your own beam, **“and then”** you will see clearly to remove his mote. That sequence preserves moral correction while changing its conditions and spirit.

Jesus therefore condemns not the recognition that another person has a fault, but the hypocrisy of correcting others while exempting oneself from scrutiny. Self-correction is not a claim to sinless perfection; it is the necessary movement toward honesty, humility, and clearer judgment.

The aim is still to help the brother—but as someone who has submitted to correction, not as a superior who stands above it.
