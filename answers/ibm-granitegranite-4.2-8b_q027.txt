The passage (Luke 18:9–14) **condemns the Pharisee primarily for the *self-display of virtue through comparative pride*—i.e., using his religious acts (fasting, tithes) and explicit comparison ("I am not as other men are," including "this publican") to affirm his own righteousness *relative to others*, thereby implying superiority and excluding the need for divine mercy.** It is not chiefly "false doctrine" (he states true facts about his practices), nor merely "pride" in a vague sense; it is *pride expressed concretely via the use of virtue as self-display and comparison*. "Contempt" is mentioned in the narrator’s description ("trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and despised others"), but the *specific act condemned in the prayer* is the self-righteous framing ("I thank thee, that I am not as other men are..."). "Comparison" alone is insufficient; the condemnation hinges on the *purpose* of the comparison—to declare superiority and implicitly deny reliance on mercy. Thus, the most precise and textually grounded answer among the options is **"the use of virtue as self-display" (i.e., using fasting/tithes + comparison to affirm "I am not like others," implying superiority and excluding humility before God).**

*Why this is the best fit:*  
- The Pharisee explicitly says: *"God, I thank thee, that I am not as other men are, extortioners, unjust, adulterers, or even as this publican."* He lists sins he avoids and directly contrasts himself with "other men" (including the publican). He then states his religious practices (*"I fast twice in the week, I give tithes of all that I possess"*). The structure is: *Thank God because I am not like bad people → Here are my good deeds*. This directly uses his virtue (fasting, tithes) to *demonstrate* and *assert* his righteousness *relative to others*, implying he stands apart, needs no special mercy, and is already justified by his own record. The phrase "that I am not as other men are" functions rhetorically to exclude others (including the publican) and affirm his own status. This is precisely "virtue as self-display": he presents his piety not humbly as dependence on grace, but confidently as proof of his superiority/righteousness.  
- It is not primarily "false doctrine": He correctly describes his own practices (fasting twice weekly, giving tithes) and accurately notes the publican’s likely sinfulness (implied by "this publican" in the comparison). The theology of justification is not directly contradicted; the issue is *how he speaks of his righteousness in prayer*.  
- It is not merely "pride" without specification: While pride is present, the text gives the *form* of the pride—using specific religious acts and a comparative statement ("I am not as other men are") to proclaim superiority. Calling it simply "pride" is true but less precise; the condemnation in the parable hinges on the *method* (self-display via comparison of virtue). "Pride" alone could include many things; the passage highlights the *prayerful self-presentation*.  
- It is not chiefly "comparison": Comparison ("I am not as other men are") is the mechanism, but the *condemnation* lies in *why* the comparison matters—it serves to assert superiority and exclude humility/need for mercy. Comparison alone (e.g., noting differences without pride) would not be condemned; the tone and purpose (affirming "I am not like them") do the condemning work.  
- It is not "contempt": The narrator says the group "despised others," but the Pharisee’s *prayer itself* does not directly express contempt toward the publican in the moment; it simply states the contrast ("not as... this publican"). The condemnation in the parable focuses on the *self-righteous framing of his own prayer*, not the narrator’s broader attitude. The key fault in the prayer is the implicit exclusion of the need for mercy for himself (by declaring he is "not like other men").

> **In short: The Pharisee is condemned for *using his religious acts (fasting, tithes) and the comparative statement "I am not as other men are" to publicly affirm his own righteousness relative to others, thereby implying he needs no mercy and stands apart—i.e., virtue presented as self-display to assert superiority.**

**Why the tax collector’s posture and words are treated as spiritually truer in the parable:**

1. **Posture & Action:** He "stood afar off, would not lift up so much as his eyes unto heaven, but smote upon his breast." This physical humility (distance from the group/heaven, no upward gaze, self-focused gesture of grief/plea) visually and physically signals *brokenness, low self-assessment, and focused dependence on God*, contrasting sharply with the Pharisee’s confident upward gaze and self-affirming posture ("stood and prayed thus with himself"). The "smite upon the breast" (common ancient gesture for mourning/penitence) conveys genuine emotional weight and self-recognition of need.

2. **Words:** He says only: *"God be merciful to me a sinner."*  
   - **No comparison/self-ranking:** He does not say "I am not like other men" or list what he *isn't* doing wrong. He does not mention fasting, tithes, or contrast himself with anyone. He names his state plainly: "a sinner." This avoids the Pharisee’s implicit exclusion ("not as other men") and the self-justifying detail ("I thank thee that I am not...").  
   - **Direct, simple reliance on mercy:** He appeals *only* to God’s mercy ("God be merciful"), without qualifying his sin ("even as this publican" or listing other people's faults) or stating his own merits ("I fast...", "I give tithes"). The phrase "a sinner" honestly acknowledges the universal need for grace; it does not try to elevate himself above others through contrast.  
   - **No self-display:** There is no proclamation of his piety; the prayer is brief, humble, and focused solely on the need for mercy. It reflects interior recognition of sin and exterior utter reliance on God’s mercy.

3. **Spiritual truth aligns with the parable’s conclusion:** Jesus concludes, *"I tell you, this man went down to his house justified rather than the other."* The tax collector’s posture and words directly embody the biblical reality that **justification (being declared right with God) comes from receiving God’s mercy for sin, not from demonstrating one’s own righteousness through comparison or self-praise.** The Pharisee, by affirming "I am not as other men," implicitly denies the need for mercy *for himself* (if he is already "not like them," why would he need special mercy?), whereas the tax collector explicitly acknowledges sin ("a sinner") and seeks mercy ("God be merciful"). The parable therefore marks the tax collector as spiritually truer *in that moment of prayer* because his prayer accurately reflects humility before God, honest confession of need, and reliance solely on divine mercy—exactly what Scripture consistently teaches (e.g., Psalm 51; Romans 3:23; 1 John 1:8–2:2; Jesus’ own teaching in Matthew 5:3–12; 7:1–5). His posture reinforces the verbal humility; together they present a credible, internally consistent posture of dependence.

> **In concise terms for the answer:** The Pharisee is condemned for *using his religious deeds and a comparative statement ("I am not as other men are") to affirm his own righteousness relative to others—i.e., presenting virtue as self-display to imply superiority and exclude reliance on mercy. The tax collector is treated as spiritually truer because his brief, humble words ("God be merciful to me, a sinner") and physical posture (standing afar, no eyes lifted, smiting the breast) honestly acknowledge sin, make no comparison or self-praise, and depend solely on God’s mercy—matching the biblical reality that justification flows from receiving grace, not from demonstrating righteousness through comparison.
