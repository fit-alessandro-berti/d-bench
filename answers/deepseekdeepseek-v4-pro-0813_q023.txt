Isaiah 55:6–7 describes repentance as a whole-person turning from sin to God. The pairing of “way” and “thoughts” is not accidental; it is a Hebrew parallelism that covers the outer and inner life of the same person.

### 1. “His way” = outward conduct

In Hebrew, “way” (*derek*) often means a road, a course of life, a pattern of behavior. The “wicked” person has a way—a lifestyle, a direction, a set of habits, choices, and visible actions that are oriented away from God.

So when the text says, “Let the wicked forsake his way,” it is calling for an outward break with sinful behavior. Repentance is not merely a private feeling; it involves turning from the actual practices and path of rebellion.

### 2. “His thoughts” = inward imagination

The Hebrew word translated “thoughts” (*maḥšᵉbôt*) can mean thoughts, plans, purposes, devices, or imaginations. In older English, “imagination” often meant not just make-believe but the inward life of the mind—what a person pictures, desires, plans, meditates on, and loves.

So the “unrighteous man” is called to forsake his thoughts because sin is rooted in the inner person. As Jesus later said, “Out of the heart proceed evil thoughts,” and those thoughts produce sinful actions (Matthew 15:19). A person might outwardly behave well while still entertaining lust, hatred, greed, or pride in the imagination. Repentance must go that deep.

### 3. Why both are required

The two phrases together mean: **repentance must be total.**

- To forsake the “way” but not the “thoughts” is only external reformation. The heart would still be in love with sin, and eventually the outward life will follow the inner desires.
- To forsake “thoughts” but not the “way” is not real repentance either. If the direction of life does not change, inner regret has not produced the fruit of obedience.

Isaiah is describing the sinner as a whole person: outward life and inward mind. True repentance turns from both. The New Testament says the same in different language: “Be not conformed to this world: but be ye transformed by the renewing of your mind” (Romans 12:2), and Paul speaks of bringing “every thought into captivity to the obedience of Christ” (2 Corinthians 10:5).

### 4. What this implies about the possibility of mercy

The command to forsake both “way” and “thoughts” is immediately joined to a promise:

> “Let him return unto the Lord, and he will have mercy upon him; and to our God, for he will abundantly pardon.”

This means:

- **Mercy is not earned by forsaking sin.** The forsaking is the shape of repentance, but the ground of mercy is God’s character and promise. We do not clean ourselves enough to deserve mercy; we return to the Lord and receive it.
- **No sin is beyond pardon.** The verse addresses the “wicked” and the “unrighteous”—those whose outward ways and inward thoughts have been corrupt. Yet God says he will “abundantly pardon.” The Hebrew idea behind “abundantly pardon” is multiplied forgiveness—pardon that is more than enough.
- **Mercy reaches both the outside and the inside.** God does not merely forgive visible sins of behavior; he also forgives inward sins of the imagination, thought, and desire. The cleansing is as comprehensive as the repentance called for.

So Isaiah 55:6–7 implies that repentance is not partial. It turns from the outward path of sin and from the inward imagination of sin, because both belong to the same life turned away from God. And the great hope is this: when a sinner does return, mercy is not reluctant or small. God will abundantly pardon.
