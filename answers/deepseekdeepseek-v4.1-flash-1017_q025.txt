The decisive problem in Matthew 6:1 is **the craving to be seen**, not visibility as such. The phrase “to be seen of them” gives the purpose or aim: Jesus warns against doing good **in order to be spectated and admired**. Publicness is the occasion; the disordered desire for human recognition is the moral danger.

Several distinctions help:

1. **Visibility is not inherently sinful.**  
   In Matthew 5:16 Jesus says, “Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father which is in heaven.” So good works *may* be seen. The difference is direction: in 5:16 visibility serves the Father’s glory; in 6:1 visibility serves the doer’s image. Public goodness becomes corrupt when the audience replaces God as the one before whom one lives.

2. **The issue is a counterfeit reward.**  
   In the following verses Jesus says such people “have their reward” (6:2, 5, 16). Human applause becomes payment. If being admired is the reward one is actually seeking, then the Father’s reward is not being sought. The act may still benefit someone, but religiously it has been redirected toward self-display.

3. **Public goodness is morally dangerous because it is spiritually formative.**  
   Praise is pleasurable, and repeated public goodness can train the heart to perform for people rather than live before God. It can turn a neighbor into an audience, charity into theater, and virtue into a brand. That is why Jesus prescribes hiddenness—not because all goodness must be secret, but because secrecy breaks the spell of applause and teaches the heart to be content with the Father’s eye.

So the answer is: **visibility itself is not the problem; the craving to be seen is.** Public good is not wrong when it serves love, truth, justice, witness, accountability, or the glory of God. It becomes morally dangerous when being seen becomes the point—when the heart is more moved by the audience than by the Father. The key question is not simply “Did anyone see?” but “Whose eyes was I seeking?”
