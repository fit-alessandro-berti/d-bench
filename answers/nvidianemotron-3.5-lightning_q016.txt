**Exegesis of the Passage (Luke 15:28–32)**  
The elder brother erupts in anger (v.28), then gives a plaintive, transactional speech (v.29): *“Lo, these many years do I serve thee, neither transgressed I at any time thy commandment: and yet thou never gavest me a kid…”* He casts his service as merit earned, and the father’s favor toward the prodigal as unjust. He resents the fatted calf (v.30), refuses the father’s invitation, and implicitly rejects his “lost” brother as kin (v.30). The father’s reply (vv.31–32) gently exposes the heart issue: all he has is already his, and the celebration is not a reward for performance but a gracious rescue of the lost.

**Evaluating the Options**

| Option | Present in Text? | Role |
|--------|------------------|------|
| **Anger** | Yes (v.28) | The immediate outburst, but the father’s plea shows it’s a symptom, not the root. |
| **Envy** | Yes (v.30: “thou hast killed for him…”) | He resents the attention/celebration given to the younger brother. |
| **Wounded fairness** | Yes (v.29: “I never transgressed… yet thou never gavest me a kid”) | He feels his obedience has been “under-rewarded.” |
| **Pride in obedience** | Yes (v.29: boasts of flawless service) | A self-righteous view of his relationship with the father as a contract of merit. |
| **Discomfort with mercy itself** | Yes (v.29–30: resents the father’s unmerited grace) | The core relational refusal to rejoice in grace shown to another. |

**The Deepest Problem**  
Anger, envy, and a sense of wounded fairness all flow from the same root: **pride in his own obedience** (self-righteousness). He has reduced his relationship with the father to a performance-for-reward system. This moralism blinds him to the father’s character and generates his true issue: a **discomfort with mercy itself**. He cannot celebrate the restoration of a repentant sinner because his identity and security are tied to his own “faithfulness,” not to the father’s grace.

If one label must be singled out as the *deepest* heart condition, it is **pride in obedience**—the self-righteous conviction that his service has earned him favor, which inherently produces discomfort with the father’s unearned mercy. The other reactions are the faithful text’s witnesses to that root.

**Answer:**  
The elder brother’s deepest problem is **pride in obedience** (self-righteousness), which generates his anger, envy, and sense of wounded fairness, and reveals his true struggle: **discomfort with mercy itself**.
