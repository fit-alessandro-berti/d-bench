The pairing makes sense once you see the argument of the passage. Ephesians 4:22–24 uses a put off / put on pattern: the old self is taken off like a garment and the new self is put on. Verses 31–32 apply that pattern to speech and relationships. Bitterness is put off, and kindness and forgiveness are put on. The opposition is between two ways of holding another person: keeping a ledger of hurt, or releasing the debt as God released yours.

The text also supplies its own motive. The forgiveness is modeled on God's act "for Christ's sake." The past tense ("hath forgiven") matters. Christians are not told to forgive in order to earn forgiveness. They are told to act from a forgiveness already received, which reshapes their posture toward others.

On your question, the text seems to treat bitterness as something closer to a posture that forms, rather than a pure feeling or a fixed personality trait. Several things point that way:

- **It is imperative.** "Let... be put away" addresses the will. You cannot be commanded to have no feelings of hurt, but you can be told not to nurse, rehearse, and harden them.
- **It is not identical with anger.** Verse 26 says "Be ye angry, and sin not." Anger can be a natural response to real wrong, even a righteous one. What is sinful is letting anger go unresolved, ending in the settled hostility the list describes.
- **It has a progression.** The list moves from inner disposition to outward action. *Bitterness* (*pikria*, sharp, sour) is the root. *Wrath* (*thymos*) is heat that bursts out. *Anger* (*orgē*) is more settled resentment. *Clamour* and *evil speaking* are what it sounds like. *Malice* (*kakia*) is the underlying ill will. Bitterness comes first in the list, which suggests it is the root from which the rest grows.
- **It spreads.** Hebrews 12:15, which echoes Deuteronomy 29:18, warns against "any root of bitterness springing up" and says that "many be defiled" by it. This is the contagion dimension. A bitter person's words and tone shape the community around them. The passage also comes right after "grieve not the Holy Spirit" (4:30), which suggests that the damage is relational and spiritual, not just private.

So bitterness is best understood as a disposition that begins with a felt wound, is chosen and sustained through repeated reliving and refusal to release, and then becomes a habit that colors speech and spreads through the body of believers. The antidote is not just suppressing the feeling. It is a counter-posture of kindness and tenderheartedness, and the active, ongoing choice to forgive, grounded in God's prior forgiveness. The Greek verb for "forgiving" (*charizomenoi*) comes from the same root as "grace," which fits that picture: forgiveness here is gracious release, not grudging tolerance.
