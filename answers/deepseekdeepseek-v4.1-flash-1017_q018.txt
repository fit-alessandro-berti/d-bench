Short answer: Ephesians 4:31–32 puts bitterness and forgiveness side by side because they are two rival ways of handling an offense. Bitterness keeps and nurses the debt; forgiveness releases it in light of God’s grace in Christ. But the text is not treating bitterness as a harmless emotion. It treats it as a heart posture that becomes a habit, produces ugly relational fruit, and can spread through a community.

In verse 31 the list moves from inward to outward: “bitterness, wrath, anger, clamour, evil speaking, with all malice.” Bitterness (*pikria*) heads the list like a hidden root; then come heated rage, loud conflict, slander, and settled ill-will. So bitterness is not just “I feel hurt.” It is resentment that has begun to take root and govern how I see and speak about others. Hebrews 12:15 uses the same image: a “root of bitterness” that springs up and defiles many. That means it can indeed be contagious.

In verse 32 the counter-pattern is “kind… tenderhearted… forgiving one another.” The Greek word for “forgiving” (*charizomenoi*) is related to grace/graciousness. It is present tense: keep practicing gracious forgiveness. So forgiveness here is not merely a warm feeling; it is a grace-shaped action and habit. The motive is vertical before it is horizontal: “even as God for Christ’s sake hath forgiven you.” Christian forgiveness flows from having received forgiveness at cost through Christ.

So, is bitterness a feeling, a habit, a chosen posture, or something contagious? The text suggests: **all of the above, but not merely a feeling.** It includes real emotional pain, but the command forbids pain that curdles into resentment. It is a chosen posture of keeping accounts and withholding grace. Repeated, it becomes a habit of the old self. And through angry speech, slander, and malice, it becomes socially contagious.

That is why forgiveness is the direct counterpart. Forgiveness breaks the cycle at the root. It does not deny that wrong happened or pretend it did not hurt. It refuses, instead, to let the wrong have final control; it entrusts justice and revenge to God and extends grace because God in Christ has first extended grace to us. Forgiveness may be a decisive act renewed many times as feelings catch up, and it is not the same as immediate trust or reconciliation where repentance and safety are absent. But in this text, the church is called to become a community where grace stops bitterness from spreading.
