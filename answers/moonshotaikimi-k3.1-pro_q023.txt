Isaiah treats sin not merely as a collection of visible offenses, but as a **direction of life sustained by a way of thinking**. Repentance must therefore reach both.

- **“Way”** suggests a road or habitual course: one’s conduct, choices, practices, and overall direction. The wicked person is not simply to regret individual acts, but to leave the path that produces them.
- **“Thoughts”** refers especially to purposes, plans, and reasonings—not every passing or involuntary thought. It points to the inner reasoning by which a person decides what is desirable, excuses wrongdoing, and imagines life apart from God. Sin has an inward script as well as an outward expression.

The pairing guards against two reductions of repentance. **Outward reform without inward turning** can become respectability or hypocrisy: the behavior changes while the same self-directed reasoning remains. **Inward sorrow without forsaking the way** can become sentimentality: one feels bad but continues walking in the same direction. Isaiah instead joins *forsaking* and *returning*. Repentance is not autonomous self-improvement; it is a turn of the whole person back to the Lord.

The connection with verses 8–9 is significant: “my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways.” The same pairing continues. Human beings must relinquish their habitual ways and self-justifying thoughts, while God’s thoughts and ways prove astonishingly different. In the immediate context, that difference is displayed especially in **mercy**. We may imagine that pardon must be scarce, reluctantly given, earned by prior improvement, or exhausted by repeated failure. God instead promises compassion and declares that he will “abundantly”—or “freely,” without stint—pardon.

That promise does not make repentance unnecessary, as though God simply endorsed the wicked person’s way. Nor does it make mercy a wage paid only after someone has achieved inward and outward perfection. The call is not, “Renovate yourself completely, and then perhaps approach God,” but, **“Forsake, return, and trust the mercy of the One to whom you return.”** Genuine turning matters, but completed self-renovation is not the price of admission.

Thus the passage makes repentance both searching and hopeful. Conduct really must change; the imagination that nurtures and justifies sin must also be surrendered. Yet neither an entrenched pattern of life nor a disordered inner world places the returning sinner beyond God’s promise. His pardon is greater than our customary calculations—and greater than our ability to imagine it.
