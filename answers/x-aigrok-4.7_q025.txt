The danger is the craving to be seen, not visibility itself.

The verse’s hinge is the purpose clause: do not give alms “before men, **to be seen of them**.” Public goodness becomes morally empty when its aim is human notice. The act may still help someone, but it is no longer done as an offering to God; it is performed for an audience, and that audience’s approval is treated as the reward. Jesus says that reward is then already received—and the Father’s reward is forfeited.

Visibility is not the problem. The same Gospel tells disciples to let their light shine so that people see good works and glorify the Father (Matthew 5:16). What changes the moral character of the act is whether being seen is the point. Secret giving is praised in the next verses not because secrecy is magically holy, but because it starves the desire to be admired. Public good done without that hunger remains intact.
