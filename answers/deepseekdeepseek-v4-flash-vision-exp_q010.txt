The power praised in Philippians 2 is a **power that can release its own rights**. It is not the power of domination, but the power of self-emptying love. In Christ, we see someone who is “in the form of God” and “equal with God”—and yet he does not treat that glory as something to cling to or assert. Instead, he “made himself of no reputation,” took the form of a servant, became human, and humbled himself in obedience to the point of death on a cross.

This is what theology calls **kenotic power**—power that pours itself out, power that serves, power that loves even at its own cost. The hymn is not praising weakness for its own sake. It is praising the astonishing freedom of greatness to become lowly. It says that God’s power is not like the world’s power. The world’s power takes, dominates, controls. Christ’s power gives, kneels, offers, dies. And because he is confident in who he is—“in the form of God”—he is not threatened by becoming a servant.

So the power here is not “power to control” but “power to redeem.” It is the power of a love that refuses to save itself, so that it can save others. It is power perfected in weakness, as Paul says elsewhere.

---

But why does humility joined to greatness feel more unsettling than open domination?

Because **open domination is recognizable.** A tyrant is dangerous, but he is predictable: he tells you what he wants, and the relationship between fear and power is clear. You can resist him, obey him, flatter him, or flee from him. But a king who washes feet, a Lord who was crucified, a God who enters our suffering and does not fight back—this is not a power you can easily locate. It does not force you to the ground; it invites you to your knees. It does not overwhelm your will; it exposes your heart.

That is more unsettling.

It subverts every instinct we have about greatness. We think greatness means being above, invulnerable, independent, imposing. But here, greatness appears as vulnerability, obedience, service, and self-giving. It does not fit our categories. It calls into question our own hunger for status and power. When we see God emptying himself, our own pride is not only contradicted—it is revealed as something ugly.

There is also the fact that humble greatness leaves no room for excuse. With a dominating power, we can say, “I was coerced. I had no choice. I was afraid.” But with a servant king, we cannot say that. He does not force us; he serves us. He does not enslave us; he frees us. And if we reject that love, no one is to blame but ourselves. That is a deeper judgment than any punishment.

Unsettling, too, is the vulnerability of this power. It allows itself to be rejected, mocked, and crucified. It means God is not safe or distant; he is exposed to our violence and yet keeps loving us. This is a power that can be hurt. In a world of domination, we know how to deal with force: we try to use our own force against it. But what do we do with a love we can kill and cannot conquer? We can only either surrender to it or turn away in defiance.

The hymn ends by saying that God highly exalted the one who humbled himself. The cross is not the final word; resurrection is. But within the verses quoted, the power is hidden in lowliness. It is a power that does not insist on being recognized—and that is exactly why it is so demanding. It does not attack our will, so it gives us the freedom to reject it. And when we are free to reject it, our choice becomes morally weighty.

Open domination can be merely endured. But humility joined to greatness must be either received or refused. It asks for faith, not fear; for love, not submission. And that is a far more intimate and dangerous kind of power.

In the end, the power praised in Philippians 2 is the power of the cross: **love that is strong enough to humble itself, and humble enough to save the world.** It is the kind of power that upends all human empires and quietly breaks our pride. That is why it unsettles us—not because it is weak, but because it is holy.
