## The Two Words

The Hebrew pairs *derekh* (way, road, course of life) with *maḥashebōth* (thoughts, plans, devices, schemes). These are not two random items but a deliberate couple: the track a person walks and the interior calculation that lays out the track. *Maḥashebōth* is not idle daydreaming — it is the word for designing, plotting, engineering. Bezalel uses it for craftsmanship in Exodus; the Psalms and Proverbs use it for the schemes of the wicked. So the second term reaches beneath behavior to the workshop where behavior is drafted.

Notice also the parallel subjects: "the wicked" (*rasha'*) and "the unrighteous man" — literally *ish awen*, "man of iniquity," where *awen* carries the sense of emptiness, futility, vanity. The verse doesn't address two classes of people; it addresses one person from two angles: what he does, and the hollow inner architecture that produces it.

## Why Both Are Required

Reform of conduct alone leaves the engine running. A man may abandon a particular sin while keeping the whole appetitive machinery that generated it — in which case the sin simply relocates. Conversely, an inward regret that never alters the road is what Scripture elsewhere calls sorrow of the world: a feeling that dissolves without issue. Isaiah refuses to let repentance be either merely behavioral or merely emotional.

There is also an order implied. "Way" comes first because it is what can be immediately laid down; "thoughts" comes second because that surrender is slower, deeper, and less accessible to the will. You can stop walking a road today. Ceasing to *want* the road, ceasing to plan for it, ceasing to keep it mapped in case of need — that is the longer labor. The verse names both so that neither can be used as an alibi for the other.

## The Hidden Hinge to Verses 8–9

The force of this becomes clear two verses later: *"For my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways."* Isaiah uses the identical pair. The repentance commanded in v. 7 is answered by the disclosure in v. 8: you are being asked to relinquish a set of ways and thoughts precisely because there is another set on offer, infinitely higher. This is not subtraction but exchange.

That reframes the whole demand. The problem with the wicked man's thoughts is not only that they are corrupt but that they are *small* — *awen*, empty, futile. He is being invited out of a cramped interior into one whose horizons are "as the heavens are higher than the earth." Repentance, on this reading, is less like surrendering a weapon and more like waking from a narrow dream.

## The Accountability of the Imagination

The implication for the inner life is serious. Genesis 6:5 diagnoses the pre-flood world as "every imagination of the thoughts of his heart was only evil continually" — the flood is a judgment on interiority. Isaiah stands in that tradition: the imagination is not a private zone exempt from moral weight. What you rehearse, entertain, and quietly keep available is already part of your case.

This is uncomfortable, but it is also strangely merciful. It means repentance can begin where no one can see it, in the person whose outward life is already respectable but whose inner theater is squalid — and it means such a person is not permitted to consider himself unimplicated.

## The Structure of Mercy

Three things about the pardon:

**It is preceded by grace, not earned by repentance.** The sequence in vv. 6–7 begins with *seek* and *call*, and the ground given is that God "may be found" and "is near." The nearness is prior. The whole chapter opens with an invitation to buy "without money and without price." So forsaking way and thoughts is not the price of God's approach; it is the response to an approach already made.

**It is return, not mere improvement.** The verb is *shuv* — to turn back, to come home. The object is personal: "return *unto the Lord*." Repentance is not primarily the correction of a record but the resumption of a relationship. This is why the inward dimension is indispensable: you cannot come home in your conduct while remaining estranged in your thoughts.

**It is disproportionate.** "He will abundantly pardon" renders *yarbeh lisloaḥ* — "he will multiply to forgive," he will forgive *much*, he will forgive *lavishly*. The verb of increase is set against the accumulated mass of the man's ways and thoughts. Whatever has multiplied on his side, pardon multiplies further. And *salaḥ*, this particular verb for forgiveness, is used in the Hebrew Bible with God as its only subject — it names something no human being does.

## The Urgency

"While he may be found... while he is near" does not imply a stingy God who closes a window arbitrarily. It implies that *seasons are real*: that hearts calcify, that the capacity to be moved is itself perishable, that a person can so thoroughly domesticate his own thoughts that the invitation no longer registers as an invitation. The limit is not in the abundance of the pardon but in the diminishing responsiveness of the one who defers.

So the two halves hold together: the demand is total — road and workshop, deed and design — and the mercy is more than total. What makes the demand bearable is that it is not a condition of acceptance but the shape acceptance takes in a human life.
