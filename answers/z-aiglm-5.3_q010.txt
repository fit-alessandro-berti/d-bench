This passage — often called the *Carmen Christi*, likely a hymn older than Paul's letter — inverts nearly every assumption the ancient world held about divinity and power. Its answer to "what kind of power?" is *kenotic* power: power whose supreme act is self-emptying.

## I. The power being praised

The Greek at the center is *heauton ekenōsen* — "he emptied himself." In Greco-Roman thought, divine greatness meant immunity: a god was great precisely because nothing could be done *to* him — he could not suffer, lose, or be diminished. This hymn stakes the opposite claim. The one "in the form of God" proves his fullness precisely by being able to pour it out. You can only empty what is full. The power praised is omnipotence that includes, among its capacities, the capacity to become powerless — voluntarily, since no one takes it from him.

The pivotal word is *harpagmon* — the KJV's "robbery," meaning a prize to be clutched, a thing seized. He did not treat equality with God as a possession to guard but as currency to spend. Where Adam reached for being "like God," and where every ruler since has clutched prerogative, this one held rank loosely. The hymn's grammar is careful on both sides: he does not grasp downward (refusing to exploit divine status), and he does not grasp upward (the exaltation in vv. 9–11 is *given* by God, not seized). Non-clutching in both directions.

The structure makes the scandal deliberate. "Form of a servant" sits in explicit parallel with "form of God" — the slave-shape is not a mask over divine power; it is what divine form looks like when fully expressed. And "even the death of the cross" is the intensification: crucifixion was Rome's technology for making bodies into warnings, reserved for slaves and rebels. Cicero said the very word should be far from a Roman citizen's ears. The hymn drags the divine through exactly that zero point — and the implicit claim is enormous: terror is the substance of imperial power, and terror requires the victim's fear. A consent that goes even to death drains domination of its content. The empire's worst instrument, received rather than inflicted, becomes a throne; the crucified slave is given the Name before which every knee bows (Isaiah 45's words for YHWH).

Note finally the direction of effect: "Let this mind be in you." This power does not compel; it propagates by imitation. You can force a slave. You cannot force anyone to *want to be like you*.

## II. Why it unsettles

**Domination is legible; this is not.** A tyrant draws a line, and you know your role: submit, resist, resent. All our scripts for power presuppose someone holding it. The one who has everything and lays it down gives you nothing to push against — no position, no counter-move that fits. It is the uncanny in its strict sense: power behaving in a way power is not supposed to behave.

**It creates an unpayable debt.** Violence can be answered with violence; the exchange stays symmetric. A gift that costs the giver everything has no reciprocal move — you cannot repay it, and refusing it only makes you smaller. Domination leaves you the dignity of opposition; sacrificial greatness leaves you only the option of being changed.

**It indicts without accusing.** The dominator lets us locate evil outside ourselves. But the one who held equality with God and did not grasp it reveals that our own grasping — for status, security, self-assertion — is chosen, not necessary. An indictment that never names you is the hardest kind to answer.

**It escapes our leverage.** Someone who has renounced all stakes cannot be threatened, bribed, or flattered. Their invulnerability is not armor but an emptiness already accepted — a freedom we don't possess, and can't help finding unnerving.

**It raises the suspicion of covert mastery.** We know humility can be strategy — the abasement that obligates, the meekness that wins by making strength feel like guilt. Nietzsche's entire critique lives here: Christian humility as the weak's covert conquest of the strong. And the suspicion has empirical warrant: the hymn has too often been preached *downward*, to the powerless, as a demand to accept what crushes them — which is the hymn inverted, since its logic runs from the top down, urging those with standing to relinquish it. Nor does the hymn fully dissolve the ambiguity on its own terms: it ends with universal acclamation, which structurally resembles total victory. The Christian answer is that the difference lies in *need* — a tyrant requires your submission to be complete; this Lord lacks nothing and extracts nothing, and the bowing is recognition of what was already true rather than tribute demanded. But that answer requires trusting the motive of unlimited power, the one thing experience teaches us never to do. Hence a permanent residue of unsettlement.

**It comes too close.** Distance is safety. A dominating god can be kept at the boundary and appeased at the altar. A God in the form of a servant has already crossed every line — and the hymn offers no spectator's seat, since "let this mind be in you" means this power is only understood from the inside, by those beginning to imitate it.

Open domination wants your submission. This wants your transformation — and of the two, transformation is the scarier gift.
