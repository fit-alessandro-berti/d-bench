WHAT KIND OF POWER IS BEING PRAISED

The passage praises a power that is displayed by being laid down. Every verb in the hymn's downward movement is active and voluntary: he "made himself of no reputation" (Greek ekenosen, "emptied himself"), "took upon him" the form of a servant, "humbled himself," "became obedient." Nothing is done to him; the descent is his own act. What is being honored is therefore not weakness, and not the absence of power, but power so complete that it includes power over itself — the capacity to renounce status, safety, and recognition without being diminished as a self.

Several features of the text sharpen this.

1. Power that does not need to grasp. The KJV's "thought it not robbery to be equal with God" renders the rare word harpagmos, which means something seized, or something clutched and exploited for advantage. Either way the point is the same: equality with God was not, for Christ, a possession to be defended or a prize to be cashed in. The contrast is with every other kind of greatness the first readers knew — Adam reaching for equality with God in Genesis 3, and, in Philippi, a Roman colony, emperors who had themselves proclaimed divine and enforced it with legions. Domination is the power of someone afraid of losing what he has. The power praised here has no such fear, and so it can afford to descend.

2. Power whose servanthood is not a disguise. Paul uses the same word, morphe ("form"), for "the form of God" and "the form of a servant." The servant-form is not a costume thrown over the divine form; it is what the divine form looks like when it acts in a world of servants. The passage is not saying that God temporarily pretended to be lowly. It is saying that lowliness of this kind is a genuine expression of what divine greatness is. That is a claim about the nature of power itself: the highest power is the kind that can pour itself out for others.

3. Power measured by how far down it will go. The hymn descends by stages — God, man, servant, death, "even the death of the cross." Crucifixion was the punishment reserved for slaves and rebels, deliberately designed to strip its victim of every shred of standing. Paul's readers would have heard "even the cross" as a scandal, not a flourish. The power praised is one that will go all the way to the bottom of the social order and stay there to the end.

4. Power that is obedient. "Obedient unto death" is a strange predicate for the greatest of all beings. Domination is the refusal to answer to anyone; here greatness consists in freely binding oneself to a will and a mission and holding to it when it costs everything. Authority and obedience, normally opposites, are fused.

5. Power that is imitable. The hymn is introduced by an imperative: "Let this mind be in you." It is quoted to a small community troubled by "strife and vainglory" (2:3), to persuade them to "esteem others better than themselves." This tells us something decisive about the kind of power in view. Domination cannot be shared; if everyone dominates, no one does. But self-emptying love can be practiced by anyone at any level, and it grows rather than shrinks when it spreads. The passage praises a power that is not zero-sum.

Finally, the verses that follow (2:9-11) say that "wherefore" — because of this descent — God exalted him and gave him the name above every name. The exaltation is not a reward for having endured the humiliation as a detour; it is the vindication of the humiliation as the true shape of glory. The kind of power being praised is one for which going down is the way up, and for which the throne and the cross are the same seat.


WHY HUMILITY JOINED TO GREATNESS IS MORE UNSETTLING THAN OPEN DOMINATION

It seems paradoxical that a servant-king should disturb us more than a tyrant, but the reasons are fairly deep.

1. Domination is legible; humble greatness is not. We have a working theory of power: it is for getting one's way and protecting one's position. The tyrant confirms the theory, and so, however much we fear him, we understand him. We know what he wants, what he will do, and how to respond — submit, flatter, resist, resent. A being who possesses total power and uses it to wash feet and die a slave's death violates the theory. It is a category error walking around in the world. The unease it produces is the uncanniness of something that should not be possible according to everything we assumed.

2. Nothing we have is leverage. Every form of resistance to domination works by finding what the dominator wants and withholding or threatening it. But someone who has already given up reputation, safety, comfort, and life itself cannot be bribed, threatened, flattered, or shamed. There is no handle by which to move him. Freedom that complete is frightening precisely because it is untouchable; it can never be made to serve us, and we cannot make it go away.

3. It removes our alibi. Against a tyrant we can be righteous victims; his brutality justifies our resentment and flatters our sense of being better than he is. But when the one with all the power stoops below us, the moral geometry inverts. We are no longer the wronged party looking up at an oppressor; we are the grasping party looking down at one who refused to grasp. His humility is an indictment without a word of accusation. Judgment by force can be resisted; judgment by example can only be accepted or fled.

4. It cannot be fought because it is a gift. Force can be met with force. A gift can only be received or refused, and refusing it makes us the ones who are hard. Being on the receiving end of a self-emptying love puts us in a debt we did not consent to and cannot repay. Many people would rather be conquered than be given to, because the conquered keep their pride and the recipients of grace lose it.

5. It asks for more than compliance. A dominator wants your outward behavior; he is content with your obedience and indifferent to your heart. The passage ends its praise with an imperative aimed at the inside: "Let this mind be in you." Humble greatness does not want your submission; it wants you to become like it. It asks for the surrender of the very self-assertion by which we usually survive. That is a far more invasive demand than any tyrant makes, and we feel it as such.

6. It reverses our hierarchy of value. If the highest being goes to the lowest place, then the lowest place is not what we thought, and neither are the high places we spend our lives climbing toward. Our ambitions are quietly exposed as confused. And our picture of God is overturned as well. Those who wanted a God who would underwrite their own power find him on the wrong side of the cross; those who wanted a distant tyrant to blame find instead someone who has suffered more than they have. Both lose a familiar footing.

7. It provokes suspicion, and the suspicion is itself revealing. Faced with humility from the powerful, we instinctively ask, "What does he really want?" We suspect condescension, manipulation, a more refined form of control. Nietzsche made this suspicion into a philosophy, reading Christian humility as the will to power in disguise. Dostoevsky's Grand Inquisitor argues that humanity cannot bear a Christ who refuses to compel and would rather be ruled for its own good. The persistence of these reactions shows how intolerable the phenomenon is: we would almost rather believe it is a trick than accept that greatness can genuinely choose lowliness, because if it can, we are left with no excuse for choosing otherwise. The hymn forecloses the escape by insisting on a real death — "even the death of the cross." A disguise can be removed; a corpse cannot.

8. Chosen lowliness is different from imposed lowliness. The humble weak are humble by circumstance, and we can pity or ignore them. The humble great are humble by decision, and a decision like that cannot be dismissed. It reveals a freedom over oneself that most of us have never had for a single hour. Standing in its presence, we recognize our own captivity to status and self-protection, and recognition of that kind is unsettling in a way no external threat can match.

In the end, open domination leaves us intact — frightened, perhaps, or angry, but still ourselves, still operating by the same logic as the one who dominates us. Humility joined to greatness does not leave us intact. It shows that the logic itself was wrong, that the summit of power looks like a servant on a cross, and that we are invited, not commanded, to take up the same mind. The tyrant threatens what we have. The servant-king threatens who we are. That is why the second is harder to bear.
