Galatians 6:1 describes correction done from **humility, gentleness, and self-awareness**, with the aim of **healing the person** rather than winning an argument or punishing a failure.

## The posture required

**1. Recognition that the fault is a "fall," not an identity.** The Greek *paraptōma* means a lapse or slip, a stepping aside from the path. The phrase "overtaken" (*prolēmphthē*) suggests being caught off guard by a temptation. The one who is restored is a brother who has stumbled, not an enemy to be exposed. This framing already shapes how the correction should sound.

**2. Spiritual maturity, not superiority.** "Ye which are spiritual" (*hoi pneumatikoi*) refers to those who walk by the Spirit. Paul has just listed the fruit of the Spirit in 5:22–23, and gentleness (*prautēs*, the same word translated "meekness") is among them. The one who corrects is expected to display the Spirit's character, not claim status over the fallen. Paul also warns in 5:26 against "vainglory," "provoking one another," and "envying one another," which are the very attitudes that corrupt correction.

**3. A restorative goal.** *Katartizō* means to mend or set something back into right order. It is used of mending nets (Matt. 4:21) and setting a broken bone. Restoration aims at reintegrating the person into the community and into obedience. Exposure, shame, and punishment are not the point.

**4. Meekness as strength under control.** *Prautēs* is not timidity or softness. It describes power governed by love, like a trained animal that responds to the bridle. Anger and harshness usually defeat the goal; as James says, "the wrath of man worketh not the righteousness of God" (Jas. 1:20).

**5. Self-examination.** "Considering thyself" (*skopōn seauton*) means "keep watch on yourself." The corrector's attention turns inward: "lest thou also be tempted" (*peirasthēs*). The corrector is no safer than the one who fell. Paul's similar warning in 6:3 ("if a man think himself to be something, when he is nothing, he deceiveth himself") reinforces this.

## Why these elements belong together

Each element guards against a distortion of the others.

- **Restoration without meekness becomes harshness.** Correction aims at healing, but it turns into condemnation when delivered with contempt. The fallen person then hardens instead of repenting.
- **Meekness without restoration becomes indifference.** Gentleness that never addresses the fault is sentimentality, which abandons the brother to his sin as surely as harshness does.
- **Both without self-suspicion become self-righteousness.** A corrector who forgets his own vulnerability slides into pride, treating the fault as foreign to himself. Self-suspicion is what makes mercy honest: "there but for the grace of God go I."

The relationship runs in both directions. Self-suspicion makes meekness possible, because you cannot be gentle with someone while believing yourself immune to the same failure. Meekness is the manner in which restoration can actually happen, because people repent under gentleness more readily than under condemnation. Restoration is the purpose that gives the whole posture direction.

## The wider context

Paul's letter is largely about the difference between life under the flesh and life in the Spirit. Some in Galatia were using the law to judge and boast in others' conduct (6:12–13). Paul's answer is that spiritual people fulfill "the law of Christ" by bearing one another's burdens (6:2). Correction is therefore a form of bearing a burden. It is an act of love that shares in the other's weakness, not a display of one's own standing. The verse shows that true spirituality is measured less by one's ability to expose sin than by one's willingness to restore a fallen brother while remembering that one could fall too.
