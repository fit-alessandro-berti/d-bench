Matthew 12:34–35 presents all three dimensions at once—symptomatic, revelatory, and volitional—but it does so within a single metaphor: **the heart is a moral treasury, and speech is its currency.** Jesus’ appeal to “the abundance of the heart” (*perisseuma tēs kardias*) and to “treasure” (*thesauros*) implies a storeroom that has been filled over time. Words are the disbursement of what has been accumulated. Because the heart in biblical anthropology is the unified center of thinking, desiring, and willing, speech is never merely accidental leakage or purely detached decision; it is the overflowing of the self.

Here is how the three categories function together.

**1. Symptomatic: Speech as organic overflow**  
Jesus has just used tree-and-fruit logic (v. 33), and He repeats it here. Fruit does not *choose* to be sweet or bitter; it reveals what kind of tree produced it. Likewise, the mouth “speaketh” not because a person intermittently selects from a neutral shelf of vocabulary, but because the heart is full and, under pressure, it overflows. The Pharisees’ words against the Spirit are not random stylistic preferences; they are symptoms of a heart already saturated with opposition to God.

**2. Revelatory: Speech as diagnostic evidence**  
The rhetorical situation makes the *diagnostic* function explicit. Jesus is exposing the inner life of His accusers. Their speech unmasks what they have concealed. Because humans control words more easily than thoughts or feelings, they often think they can hide their true posture. Jesus reverses this: their own utterance becomes the public evidence of private enmity. This anticipates Matthew 12:36–37, where words function as the legal record rendered at judgment. In this sense, speech reveals a moral “stockpile” that would otherwise remain hidden.

**3. Choice: Speech as accountable moral action**  
Yet the passage is not deterministic fatalism. The treasury is filled by prior free acts of allegiance, attention, and desire. A person becomes a “good man” or an “evil man” through habitual choices, and every act of speaking is itself a choice. Jesus’ warning that people will give account for “every idle word” (v. 36) treats speech as voluntary action, not involuntary spasm. So while speech *reveals* a pre-existing condition, it also *contributes* to the condition. The mouth is both barometer and builder.

**Synthesis**  
The passage holds these together because, for Jesus, the inner life is not a compartment behind the personality; the person *is* the heart, and the mouth is the heart made audible. Therefore:

> **Words are chosen revelations of a symptomatically accumulated character.**

You cannot treat speech as meaningless leakage (“I didn’t mean it”), nor as purely external performance (“it’s just words”). They are the overflow of what is loved and stored, yet they remain acts for which the speaker is responsible. Consequently, hearing one’s own speech is a form of spiritual diagnosis: if the treasure is foul, the overflow will eventually tell.
