The image is sharp because it places the two faults in the same organ—the eye—the very faculty by which one judges. The critic is supposed to help someone else see more clearly, yet he himself has a “beam,” a large wooden plank, blinding his own sight. So he offers to remove a tiny “mote” or speck while being obviously, ridiculously blind himself.

Several details intensify the satire:

- **Scale contrast:** a mote is trivial; a beam is massive. The disproportion exposes the critic’s lack of self-awareness.
- **Visual absurdity:** a man with a beam in his eye trying to perform eye surgery is a comic, impossible image.
- **Rhetorical questions:** Jesus frames it as an illogical contradiction rather than merely a moral failing: “Why beholdest thou…? Or how wilt thou say…?”
- **The word “hypocrite”:** in Greek it suggests playing a role, like an actor. The person is performing the part of a wise helper while hiding his own moral blindness.

But the passage does not forbid moral correction. In fact, the final command assumes it: “first cast out the beam out of thine own eye; and then shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote out of thy brother’s eye.” The issue is not whether one should help another, but the order, condition, and spirit in which one does so.

So Jesus condemns judgment that is:

- self-deceived,
- hypocritical,
- lacking humility,
- and blind to one’s own larger faults.

But he leaves room for loving correction after self-examination, repentance, and clear sight. The point is not “never judge,” but “first deal honestly with yourself; then you may help your brother see clearly.”
