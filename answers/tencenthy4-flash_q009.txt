Paul defines victory over evil as **refusing to become evil’s agent** and instead **answering evil with active, undeserved good**.

The passage’s logic is:

1. **Do not avenge yourselves** — judgment belongs to God: “Vengeance is mine, I will repay” (Rom. 12:19).
2. **Meet the enemy’s real need** — feed him, give him drink.
3. **Do not be overcome by evil** — do not let hatred, fear, revenge, or cruelty conquer your character.
4. **Overcome evil with good** — victory is not retaliatory superiority, but goodness that evil cannot corrupt.

So the “victory” is not primarily that the enemy is destroyed or humiliated. It is that **evil fails to reproduce itself in you**. If the enemy repents, the victory is redemptive. If the enemy remains hardened, the victory is still theirs-by-faith’s preservation from evil and God’s final justice.

Regarding “heap coals of fire on his head,” the phrase quotes Proverbs 25:21–22. It likely means that undeserved kindness will “burn” the enemy’s conscience—bringing shame, remorse, and possibly repentance. Some also see an echo of God’s judgment: if the enemy rejects the kindness, he stores up accountability before God. Either way, the Christian’s task is not to inflict pain but to do good.

Therefore the command is:

- **Not weak**, if by “strength” we mean mere force, retaliation, or dominance. But it is deeply strong in the biblical sense: it requires self-mastery, courage, trust in God’s justice, and love under provocation.
- **Not manipulative**, if done as Paul commands: sincerely, without disguise, seeking the enemy’s true good. It would become manipulative if used as a calculated “kill them with kindness” tactic to make someone feel guilty.
- **Genuinely strong**, because it breaks the cycle of evil. It is cruciform strength—the strength displayed in Christ, who overcame evil not by mirroring it but by suffering, forgiving, and doing good.

This does not mean enabling danger or abandoning justice. Feeding an enemy does not mean giving them opportunity to harm others. Love may include boundaries, truthful confrontation, protection of the innocent, and lawful accountability. But it excludes personal hatred and revenge.

In short: **the passage defines victory as good overcoming evil without becoming evil.** It is not weakness; it is disciplined, God-trusting, Christlike strength.
