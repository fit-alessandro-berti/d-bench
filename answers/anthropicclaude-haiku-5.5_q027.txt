The parable names its own target in the first verse: those "which trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and despised others." So the primary charge is **self-trust**, the confidence that one's standing before God rests on one's own record. Contempt for others is the second half of that verdict. The other candidates you list are real, but they are better understood as ways this root shows itself.

**What condemns the Pharisee**

- **Not mainly false doctrine.** His facts are largely accurate. Fasting twice a week and tithing were commendable practices, and Luke 11:42 shows Jesus affirming Pharisaic tithing. The parable does not condemn good works or orthodox practice. The problem is what the Pharisee does with them.
- **Pride (the root).** He treats his virtues as his own achievements, and his gratitude is really self-congratulation. "I thank thee that I am not..." thanks God for a difference he credits to himself.
- **Comparison.** He measures himself against other men rather than against God's holiness. Anyone can look good next to an extortioner. The standard he uses is horizontal, so it can never expose him.
- **Contempt.** His gaze on the publican is the visible result of the comparison. Looking down on others is what self-trust requires in order to stay comfortable.
- **Virtue as self-display.** His virtues function as credentials. He is not confessing and asking; he is presenting a résumé. "I give tithes of all that I possess" reads more like a claim on God than a gift to God.
- **Prayer that is not really directed to God.** The Greek *pros heauton* ("with himself" or "to himself") allows the reading that he prays inwardly about himself. He is a spectator of his own goodness, and God becomes an audience for his self-assessment. He asks for nothing because he thinks he needs nothing.

So the diagnosis is pride, expressed through comparison and contempt, performed through virtue, and shown in a prayer that never truly reaches God. Doctrine is implicated only in the sense that his posture embodies a works-based self-justification, which is the opposite of what "justified" means.

**Why the publican's posture is spiritually truer**

- **He sees God accurately.** His plea, "God be merciful" (*hilasthēti*, a word with propitiatory connotations), addresses a holy God who must show mercy. He does not treat God as a rubber stamp for his self-evaluation.
- **He sees himself accurately.** "The sinner," with the definite article, names himself as *the* sinner, not as one sinner among the respectable. He brings no comparisons and no claims.
- **His posture matches his need.** Standing afar, lowered eyes, and beating his breast are gestures of someone who knows he has nothing to offer. His body expresses his prayer.
- **He asks for exactly what he lacks.** The Pharisee needed nothing; the publican needed everything. God answers need, not display.
- **He has nothing to protect.** Because he does not trust himself, his only resource is God's mercy, which is the only ground on which justification has ever rested.

Jesus's closing principle, "every one that exalteth himself shall be abased; and he that humbleth himself shall be exalted," describes the mechanism. Self-trust makes a person unable to receive what God gives, while honest need is precisely the condition in which mercy can be received. The publican goes home justified not because his posture earns anything, but because it is the only posture that accepts a verdict of grace.
