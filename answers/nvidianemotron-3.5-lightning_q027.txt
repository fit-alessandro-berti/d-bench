### What Condemns the Pharisee?

The text itself singles out the Pharisee's **heart posture**—specifically, that he **"trusted in himself that he was righteous, and despised others"** (v. 9). The other options you listed are all real, but they function as symptoms of that root condition:

| Option | Is it present? | How the text uses it |
|--------|---------------|----------------------|
| **False doctrine** | No | The Pharisee isn’t teaching wrong theology; he’s observing right rituals (fasting, tithing). His sin isn’t doctrinal error but *misplaced trust* in his own performance. |
| **Pride** | Yes, and it’s the core sin | “Trusted in himself” = self-righteousness. He doesn’t seek God’s mercy; he performs for an audience of one (himself) and God, to confirm his own status. |
| **Comparison** | Yes, it’s the method he uses | “I am not as other men are…” His entire prayer is defined by looking downward at others to elevate himself. Comparison is the visible expression of his pride. |
| **Contempt** | Yes, it’s the relational result | He “despised others.” His righteousness creates a caste system where he is superior and the tax collector (and others) are inferior. |
| **Use of virtue as self-display** | Yes, it’s the mechanism | He trots out his fasting and tithing not as gratitude to God, but as credentials to prove his acceptability. The virtues are real, but they’ve been turned into a mirror for self-admiration rather than a window to God. |

**The condemnation is ultimately prideful self-trust.** The Pharisee doesn’t need God’s grace because, in his own estimation, he *is* the gift. Everything else—comparison, contempt, performative virtue—flows from that central assumption. Jesus’ concluding saying makes this explicit: *“For everyone who exalts himself will be humbled, and he who humbles himself will be exalted”* (v. 14). The Pharisee exalts; the tax collector humbles.

### Why the Tax Collector’s Posture Is Spiritually True

The tax collector stands “afar off,” refuses to lift his eyes heavenward, beats his breast, and prays: **“God, be merciful to me, a sinner.”** Several things make his posture the model of true spirituality here:

1. **He recognizes his actual condition** — He doesn’t pretend to be something he’s not. He owns the label “sinner” without qualification or excuse.
2. **His prayer is entirely receptive** — *“Be merciful to me”* (Greek *hilastheti*) is a plea for *propitiatory mercy*—the kind of grace that deals with guilt and restores relationship. He brings nothing to the table but his need.
3. **He trusts God’s character, not his own performance** — Whereas the Pharisee says, “I am not like other men,” the tax collector says, “God, *you* be merciful to *me*.” He leans on God’s compassion, not his own merit.
4. **The theological principle is inverted** — Jesus’ own verdict and the proverbial saying of v. 14 lock this in: justification comes not to the self-confident, but to the humble. The tax collector goes home “justified” (declared righteous) because he came empty-handed and left receiving mercy. The Pharisee goes home still “unjustified” because he came full-handed and left still trusting in his own hand.

In short: the Pharisee’s sin is **self-trust masquerading as piety**; the tax collector’s spirituality is **God-trust masquerading as contrition**. One performs righteousness to be seen; the other pleads mercy to be saved. That’s why the tax collector’s posture is the one Jesus affirms.
