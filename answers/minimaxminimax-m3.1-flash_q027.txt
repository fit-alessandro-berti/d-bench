## The short answer

None of those five is the *root*. All of them are symptoms of one thing, and the text itself names that thing in its opening line: "trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and despised others." The condemnation is not primarily *what the Pharisee believed*, not *pride in the generic sense*, and not even comparison or contempt as such — it is **self-righteousness in the sense of making his own merit the ground of his standing with God.** Comparison, contempt, and self-display are the *mechanism* by which that self-righteousness becomes visible and disqualifying.

The most precise single phrase for the Pharisee's error is the one buried in the Greek: *ὁ μὲν ἑαυτῷ δικαῖος* — "the one who was **righteous in his own eyes**." And the publican's corresponding posture is equally marked in the Greek: *ὁ δὲ ἑαυτῶν ἔλειπε* — "the one who **humbled himself**." The whole parable turns on that pair.

---

## 1. Sorting the five possibilities

**False doctrine — no.** There is no heresy here. The Pharisee's theology is substantially orthodox; his problem is orthopraxy. He believes in God, he believes in the law, he fasts and tithes correctly. This parable is not about *what he taught* but about *how he stood before God*.

**Pride — yes, but too vague.** "Pride" is the popular label, but it flattens something specific. The point is not that the Pharisee thinks well of himself in the abstract; it's that he thinks well of himself *in relation to God*. He has quietly relocated the ground of his righteousness from God's grace to his own record. That relocation is the sin.

**Comparison — yes, and it's the explicit hinge.** Note the structure of his prayer: "I am **not as other men are**… or even as this publican." He builds his righteousness *by subtraction*, by sorting humanity into two bins. Worse, he does it *to a specific man standing in the same room*. The parable is engineered so that the comparison has a face on it.

**Contempt — yes, and Jesus names it.** "Despised others" is in the frame verse. This is a moral and spiritual vice, not just a rhetorical one: contempt is the sin of the *human being in the presence of other human beings*.

**Virtue used as self-display — yes, and this is the sharpest available label.** The Pharisee isn't wrong that he fasts and tithes. He's wrong about *what those acts are for*. They function as credentials presented to God — and, inevitably, as a public demonstration before the room. This is the point where the parable touches modern readers hard: religious practice that has become a resumé.

---

## 2. The one thing the Pharisee never does

Read the two prayers side by side and a structural asymmetry appears that sermons often miss.

The Pharisee **addresses God** — "God, I thank thee" — and then talks entirely about *himself*: his practices, his separations, his comparisons. His prayer contains **no request**. He never asks God for anything. He informs God of his record. It is, grammatically, a eucharistia — a thanksgiving — and it is a thanksgiving for *himself*.

The publican also addresses God, but his whole prayer is one petition: "God, **be merciful to me** a sinner." He asks.

So the contrast is not "one man was religious and the other was irreligious," nor even "one was proud and one was humble." It is **two men relating to God in fundamentally different ways**: one arrives with a portfolio, the other arrives with nothing but a cry. The Pharisee has come to *be seen to be justified*; the publican has come to *be justified*. The Pharisee treats prayer as the place where his status is confirmed. The publican treats it as the only place where his status can possibly be settled, and he knows he has no standing to bring.

And note the word at the end: **δικαιωθη — "justified."** Not "humbled," not "forgiven," not "accepted." *Justified* is Paul's word, and Luke drops it here deliberately. Justification is God's act of acquittal on the ground of Christ's righteousness, received by faith. The publican goes home in a state he did not manufacture.

---

## 3. Why the posture is truer: the theology of the temple

This is where the physical details stop being piety accessories and start carrying theological weight.

**"Standing afar off."** In first-century temple geography, "afar off" was the *outer court* — the place of Gentiles and of those whose ritual eligibility was in question. The publican stands where a man stands who does not presume to come near. Luke 19:7 will use the same spatial language ("standing afar off" of Jesus) to describe Zacchaeus, and there it signals both distance and shame.

**"Would not lift up so much as his eyes unto heaven."** Compare the temple liturgy: the priest stood before the altar with *eyes lifted and hands extended*, offering the incense and the prayer. The publican cannot pray that prayer. His body will not perform the priest's posture. He is not merely choosing humility as a style; he is *unable to assume a posture of entitlement*.

**"Smote upon his breast."** This is the gesture of self-accusation — the physical acknowledgment of a wound. It is the body confessing what the mouth can barely say. And it is a *solitary, non-performative* gesture: it is not done for the room. It is done for God.

**And "standing."** The Pharisee *stood* — the same verb used of the priest at his post. He is at ease, upright, in his place, and he looks around.

The whole parable is a contest between two bodies in a courtyard, and the text has already told us who wins.

---

## 4. Why the Pharisee's prayer is *falsely* oriented

Here's the deepest layer. The Pharisee's prayer is not simply boastful; it is **mis-addressed**.

- He thanks God for his own excellences, which implicitly asks God to ratify a standard of excellence *other than* God's own grace. His thanksgiving is really a claim: *I am qualified, so receive me.*
- He compares himself to others, which requires that God be a judge who evaluates candidates by comparison. But the publican comes to a God who saves the unrighteous and destroys the self-righteous (cf. Luke 20:41–44; Isa. 28:21).
- He despises the publican, which means he cannot be in the same posture of need. You cannot simultaneously say "God, be merciful to me, a sinner" about a man you have just sorted out as worthless.

So the three evils are not a list; they are a *chain*. Comparison requires contempt; contempt requires the self-justification that makes comparison tempting; and self-justification requires that his own merit be the ground of his standing — which is precisely what disqualifies him. **The root is: he wanted to be justified on his own account, in the sight of God and men.**

---

## 5. The final irony: "this man"

"I tell you, **this man** went down to his house justified rather than the other."

"This man" — meaning the one nobody would have named. And the ending is *rather than*, a comparative. It's not that the Pharisee was condemned and the publican accepted as a second-best. It's that the publican stands *closer to the way of the kingdom*, because the publican is doing, in his body, what the kingdom requires: coming as a claimant-less person and receiving mercy.

And this lands with force in Luke's narrative setting. In Luke 15 the Pharisees have already said, "He eats and drinks with tax collectors and sinners" (Luke 15:1–2) — the same men, the same tax collector, the same dinner. In Luke 18 the disciples ask, "Who is the greatest?" and Jesus answers with a child (Luke 18:15–17), and then with this parable. **The Pharisees are hearing a verdict on themselves.** The older brother in Luke 15 is the Pharisee of Luke 18, and the great feast at the end of Luke 15 is the table at which the tax collector is welcomed and the self-righteous man stands outside. Luke has been building toward this for a long time.

So the Pharisee's exclusion is not primarily about his sins being worse. He may have been *tax*-free while the publican was extortionate — the two were in the same trade family. The publican doesn't get a better report card; he gets a different posture.

---

## 6. Two cautions

**Don't moralize this into "sinners are better than religious people."** The publican is not condemned for his tax collecting, and he's not admired for it. Nor is he admired for wallowing in guilt. His prayer is not "God, forgive me, I am sorry, but I am at least not like that Pharisee" — he doesn't even look up. The point is not comparative merit but *whose merit is in view*. This parable kills the "at least I'm not like them" morality just as decisively as it kills "I'm basically a good person."

**Don't turn the publican into a model of self-deprecation.** "God be merciful to me a sinner" is not performative self-hatred; it is confession of fact — *ou tous hamartoloi* — "I am a sinner." It's the acknowledgment of a real condition that only grace addresses. And crucially, it is *answered*: the verdict is justification. This is not a parable about how sorry you feel; it's about whether your sorrow is *for your sin* or *for your image*.

---

## A summary

| | Pharisee | Publican |
|---|---|---|
| Basis of standing | own merit | God's mercy |
| Prayer is | a report (no request) | a plea |
| Eyes | presumably lifted, scanning | will not lift up |
| Body | standing at ease, "stood" | afar off, hand on breast |
| Speech | comparison + contempt | confession of sin |
| Relation to others | sorting them into bins | not thinking about them at all |
| Verdict | not on this ground | *δικαιωθη* — justified |

The Pharisee is condemned because **his virtues were his case, and his case excluded him.** The publican's posture is truer because his body said what his mouth said, and what both said was the only thing that can be said and believed: *I have nothing; have mercy.* That is the posture of faith, and it is the posture of the kingdom Jesus announces — where the last are first, and where the man nobody was watching is the one who goes home right with God.
