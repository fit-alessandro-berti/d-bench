It does not forbid judgment. It takes the accusation as real, leaves the sin named, and still takes the stone out of every hand present.

That is why it is destabilizing. The crowd’s moral picture depends on a clean split: her guilt on one side, their right to punish on the other. The sentence accepts the first and cancels the second. “Without sin” is not a technicality about this particular offense. It means: if you are going to make the law into a weapon, you must first stand outside the condition the law condemns. No one there can. So the law remains true and becomes unusable as a license for the mob.

Hypocrisy is exposed, but not as a simple “you did it too.” The accusers are disqualified by standing under judgment while performing the role of pure executors. Mob-righteousness is interrupted because “first” and “among you” force each person out of the collective and into a private reckoning. The oldest leave first because they understand the standard soonest.

The deeper cut is this: moral correctness does not confer moral authority to destroy. Knowing she is guilty does not make them innocent enough to kill her. The sentence separates the truth of a condemnation from the standing of the condemner. It leaves the sin intact and the punishment impossible, which is intolerable to any righteousness that needs a victim in order to feel clean.

And the only one in the scene who could have met the condition does not throw. That is the final destabilization: the right to cast the stone, if it exists at all, is not exercised.
