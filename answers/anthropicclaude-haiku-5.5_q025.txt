The verse locates the danger in **purpose**, not visibility. Jesus assumes that good deeds will often be seen. Just before this, he tells his hearers to let their light shine before others so that they may "see your good works and glorify your Father" (5:16). The same public visibility is commended there and warned against here, so visibility cannot be the problem.

The key phrase is the Greek *pros to theathēnai autois*, "with a view to being seen by them." The grammar points to intention. The danger is doing the act *in order to* be watched and admired. Visibility becomes the goal that governs the deed.

Several features of the text support this reading:

1. **The reward is the audience.** Jesus says that those who perform for an audience "have their reward" (6:2, 5, 16). The approval they wanted is what they receive, and nothing more is owed. The "reward" of the Father is contrasted with this: the one who acts for God is rewarded by God, which is not the same as receiving human praise.

2. **The secret giving tests the motive.** Verses 3–4 ("let not your left hand know what your right hand doeth") are not a rule that goodness must be hidden. They are a test of whether the act survives without an audience. If your giving would stop when no one could see it, the audience was the real motive.

3. **The verse is a warning about the self.** The question is what the act is *for*: God's glory, the good of the neighbor, or one's own standing. Public goodness is dangerous because it tempts the doer to make a self-display out of an act of love.

So the craving to be seen is the precise problem. But the craving should be understood as the operative desire, the thing that would change your behavior if the audience disappeared. Being seen is not the sin. Needing to be seen is. This also explains why the warning requires "take heed": the motive is hard to inspect, and people can deceive themselves about it, sometimes performing for an imagined audience even when no one is present.
