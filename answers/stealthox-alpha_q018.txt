## The architecture: put off / put on

The placement isn't accidental — it's structural. Verses 31–32 are the final movement of a section (4:17–24) built entirely on the metaphor of undressing the "old self" and dressing the "new self." Verse 31 is the *taking off* (ἀρθήτω, "let it be lifted away"); verse 32 is the *putting on*. Bitterness and forgiveness aren't merely contrasted as opposing emotions — they're presented as two garments that cannot be worn at once, occupying the same position in the same wardrobe.

Notice also that the replacement is *more demanding* than the removal. Paul doesn't say "stop being bitter and leave it at that." He commands active kindness (χρηστοί), visceral compassion (εὔσπλαγχνοι — literally "good-inward-parts"), and continuous forgiving. The vacuum left by uprooted bitterness must be filled with goodwill toward the very person who caused it.

## What the text thinks bitterness is

Look at the internal order of verse 31: **bitterness → wrath → anger → clamour → evil speaking**. Most commentators see an inward-to-outward gradient. Bitterness (πικρία) is the root condition; wrath (θυμός) is the hot flare; anger (ὀργή) is the settled version of it; clamour and slander are what it sounds like when it goes public. On this reading, bitterness is *generative* — it never stays private. Hebrews 12:15 makes the same point with agricultural imagery: a "root of bitterness" that "defiles many."

And the immediate context sharpens this. Just verses earlier (4:26–27) Paul permits anger as an experience — "be angry and do not sin" — but forbids giving it *tenancy*: "do not let the sun go down on your provocation, neither give place to the devil." Bitterness, in this framework, is precisely anger that has been given lodging. It's not the sting of injury; it's the decision to keep the wound open and itemized.

## The grammar answers your question directly

This is the strongest evidence. Feelings, strictly speaking, can't be commanded away — but *postures* can. And every verb here is imperative:

- **ἀρθήτω** — a decisive, punctiliar removal, like stripping off a garment. You can do this today.
- **γίνεσθε** — a present imperative: *keep becoming* kind. Ongoing.
- **χαριζόμενοι** — a present participle: *continually forgiving*. A practice, not an event.

Both sides of the antithesis are durative. Neither bitterness nor forgiveness, in this text, is a passing emotional weather pattern. They are maintained states — which means the load-bearing category is **chosen posture that hardens into habit**. The feeling is the seed; the posture is the plant; the habit is the trunk.

## Is it contagious?

Yes — and the text signals this in several ways:

1. **Everything is plural and reciprocal.** "To one another," "among yourselves." Ephesians addresses a community whose unity is the letter's central concern (4:3). One member's unforgiveness calcifies relationships far beyond the original dispute.
2. **Unresolved anger gives "place to the devil"** (4:27) — it creates territory beyond yourself.
3. **Verse 30 stands right before the list:** bitterness "grieves the Holy Spirit" — the Spirit who seals the *whole* community, not just the aggrieved individual.
4. Colossians 3:19 uses the same verb-family relationally: husbands must not be "bitter" *against* their wives — bitterness as something done *to* others, not merely felt within.

## The deepest reason they're set against each other

They are rival economies. The verb for forgive, χαρίζομαι, is built on χάρις — *grace*. To forgive is to "grace" someone: to cancel a debt gratuitously. Bitterness is the refusal to cancel it — the insistence that payment is owed. And the standard is explicit: "even as God in Christ forgave you." You were forgiven at cost, before you repaid anything. Bitterness quietly denies that the gospel applies to *this* offender.

That's why they can't coexist. Given a real wrong, you will render exactly one verdict: the debt stands, or the debt is cancelled. Bitterness and forgiveness aren't opposite points on an emotional spectrum — they're opposite rulings on the same case file. One keeps the ledger; the other tears it up because your own page was already torn up first.

**So, to name it plainly:** the text treats bitterness as a feeling in its origin, a posture in its essence, a habit in its persistence, and a contagion in its effect — but the imperative grammar shows that Paul holds you responsible for it precisely because it sits at the level of *decision*, not chemistry.
