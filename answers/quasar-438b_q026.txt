In this passage from Don Quixote’s story *El Quijote*, speech is stripped of its modern function as a tool for strategic communication and reimagined as a hydraulic mechanism of the soul. 

Here is how the passage connects speech to hidden moral stockpiles, and how it answers the question of whether words are symptoms, choices, revelations, or all three.

### 1. Connecting Speech to "Hidden Moral Stockpiles"
The passage establishes a strict, isometric ratio between the hidden internal state of a person (the "treasure of the heart") and their external verbal output (what "the mouth speaketh"). 

Don Quixote uses metaphors of quantity and storage: "abundance," "treasure," "bringeth forth," and "generation." He conceptualizes the human soul as a reservoir hoarding a specific moral substance—either good or evil. Speech is not drawn from the intellect or the throat, but squeezed out from this cardiac reserve. 

Because speech originates from this hidden stockpile, it cannot contradict it. An evil man cannot dip into a good vocabulary because his reservoir only contains "evil treasure." His mouth is merely a faucet attached to the tank of his soul; whatever pours out will necessarily match the moral quality of what is stored within. 

### 2. Are Words Symptoms, Choices, Revelations, or All Three?
The passage suggests that words are **revelations** and **symptoms**, but Quixote specifically uses this logic to attack the very idea that words are voluntary **choices** (at least in the way a hypocrite might claim). 

**Words as Revelations:**
The passage functions as an epistemological key. Quixote is arguing with hypocrites (the "generation of vipers") who speak virtuously but act wickedly. Quixote uses this passage to assert that speech *reveals* the hidden truth of a man's soul. If a seemingly virtuous man's actions are evil, his hidden heart must be evil, and therefore his seemingly "good" speech is a deception. Good speech reveals a good heart; evil speech reveals an evil heart. It lifts the veil on the invisible interior.

**Words as Symptoms:**
"Out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaketh" suggests an involuntary, almost physiological overflow. Words are the necessary *symptom* of the soul's condition. Just as a feverish body sweats or a bursting fruit seeps juice, a heart overburdened with moral quality (good or bad) will naturally excrete that quality through the mouth. The hypocrite cannot choose to simply hold his evil inside; if it is abundant, it will symptomaticly leak into his diction.

**Words as Choices (The Denial Thereof):**
In modern terms, we view words as choices—linguistic tools we select to achieve a goal. Don Quixote rejects this. To him, words are not a menu of options from which an evil man can pick a "good" item to disguise himself. You cannot *choose* to speak good things if your heart is evil, because speech is not a mask you can wear; it is the direct, siphoned liquid of your inner moral reserve. 

If there is any "choice" in this framework, it is only the choice of the good man, who consciously "bringeth forth" (draws from) his good treasure to produce good words. The evil man has no such agency to select good words; he is mechanically locked to his evil lexicon. 

### Summary
Ultimately, the passage implies that **words are the symptomatic revelation of a hidden moral stockpile**. By defining speech this way, Don Quixote logically destroys the hypocrite's defense: one cannot separate the moral quality of what one *says* from the moral quality of what one *is*. Speech and soul are made of the exact same substance, turning every utterance into an unforgeable X-ray of the heart.
