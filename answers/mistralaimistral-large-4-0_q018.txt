The short answer is that Ephesians 4 treats bitterness not as a passing feeling but as a **chosen, cultivated posture that is contagious** — and it places forgiveness directly against it because forgiveness is the gospel-shaped act that displaces bitterness at its root.

**Bitterness as the root of a cascade.** Look at the order of the list in verse 31: *pikria* (bitterness) comes first, then wrath, anger, clamour, evil speaking, and finally “all malice.” The sequence moves from the hidden interior outward into public speech and action. Bitterness is the seed; wrath, anger, shouting, and slander are the harvest. So the text is not describing a momentary irritation but a settled inner orientation that inevitably expresses itself and infects the community around it. The church is the “you” throughout, so bitterness is never merely private — it corrodes the body.

**A posture to be “put away.”** The command is that these things be “put away from you” (ἀρθήτω ἀφ’ ὑμῶν). That is the language of decisive removal — stripping off a garment. It echoes the “put off / put on” logic of Ephesians 4:22–24 (the old man and the new man). Bitterness is therefore something we are actively, repeatedly refusing to keep wearing. It is not an involuntary emotion that simply happens to us; it is a habitual stance we maintain toward someone who has wronged us.

**Contagious by design.** The contagion is explicit in the cascade itself, and the biblical background makes it sharper: Hebrews 12:15 warns of “a root of bitterness” (ῥίζα πικρίας) springing up and defiling many. Ephesians does not use the word “root,” but the logic is the same — bitterness spreads. It turns a private grievance into public wrath, shouting, and slander. Left unrepented, it doesn’t stay inside one person; it becomes a climate.

**Why forgiveness is the direct opposite.** Verse 32 answers with three present-tense, ongoing realities: *become* kind, tenderhearted, and *forgiving one another* (χαριζόμενοι ἑαυτοῖς). The participle is continuous and reciprocal — forgiveness is a mutual, habitual practice, not a one-time transaction. And the word itself, χαρίζομαι, literally means “to give grace.” Forgiveness is not merely ceasing to resent; it is actively giving unmerited favor.

That is why the two are set against each other. Bitterness says, “You owe me; I will hold this against you.” Forgiveness says, “I give you grace.” Bitterness hoards the offense; forgiveness releases it — and releases it *because* of the closing clause: “even as God in Christ has forgiven you.” The indicative (God’s finished forgiveness of us) grounds and empowers the imperative (our ongoing forgiveness of one another). We forgive not from moral willpower but from the position of the forgiven.

So the text treats bitterness as all of what you named — a feeling that hardens into a habit, a habit that becomes a chosen posture, and a posture that turns contagious — while forgiveness is the grace-shaped habit, grounded in Christ’s forgiveness of us, that breaks the cycle at its root and rebuilds the community instead of corroding it.
