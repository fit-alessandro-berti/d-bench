## What the Text Actually Does

Notice the sequence: prohibition (19), prescription (20), and then an explanation with the explanatory particle *gar* — "**For** where your treasure is, there will your heart be also" (21). The word *gar* introduces a *reason*. So the relation between treasure and heart is the ground on which the command to "lay up" makes sense.

That ordering matters. If the text merely *revealed* the heart, it would be a diagnostic proverb and the imperative in verse 20 would be pointless. But the imperative stands first, and the disclosure follows. The text assumes that treasure *does something to a heart* — and that what it does is then visible.

So the honest answer is: **it says both, and it joins them.** Treasure is not a mirror held up to the heart; it is a mold pressed against it.

## The Revealing Sense

Verse 21 is a statement of fact, not a command: "your heart *will be* also there." This is disclosure. Where a person's treasure lies is where their heart lies — and the world gets to watch this happen in a way you cannot supervise. You do not have to announce your loves; your treasury, your calendar, your checking account, and your attention announce them for you.

Two features of the vocabulary sharpen this. The verb in vv. 19–20 is *thēsaurizō*, "to lay up in a storehouse" — the language of deliberate, surveyed accumulation, of choosing what to secure and where to secure it. And "treasure" in Matthew's usage is not restricted to money; it is whatever a person treats as their own secure provision, their *own* (note the phrase "lay up for *yourselves*"). Matthew 13's treasure in the field is treasure in a field; the treasure is in the field, and to *find* it is to *lose* everything else and go there. So the question Jesus presses is not "do you have treasure" but "whose treasury is your life stored in."

## The Training Sense — and Why It Is the More Urgent One

If treasure only revealed the heart, verse 20 could have been phrased as an invitation ("consider whether earth or heaven has your heart"). Instead it is an imperative with parallel structure — *not* this, *but* that. Consequently the text asserts that the direction in which a person lays up treasure *changes* where the heart goes.

What is the mechanism? Look closely at the language of corruption in verse 19: "moth and rust doth corrupt, and where thieves break through and steal." These are three agents of decay and intrusion. And they are not only outside threats to the treasure; they are simultaneous threats to the heart that treasures it. The condition that makes moth-and-rust damage *also* makes it corrosive to the one treasuring it — — the treasure is "where moth and rust doth corrupt," so the heart's attention is being trained by decay, and the training is toward decay. Meanwhile the thieves that "break through" are not merely a thief in the storeroom; "break through" is a phrase used of breaking into a house or a stronghold, i.e., **habitual, systematic intrusion**. That is precisely what happens to a heart whose treasure is earthly: something breaks in through the routine and takes over.

And heaven is described in v. 20 with the *same* terms negated: "neither moth nor rust doth corrupt, and where thieves do not break through nor steal." The repetition makes the connection: **the imperishability of the treasure is the same as the integrity of the heart that holds it.** Treasure that cannot be moth-eaten cannot make you moth-eaten. Treasure that cannot be stolen through a breach cannot be seized through a breach in your life.

## Possession, Attention, Love: How They Interlock

The text does not give a syllogism here; it gives a sequence that loops. Read it in three movements:

**1. Possession fixes a direction of attention.** You cannot treasure what you have not chosen to secure. The choice of what to lay up is the choice of what to *attend to*, and attention is the mechanism by which a thing becomes an object of love.

**2. Attention is the hinge.** This is the key that many readings miss. Verse 22 (immediately following) says "the light of the body is the eye," and in that verse the eye is the *lamp* through which the whole body is flooded with light — and the eye, Jesus says, "follows the treasure." Attention in the biblical psychology is not a neutral gaze; it is the soul's reaching. Where you look repeatedly, you come to love.

**3. Love, once fixed, becomes the new possession.** "Where your treasure is, there will your heart be also" runs in both directions at once. Treasure conditions the heart (v. 20's imperative), and the heart's location is disclosed by the treasure (v. 21's *gar* — "for"). So the shape is a **ratchet**: treasure → attention → love → habituated regard for the same objects → a life reorganized around securing those objects → treasure again, now more deeply embedded.

That ratchet is exactly why treasure *trains*. It is not that you consciously love what you secure. It is that your securing is itself a form of love — a daily, repeated act of preference — and your heart follows your hands. Hence Calvin's line, usually quoted as glib but here quite exact: **"Everyone's heart is where their treasure is."** The treasure does not represent the love; it *constitutes* the love, and then displays it.

And this is the point at which possession and love become *tied*, in the strict sense: the Bible's word for "treasure" and its word for "love" are nearly interchangeable. "Where your treasure is" ≈ "what you love most." That is why the verse can claim an identity without argument. A person who has laid up treasure in heaven *has*, in that very act, loved heaven; and a person whose treasure lies on earth *has* loved earth, whether or not they would confess it.

## The Two Treasures Are Not Weightless

The passage is often softened into "balance," as though Jesus were saying "hedging your bets is fine, just put a little in heaven." But the grammar is exclusive. *Lay not* up treasure on earth… *but* lay up treasure in heaven. There is no third pile. And the following verses (6:24) sharpen it: "No man can serve two masters." And "mammon" there is not simply money — it is the deity of the self-sufficient hoard, of what you *own* being what you *are*. To serve mammon and God is not a budgeting split; you cannot, because the treasure *is* the allegiance.

Notice also that the two sentences in v. 19 are not a reason *against* earthly treasure ("it gets stolen") but a description of its *intrinsic* constitution: moth and rust corrupt; thieves break through. Earth-treasure is treasure *by nature* in its tendency to perish. If your treasure genuinely cannot be moth-eaten or rust-eaten or stolen through a breach, that is not a sign of good financial management — that is a description of what does not decay.

## The Highest Stakes: Where the Treasure Actually Is

The question Jesus is pressing is not "do you love money?" but "**what have you actually made your treasure?**" Because the follow-on verses make clear that this is existentially serious in a way that goes beyond pies in the sky. 6:22–23: the eye is the lamp of the body, and if the eye is evil the whole body is full of darkness. 6:24: two masters. 6:25–34: anxiety, worry, the futility of trying to serve both. Then 6:33 closes the section: "seek first the kingdom of God and all these things shall be added." Which is itself a treasure statement — because "all these things shall be *added* to you" is a promise that even the things you were anxious about are, in Christ's hands, *layable up* where they cannot perish. Jesus is not discouraging provision; he is relocating where provision is secured.

And in Matthew's own imagery, the treasure in heaven is not a warehouse of cash. It is the kingdom, the field, the pearl of great price, the house of the father, the man who went into the field and sold all he had. In this Matthew, treasure in heaven belongs to a King who *is* in the field — so "seek first the kingdom" means: go where the treasure is actually located, and *find* it; which is the same as losing what you were gripping. The treasure in heaven is not money relocated; it is a Person, and finding him requires the exchange of verse 19's accumulation.

## A Consequence for Reading Your Own Life

If treasure reveals the heart, then your treasury is a *public* document, and you cannot edit it. If treasure trains the heart, then your attention is plastic and your habits are the curriculum in which you are being formed. Which means the question Jesus asks is not answered by what you believe, or even by what you *say* you love, but by what your hands reach for when no one is watching: what you plan to secure, what you save, what you cannot let go of, what you check.

And it means the way out is not primarily *diagnosis* but *discipline*. The revealing is what you cannot hide; the training is what you can, by grace, be formed by. The Good Treasure, retrain the eye.

---

**In short:** the text says treasure *reveals* the heart in verse 21 (a statement of fact about where your heart already is) and *trains* the heart in verses 19–20 (an imperative about what to do with your hands). Possession directs attention; attention, by repeated habit, constitutes love; love makes the possession *yours* in a way that cannot be undone — and the whole triangle is legible to others. Which is why Christ's final word on it is not a warning about money but a promise: seek first the kingdom, and you will find that your treasure, and your heart, and your Master are all in the same place.
