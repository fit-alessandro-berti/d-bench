The move from "cease" to "learn" shows that the prophet does not think avoiding evil is the whole of righteousness. Ceasing is the threshold. Learning is the path.

**The movement itself.** The first four imperatives are negative or cleansing: wash, put away, cease. The next two are positive and educational: learn to do well, seek judgment. The sequence warns against a particular self-deception. A person can stop committing visible wrongs and still be practically useless to the people who most need help. Isaiah has just condemned Judah's worship (1:10–15): abundant sacrifices, prayers, and festivals offered by people whose hands are "full of blood." Stopping the bloodshed is necessary, but an empty space where wrongdoing used to be is not yet justice. Restraint that produces no works toward the vulnerable is just a quieter form of the same indifference.

**Why "learn."** The Hebrew verb *lamad* is the word for education and habituation, as in learning a craft or learning Torah. It implies that doing good is a skill: it requires attention, practice, correction, and growth. Nobody learns to do well by accident or by good intentions alone. This resembles Aristotle's point that virtue is formed through habituation, and the prophetic view is similar: goodness has to be cultivated and trained, not just assumed.

**The concrete content.** The examples are specific: seek judgment, relieve or rescue the oppressed (some translations read "correct the oppressor"), defend the fatherless, plead for the widow. These are people without a patron, the ones an ancient legal and economic system most easily ignored. The text suggests that love and justice are not abstractions. They are oriented toward the particular person who cannot repay, which is why justice is often described in terms of the orphan and the widow throughout Scripture.

**Restraint or practiced love?** The text points to the second, but not in a way that dismisses restraint. Three observations:

1. Restraint is necessary, but it is not sufficient. The command to "cease" is a real moral demand. It is just not the final one.
2. Goodness is a practiced form of love and justice, meaning it becomes a trained disposition: seeking, learning, attending to need, repeatedly choosing what helps. Love that is never practiced tends to remain sentiment.
3. The sequence begins with washing and ends with an invitation: "Come now, and let us reason together" (1:18). The learning is not self-generated moral heroism. It follows from having been made clean and taking up a new relationship with God and neighbor. So goodness is both received and practiced: God cleanses, and the cleansed person is then trained toward mercy and justice.

**Summary.** Isaiah suggests that goodness is more than the absence of wrongdoing. It is a learned way of attending to others, especially those who are weakest, in which justice and love take concrete form. Ceasing evil clears the ground; learning to do good is what gets built on it. Micah 6:8 expresses a similar vision: "to do justly, to love mercy, and to walk humbly with your God."
