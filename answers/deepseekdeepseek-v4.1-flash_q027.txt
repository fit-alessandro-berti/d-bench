The Pharisee is not condemned primarily for **false doctrine**. His words are not formally heretical: fasting and tithing were good practices, and “I thank thee” can be genuine. What condemns him is the **heart-orientation** Jesus names in v. 9: he “trusted in himself that he was righteous, and despised others.”

So the condemning thing is a syndrome:

- **Pride/self-trust** is the root: he stands before God on the basis of his own record.
- **Comparison** is the method: “I am not as other men are… or even as this publican.”
- **Contempt** is the fruit: he despises the tax collector.
- **Use of virtue as self-display** is the speech-form: his fasting and tithing become badges of superiority rather than gifts received or offerings given to God in humility.
- If “false doctrine” means a false practical theology—that God grades by comparative merit and that he has no need of mercy—then yes, that is involved. But the parable’s emphasis is not doctrinal error; it is self-righteousness and contempt.

The tax collector’s posture is treated as spiritually truer because it matches reality. He stands afar off, will not lift his eyes, smites his breast, and says, “God be merciful to me a sinner.” That is not a posture that earns justification; it is an embodied confession. It says: I have no claim, no record to plead, no superiority. I need mercy. Before a holy God, that is the truth about the human condition.

The Pharisee’s posture says: “I am fine; he is not.” That is false. The tax collector’s posture says: “I am a sinner; God is merciful.” That is true. So Jesus says the tax collector went home **justified**—declared righteous by God—not because being a tax collector or having bad posture saves, but because God justifies the humble who cast themselves on his mercy. The Pharisee trusted in himself and went home unjustified; the tax collector trusted in mercy and received justification as gift.
