Bitterness and forgiveness stand so directly against each other because they represent **opposing ways of carrying an injury into a relationship**. Bitterness keeps the injury active as a source of hostility; forgiveness refuses to let it govern how one treats the other person.

But Paul’s contrast is broader than a single feeling versus a single decision.

### Bitterness is a feeling that can become a posture

The Greek word translated “bitterness,” *pikria*, evokes something bitter or acrid. Applied to relationships, it suggests resentment, an inward sourness toward another person.

Paul is not giving a precise psychological definition, so your categories overlap:

- **As a feeling:** bitterness can begin in genuine hurt, disappointment, or anger.
- **As a habit:** resentment can be repeatedly rehearsed until it becomes one’s usual way of interpreting another person.
- **As a posture:** it can harden into a settled readiness to accuse, retaliate, or withhold kindness.

Importantly, the surrounding passage does not equate every experience of anger with sin. Earlier Paul says, “Be ye angry, and sin not” (4:26). His concern is not simply that someone feels wounded, but that anger is allowed to settle in and shape conduct.

### The list shows bitterness becoming social

Verse 31 moves through bitterness, wrath, anger, clamour, evil speaking, and malice. We should not force this into a strict sequence, as though Paul were describing inevitable stages. Nevertheless, the list encompasses both **inner hostility and its outward expression**: resentment, angry outbursts, shouting, damaging speech, and ill will.

That makes your word “contagious” insightful—with a qualification: Paul does not explicitly call bitterness contagious. But he is addressing a community, and these behaviours can spread hostility through it. One person’s resentment becomes another person’s wound; accusations invite counteraccusations.

The wider context concerns truthful speech, words that build others up, and life together as members of one body. Bitterness is therefore not merely a private burden. It can become a communal atmosphere.

### Forgiveness interrupts that pattern

Verse 32 does not offer forgiveness alone. It gives a cluster of alternatives:

> “kind … tenderhearted … forgiving”

These answer hostility with a different relational disposition: benevolence rather than malice, compassion rather than hardness, forgiveness rather than the continued holding of an offence against someone.

The verb translated “forgiving,” *charizomai*, is related to the language of grace: it describes graciously granting forgiveness. And its pattern is “even as God for Christ’s sake hath forgiven you”—or, more literally, **as God in Christ forgave you**. The appeal is not that the injury was insignificant, but that the believers’ own life together rests on forgiveness they have received.

So the passage treats bitterness most fully as **resentment that can become a settled, socially destructive disposition**. Forgiveness opposes it not by denying pain or pretending wrongdoing did not happen, but by refusing to make that wrongdoing the permanent organizing principle of the relationship. That need not mean immediate restored trust or the removal of appropriate boundaries.
