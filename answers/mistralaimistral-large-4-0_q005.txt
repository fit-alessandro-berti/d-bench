Enemy-love is difficult to take seriously because it collides with the architecture of human moral intuition. We are wired for reciprocity, for boundaries that protect, for justice that balances the scales. The command to love those who curse, hate, and persecute us feels not just impractical but morally disorienting—like being asked to abandon the very instincts that keep us safe. It threatens our tribal loyalties, our sense of righteous anger, and our conviction that love must be earned.

Yet this is precisely why it cannot be reduced to sentimentality. Sentimental love is passive, warm, and selective; it feels good because it costs little. The love Jesus commands is active, costly, and indiscriminate. It is *agape*—volitional goodwill that persists even when affection has evaporated. To bless those who curse you is not to feel nice about them; it is to refuse to let their hatred define the shape of your moral life. That requires a discipline that sentimentality cannot supply.

Nor is it merely strategic—though it does break cycles of violence and disarm hostility. If enemy-love were only a tactic for winning friends or converting foes, it would collapse the moment it failed. But Jesus grounds the command not in effectiveness but in ontology: *“That ye may be the children of your Father.”* The imperative is to become like God, who makes the sun rise on the evil and the good. This is imitation of divine character, not manipulation of outcomes. The Father’s indiscriminate provision of sun and rain is not a strategy for conversion; it is simply what God *is*—beneficent without condition.

Here lies the deeper moral strength. Worldly strength is the capacity to dominate, to retaliate, to protect the self at all costs. The strength Jesus commends is the capacity to remain unbroken by hatred, to refuse the dehumanization of the enemy, and to act from a center of gravity that the enemy’s violence cannot touch. It is the strength of the crucified—not weakness masquerading as virtue, but the power to absorb evil without transmitting it. To love your enemy is to claim a freedom that your enemy cannot revoke: the freedom not to be their mirror.

The difficulty, then, is that this love requires a kind of death. It asks us to die to the self that demands vindication, to the ego that defines itself against opposition, to the illusion that our moral worth depends on the defeat of our foes. It is hard to take seriously because it exposes how much of our “morality” is actually self-protection dressed in righteousness.

But precisely because it is so demanding, it is the only thing that can transform the world. Sentimental niceness changes nothing. Strategic kindness manipulates. But enemy-love—grounded in the imitation of a Father who sends rain on the unjust—creates the possibility of a new humanity, one that is finally capable of being the children of God.
