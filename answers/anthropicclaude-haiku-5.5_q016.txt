I'd say his deepest problem is that he has misunderstood his relationship with his father. He thinks of himself as a hired worker, not a son, and his obedience was a ledger of wages owed rather than love given. Anger, envy, wounded fairness, pride in obedience, and discomfort with mercy all grow out of that root.

The text supports this in several ways:

- **He describes his life as service.** "Lo, these many years do I serve thee" uses the language of a slave or hired hand. He says nothing about enjoying his father.
- **He expects payment.** "Thou never gavest me a kid" treats the household as an account in which he should have been paid. A young goat was the cheapest celebration, and he still feels cheated of it.
- **He would celebrate without the father.** He wants a feast "with my friends," not with his father. His idea of joy is separate from the family.
- **He disowns his brother.** He says "this thy son," not "my brother." The father corrects him: "this thy brother."
- **He exaggerates the sin.** "Devoured thy living with harlots" adds details the story never gave. He needs his brother to be as bad as possible.
- **The father has already given him everything.** "Son, thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine." The elder brother has been living in the inheritance without recognizing it. His presence in the house was already the gift he claimed to be denied.

So the anger is real, but it is a symptom. Mercy to the younger son exposes the ledger. If your standing depends on being owed, then grace to someone who didn't earn it looks like theft. And if your obedience was partly a way of securing the father's favor, then the father's generosity cancels your advantage. That is why the feast offends him more than the sin does.

Jesus tells this parable to people who grumble that he "receives sinners and eats with them" (Luke 15:2), so the elder brother is a mirror for the religious listeners. The story also ends without telling us whether he goes in. The father's last words are an appeal, not a verdict. The question is left open for the hearer.

The fix the parable points toward is not simply to feel less angry. It is to see that the father's joy was always available to him, and that the brother's return is good news rather than a loss to him.
