In Mark 8:34–35, Jesus is not telling us to hate ourselves. He is naming the deeper problem: the “self” we are naturally centered on is a false self—a self that tries to be its own center, its own Lord, its own source of safety and meaning.

The word “deny” here means something like “disown” or “renounce”—the way Peter denied Jesus, turning away from him. So to deny oneself is not to destroy our God-given personhood; it is to dethrone the ego. It is to stop letting the small, self-protective, self-justifying “I” rule everything. It is to say, “I am not the center of the universe; Christ is.”

And then Jesus sharpens it into a paradox:

> “Whoever wants to save their life will lose it, but whoever loses their life for my sake and the gospel’s will save it.”

The Greek word for “life” here is *psychē*—the soul, the self, the whole person. Jesus is saying: the self that clutches itself will suffocate. The self that lets itself go for His sake begins, for the first time, to truly live.

## The self being denied is the ego-self, not the true self

In Christian tradition, this is often described as the difference between the “false self” and the “true self.” The false self is the identity we construct out of our achievements, possessions, reputations, defenses, desires, and fears. It is the self that constantly asks:

- What can I get?
- What will people think of me?
- How can I protect myself?
- How can I prove myself?

Jesus says: that self must be denied. Not because you are worthless, but because that self is a prison. It is full of anxiety, pride, shame, and self-obsession. “Taking up your cross” means accepting that this ego must die—willingly, daily, in order to follow Him.

This is not self-hatred. It is not the erasure of personality or dignity. It is the discovery of who you actually are, hidden with Christ in God. The self you lose is the self you were pretending to be. The self you find is the self God created and loves.

## Why it sounds liberating

To someone who has felt the crushing weight of trying to save themselves—trying to be good enough, impressive enough, safe enough—Jesus’s words are a massive relief.

He is saying: *You can stop carrying the burden of self-justification. You don’t have to protect your ego anymore. You don’t have to be your own savior. Follow Me, and I will give you a life you could not manufacture for yourself.*

Self-denial sounds liberating because the ego is exhausting. When you are always calculating, defending, comparing, and grasping, you are never free. Jesus offers a different way: lose yourself in love for Him and for the gospel, and you will find that the life you were afraid to lose is returned to you—transformed.

## Why it sounds offensive

To a culture that says “be true to yourself,” “follow your heart,” and “you are enough,” Jesus’s words are an affront.

He does not say, “Express yourself” or “unlock your potential.” He says, “Deny yourself and take up your cross.” That sounds like the opposite of everything modern people are taught about freedom. It sounds like self-negation, even self-destruction. It wounds our pride, because it tells us that the “self” we are so committed to is not trustworthy. It says we are not our own lords, and that our lives are not our own.

It can also sound dangerous when it is taken out of context. Sadly, these verses have been used to keep people in abusive situations: “You just need to deny yourself and carry your cross.” That is a betrayal of Jesus’s meaning. Jesus is not commanding people to accept any suffering that others impose on them. He is calling people to *voluntarily* follow Him—to take up *their own* cross, not to have someone else’s cross forced onto them. And the purpose is specified: *for my sake and the gospel’s*—not for the sake of an abuser, an institution, or an unjust system.

## The paradox at the center of the gospel

Jesus’s paradox is the shape of the gospel: the way to life is through death. The way to glory is through the cross. The way to be great is to become the servant of all. The way to be free is to surrender control. This will always be both deeply attractive and deeply off-putting.

It is attractive to those who realize that their “self” is not the answer—that their real problem is, in fact, their obsession with themselves. It is offensive to those who still believe that they can save themselves by managing their own image, reputation, and desires.

Jesus is saying: *If you cling to your life, you will lose it.* This is not a threat—it is a statement of how the world works. The ego never gets enough. The self-centered life collapses inward.

*But if you lose your life for My sake and the gospel’s, you will save it.* You will not lose yourself; for the first time, you will find yourself.
