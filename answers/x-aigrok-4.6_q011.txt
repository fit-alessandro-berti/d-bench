**The text moves from cessation of evil to active learning of good because biblical righteousness (and the prophetic call here) treats “not doing harm” as necessary but incomplete; goodness is a cultivated, practiced habit of justice and mercy.**

Isaiah 1 is a covenant lawsuit: God indicts Judah’s empty ritual (v. 11–15) while they ignore the vulnerable. The sequence is deliberate:

- First, negative: “put away the evil… cease to do evil.” Stop the injustices already in view (oppression, bloodshed, corrupt worship).
- Then, positive and formative: “Learn to do well” (*limdû hêṭēb*). The verb “learn” implies instruction, repetition, and skill—not a one-time decision. What follows specifies the curriculum: seek justice (*mišpāṭ*), relieve the oppressed, defend the fatherless, plead for the widow.

This is not mere restraint or “do no harm.” It is an apprenticeship in love-of-neighbor expressed as public justice. The same pattern appears elsewhere (e.g., Ps 34:14 “turn from evil *and* do good”; Amos 5:14–15; Micah 6:8). Goodness is therefore both moral *and* social, both internal conversion *and* concrete practice. Empty piety that never reaches the widow and orphan is precisely what the chapter condemns.

In short: the text presents goodness as a learned, active form of justice rather than the absence of vice.
