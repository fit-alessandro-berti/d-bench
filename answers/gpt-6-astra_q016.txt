The elder brother’s deepest problem is **that he understands life with his father as a relationship of earned reward rather than shared love—and therefore experiences mercy toward his brother as an injustice to himself.**

Anger, envy, wounded fairness, and pride in obedience are all present. But they converge in his inability to share his father’s joy.

### His words reveal the problem

- **“These many years do I serve thee.”** He describes his place primarily in terms of service rendered. His obedience is real work, but in this speech it becomes a claim upon his father.
- **“Neither transgressed I at any time thy commandment.”** He presents his record as the basis for what he deserves.
- **“Yet thou never gavest me a kid.”** His brother’s welcome exposes a grievance: *What has my faithfulness earned me?*
- **“This thy son.”** He distances himself from his brother. The father gently answers, **“this thy brother.”**

The issue is not simply that he wants wrongdoing taken seriously. The father does not call the younger son’s conduct good; he calls his former condition **“dead”** and **“lost.”** The celebration is not approval of the wrongdoing. It is joy over restoration.

### The father answers his deeper misunderstanding

**“Son, thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine.”**

The father does not dismiss his faithfulness or deny his belonging. He reminds him that the relationship and inheritance are already his. His brother’s restoration need not mean his own diminishment.

But the elder brother is measuring his father’s goodness comparatively: *If you welcome him like this, what was the point of my obedience?* That question reveals how readily obedience can become leverage rather than love.

So **“discomfort with mercy itself” comes closest, with a qualification**: he resents mercy when it appears to undermine his claim to superior treatment. His problem is not obedience, but an understanding of obedience that leaves him unable to rejoice when the undeserving are welcomed.

Luke frames the parable with complaints that Jesus receives sinners (15:1–2). The elder brother embodies that complaint—and the father comes out to entreat him too.

**The younger son had been lost away from home; the elder risks remaining alienated while at home.** The ending leaves him—and the hearer—with the question: *Will you enter a joy you cannot control by deciding who deserves it?*
