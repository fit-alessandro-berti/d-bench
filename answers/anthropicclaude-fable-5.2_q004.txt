## The trust being asked for

The Hebrew images are concrete. To trust (*batach*) is to rest securely on something, and to lean (*sha'an*) is to put your weight on it, as on a staff. The verse does not forbid having or using understanding. It forbids making your own understanding the thing that bears your full weight.

Two details sharpen this:

- **"With all thine heart."** In Hebrew thought the heart (*leb*) is the seat of thinking and willing, not just feeling. Wholehearted trust engages the mind rather than bypassing it.
- **"Acknowledge him"** is literally "know him" (*yada*). The positive command is itself an act of knowing.

So the trust is comprehensive reliance on God ("all thy ways"), in which reason serves as an instrument, not a foundation.

## Why this cannot be anti-intellectualism

The word for "understanding" here (*binah*) is the same thing Proverbs elsewhere commands the reader to pursue: "Get wisdom, get understanding" (4:5), and "Happy is the man... that getteth understanding" (3:13, just eight verses later). The whole book is a curriculum in thinking well, so it cannot be condemning thought.

The key qualifier is "thine own": understanding treated as self-sufficient and closed to correction. Verse 7 gives the parallel: "Be not wise in thine own eyes." The contrast is between understanding received as a gift (2:6) and understanding held as an autonomous possession.

## The distinction

**Humble self-distrust** doubts my sufficiency, not my faculties. It recognizes that I am finite and cannot see where paths end, and that I am prone to self-deception: "There is a way which seemeth right unto a man, but the end thereof are the ways of death" (14:12). Its fruit is more inquiry: seeking counsel (15:22), welcoming reproof, and thinking hard while holding conclusions with an open hand.

**Anti-intellectualism** distrusts thinking itself, and it is often self-trust in disguise. The person who refuses study, counsel, or evidence "because I just trust God" is usually leaning on their own impressions, feelings, or unexamined interpretations. That is still leaning on one's own understanding, only an untested one. Proverbs is blunt about this posture: "He that hateth reproof is brutish" (12:1), and "He that trusteth in his own heart is a fool" (28:26).

## A practical test

Does my "trust" make me more teachable or less? Humble self-distrust says, "I may be wrong, so I will listen, learn, and submit my conclusions to God." Anti-intellectualism says, "I need not think, so no one can correct me." The first is the fear of the Lord, which Proverbs calls the beginning of knowledge (1:7). The second is being wise in one's own eyes, under a pious name.
