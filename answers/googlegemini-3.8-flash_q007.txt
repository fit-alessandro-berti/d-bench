Luke 6:37 is one of the most frequently quoted—and most consistently misunderstood—verses in the New Testament. It is often cited as a charter for absolute moral relativism, treated as if Jesus were saying, *“Never evaluate anyone’s actions; never call evil evil; pretend moral differences do not exist.”* 

However, taking the verse this way contradicts both the broader biblical witness and basic human reason. Jesus himself routinely made profound moral judgments: he warned against "false prophets," rebuked the hypocrisy of the religious elite, and explicitly commanded elsewhere, *“Do not judge by appearances, but judge with righteous judgment”* (John 7:24). 

The distinction Jesus makes in Luke 6:37 is not between **having moral standards versus having none**; it is the distinction between **sober discernment** and **the appetite to condemn**.

---

### 1. The Distinction in Terms: Discernment vs. Condemnation

To understand the verse, one must contrast the posture of discernment with the spirit of condemnation:

*   **Discernment (Moral Reality):** Discernment is the capacity to tell the difference between truth and falsehood, good and evil, health and toxicity. It is an act of clarity. Just as a physician must identify a tumor to treat it, a morally mature person must recognize sin, injustice, or destructive behavior. Discernment seeks to understand reality as it is so that goodness can be protected and healing can occur.
*   **Condemnation (The Usurpation of God):** In Greek, the words used carry legal weight. *Krinō* (judge) can mean to pass a final sentence, and *katadikazō* (condemn) means to pronounce guilt with the intent to punish or write someone off. Condemnation does not merely identify a wrong deed; it passes an ultimate, irrevocable verdict on the *worth and soul of the doer*. It presumes to sit on the divine throne.

### 2. The Anatomy of the "Appetite to Condemn"

Why does Jesus link "judging" with "condemning"? Because fallen human nature possesses a dark, voracious appetite to condemn others. This appetite is characterized by several traits:

*   **Cheap Righteousness:** Condemning others provides an emotional shortcut to feeling virtuous. If I can prove how corrupt, foolish, or evil *they* are, I automatically feel justified by comparison.
*   **A Weaponized Standard:** The appetite to condemn applies a strict, unforgiving standard to the neighbor while quietly using a lenient, context-sensitive standard for oneself. (This is why Jesus follows this warning with the metaphor of having a log in one’s own eye while picking at a speck in a brother’s eye).
*   **Vindictiveness over Restoration:** The spirit of condemnation delights in exposure, ostracization, and punishment. It does not grieve over sin; it relishes catching someone in it.

### 3. Why Forgiveness Requires Moral Judgment

The second half of Luke 6:37 reveals the flaw in the "moral judgments don't matter" interpretation: 
> *“Forgive, and ye shall be forgiven.”*

Forgiveness is impossible without moral judgment. If moral distinctions are an illusion, there is nothing to forgive. You do not "forgive" an earthquake or the weather; you only forgive a moral agent who has committed an actual wrong. 

Therefore, Jesus is not calling for moral blindness. He assumes that you *will* perceive that you have been wronged, or that someone has violated the good. But instead of letting that moral perception turn into a hunger to destroy the offender (condemnation), you are commanded to release the debt (forgiveness).

### 4. A Difference in Posture, Not in Standards

| Dimension | Discernment | The Appetite to Condemn |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Primary Motivation** | Love, protection, and restoration | Self-elevation, vindication, and malice |
| **Self-Awareness** | "I am a fellow sinner capable of the same fall." | "I am the standard of righteousness." |
| **The Instrument** | A surgeon's scalpel (cuts to heal) | An executioner's axe (cuts to destroy) |
| **The Verdict** | Provisional; leaves the soul to God | Final; writes the person off |

### Summary

Luke 6:37 does not ask us to surrender our minds or pretend that virtue and vice are identical. Rather, it demands that we surrender our **malice**. 

Discernment names the poison so that the patient can be saved; the appetite to condemn names the poison so that the patient can be executed. Jesus is calling his followers to retain the moral clarity of the former while utterly crucifying the latter.
