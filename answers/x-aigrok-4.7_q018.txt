They are set against each other because Paul is not offering two separate pieces of advice. He is describing one exchange: what must leave the community, and what must take its place.

Ephesians 4:25–32 is a put-off / put-on passage. Speech, anger, work, and relationships are all being reordered around the new life in Christ. Verse 31 names a cluster to be removed; verse 32 names the posture that replaces it. The link is not accidental. Bitterness is the settled refusal to release an injury. Forgiveness is the act of releasing it. Kindness and tenderheartedness are what that release looks like in ordinary dealings. The ground is not “try harder to feel better.” It is “even as God in Christ forgave you.” People who have been released are told to stop living as people who keep score.

The list itself shows how Paul is treating bitterness. He does not isolate it as a private mood.

- **Bitterness** (*pikria*) is named first: a sour, settled disposition, not a passing flare.
- **Wrath and anger** are the heat that disposition produces.
- **Clamor and evil speaking** are that heat made public—shouting, slander, speech that tears someone down.
- **Malice** is the ill-will underneath the whole sequence.

So the text is not mainly classifying an emotion. It is tracing a path from an inner posture, through habit and speech, into damage done to other people. Fleeting anger is already addressed just before this (4:26). What remains in 4:31 is what anger becomes when it is kept, nursed, and then spoken.

That is why “contagious” fits, even though Paul does not use the word. The verbs are plural. The harm runs through “one another.” Corrupt talk has already been contrasted with speech that builds up (4:29), and these vices are said to grieve the Holy Spirit (4:30). Bitterness becomes common property once it is clamored and spoken. A related image appears in Hebrews 12:15: a root of bitterness that springs up and defiles many. Ephesians makes the same communal point by pairing the inner sourness with public speech and then with mutual forgiveness.

It is also treated as something people can renounce. “Let all bitterness… be put away” is a command, not a description of a feeling that will pass if left alone. Paul does not pretend feelings are switched off by decision. He treats the posture, the speech, and the malice as accountable. They can be laid down because a stronger fact is already in place: God has forgiven them in Christ. Forgiveness here is not denial that the injury happened. It is the refusal to let injury harden into a way of life toward one another.

So the text holds several things at once. Bitterness begins as a feeling, settles into a posture, hardens by habit, and spreads by speech. Forgiveness stands directly opposite it because it cuts that sequence off at the root and replaces it with the way God has already dealt with them.
