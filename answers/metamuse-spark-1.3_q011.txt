That movement — from **"cease to do evil"** to **"learn to do well"** — is the whole point.

In Hebrew it's even more striking:

> *hadlu hare'a* — cease evil
> *limdu hetev* — learn good

The second is not automatic. You don't just stop one and drift into the other. Goodness has to be *learned*.

That tells you three things about how Isaiah — and the Bible as a whole — understands goodness:

**1. Repentance has two halves: put off and put on.**

This is a classic biblical pattern. Psalm 34:14: "Depart from evil, and do good." Psalm 37:27, Amos 5:14-15 say the same.

Why? Because Israel's problem in Isaiah 1 wasn't just that they *did* evil, it's that they *neglected* good while covering it with religion. Verses 11-15: God is sick of their sacrifices, feasts, and prayers because "your hands are full of blood."

To merely "cease" would leave a vacuum. True washing — verse 16: "Wash you, make you clean; put away the evil of your doings" — must become visible in a new way of living. Stopping the harm is necessary, but not sufficient.

**2. Goodness is not mere restraint, it's an apprenticeship.**

"Learn" implies goodness isn't instinctive, especially for people trained in injustice. It's a skill, a craft, a habit to be practiced until it becomes character.

You don't *learn* restraint in the abstract. You learn piano by playing, a trade by apprenticing, a language by speaking. Isaiah says justice is like that: *dirshu* — seek, pursue, study it.

That is why mere non-harm is too thin for the prophets. Not killing the widow and orphan is not enough. God commands Israel to actively move toward them.

**3. Learned goodness looks like concrete justice.**

Isaiah immediately defines "do well":

> seek judgment [*mishpat*], relieve the oppressed, judge the fatherless, plead for the widow.

All four are verbs of advocacy in a court and society where those people had no patron, no land, no voice:

* **seek justice** — pursue right order, not just avoid wrong
* **correct oppression** — set right the oppressor / lift up the crushed
* **vindicate the orphan** — defend the fatherless in judgment
* **plead for the widow** — be her legal advocate

In other words, goodness is practiced love and justice for the vulnerable. It's outward, costly, and specific. This becomes the biblical definition of pure religion — see James 1:27, which is almost quoting Isaiah: "visit the fatherless and widows in their affliction."

Isaiah 1:16-17 therefore reframes holiness. God is not saying, "Be less bad and I'll accept your worship." He is saying, "Let your worship *become* justice. Unlearn exploitation, and apprentice yourselves to mercy until doing good becomes who you are."
