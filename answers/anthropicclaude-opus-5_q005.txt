## Why it resists belief

Part of the difficulty is that the command sounds like a category error. Love is normally *responsive* — it tracks something lovable in its object. An enemy is defined precisely as one who has forfeited that claim. So the command seems either to demand a feeling that cannot be produced on command, or to demand that we pretend a person is other than he is.

But there is a sharper objection. Enemy-love can look like the abolition of moral seriousness. If I bless the one who curses me, have I not made cursing and blessing equivalent? Indignation is not merely a lapse; it's a form of perception. To be unable to resent cruelty is arguably to have stopped noticing that it is cruelty. This is why Nietzsche read the commandment as a symptom rather than an ideal: the weak, unable to retaliate, rebrand their impotence as virtue and call it love. And there is a version of the suspicion that doesn't require Nietzsche's cynicism — one need only recall how often these verses have been pressed on the oppressed by those who benefited from their patience.

Then there's the third-party problem, which the passage does not address at all. The Sermon speaks in the second person singular about *your* enemy, *your* cheek, *your* cloak. Whether the ethic scales from personal injury to the protection of others is a genuine question, not a quibble. Reinhold Niebuhr's objection retains its force: to absorb an injury oneself may be sublime; to absorb it on behalf of someone else, who did not consent, may be a way of purchasing one's own moral purity with another's safety.

## Sentimental?

The text is oddly resistant to a sentimental reading. Notice that every imperative is a verb of *doing*: bless, do good, pray. No emotion is commanded. Whatever *agapē* means here, it is not warmth of feeling; it is something closer to willed regard — treating a person as though his good mattered, whether or not you can feel that it does.

The grounding clause is stranger still. Why do this? Not "because deep down your enemy is good," and not "because you will feel better." Because "he maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good." The model offered is meteorological. Sunlight and rain are indifferent, indiscriminate, unearned. There is nothing tender about them. If anything, the analogy is faintly disquieting: it grounds the highest human ethic in a divine refusal to sort people by desert. Some readers find that gracious; others find it morally alarming, since a God who sends rain equally on the just and unjust looks a great deal like a universe that doesn't care. The verse invites both readings and doesn't defend itself against the second.

## Strategic?

There is a strategic tradition, and it isn't foolish. Paul's quotation of Proverbs — "in so doing thou shalt heap coals of fire on his head" — has been read as shaming the enemy into repentance (though some commentators note that in the ancient Near East carrying coals meant rekindling a neighbor's dead hearth, an image of restored relationship rather than humiliation). King and Gandhi argued explicitly that nonviolence works: it refuses the escalation cycle, it splits the opponent's coalition, it converts. Even the iterated-game literature finds that forgiving strategies outperform relentlessly punitive ones.

But the strategic reading corrodes itself. If I love my enemy because it will disarm him, I am still operating inside the economy of retaliation — just playing a longer game, with love as the instrument. And the passage's own logic blocks this exit. The sun rising on the evil does not reform the evil. It just rises. Efficacy is not the stated reason; resemblance is.

## Or something else

The third option — moral strength — comes closest, provided we don't hear "strength" as heroic self-mastery. It isn't Stoic *apatheia*, the achievement of not being wounded. The Sermon assumes you *are* wounded; it says "them that persecute you," not "them that mildly irritate you."

The strength in question is closer to this: refusing to let your enemy determine what kind of person you become. Hatred is a form of dependence. It hands the enemy authorship of your inner life; you organize yourself around him, and he sets the terms. The person who can act toward an adversary out of some source other than the adversary's own conduct has a kind of autonomy that retaliation never achieves. Verse 45 names this as likeness — "that ye may be the children of your Father" — and verse 48 completes it with *teleios*, usually rendered "perfect" but carrying the sense of whole, undivided, complete. The relevant completeness is impartiality: goodness that does not have two settings depending on whom it is addressing.

The verse just before this one — "thou shalt love thy neighbour, and hate thine enemy" — quotes a command that appears nowhere in the Hebrew Bible, though something very like it turns up in the Qumran Community Rule: love the sons of light, hate the sons of darkness. Whether or not that's the target, the polemic is clear. The teaching attacks *bounded* love, love with an in-group perimeter. Its real objection is not to hatred as an emotion but to the sorting mechanism that makes some people eligible for decency and others not.

## What remains hard

Two things, honestly.

First, the ethic seems to require either a theological foundation or something functionally like one. If the argument is "be indiscriminate because God is," the argument doesn't obviously survive translation into secular terms. Something *can* survive — a Kantian version, perhaps, on which each person's claim on you is independent of their conduct toward you. But it is thinner, and it lacks the passage's confidence that indiscriminate goodness is the deep grain of things rather than a noble stance against the grain.

Second, no reading fully resolves the tension between forgiveness and justice. The most defensible position is probably that enemy-love governs the *disposition* while leaving room for resistance in the *act* — that one may oppose, restrain, even fight a person whose destruction one does not desire. That distinction is coherent. Whether it is livable, under pressure, in real time, is exactly what the commandment refuses to make easy.
