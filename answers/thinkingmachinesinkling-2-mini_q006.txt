My reading—not the only one—is that Mark is not talking about “deny” as in “abolish your identity” or “hate yourself.” It means refuse the self that tries to preserve itself by staying separate, admired, protected, and in control. In the gospel horizon, “life” is not merely personal safety or self-esteem; it is flourishing in the kingdom, under the gospel. So to “deny self,” “take up the cross,” and “follow” Jesus is to re-order one’s life away from self-preservation toward allegiance, service, and costly belonging.

That is why it can sound liberating: it unhooks identity from approval and survival, frees people from the anxiety of being “secure,” turns suffering from a private defeat into a public orientation, and gives meaning to risk rather than comfort. For many marginalized or oppressed people, it reads less like self-humiliation and more like refusal to let a dominant “strong self” define worth.

It can sound offensive for the same reasons, or for the flip side. Because the language of “deny,” “take up,” and “lose life” is severe, it can be heard as shame, as merit-by-suffering, or as a demand that certain bodies—disabled, poor, traumatized, gay, gender-diverse, neurodivergent—must “kill” parts of themselves to become valuable. It can become easy to justify martyrdom, austere obedience, purity rules, or punishing one’s own desires. And it is dangerous when applied to injustice by people who themselves benefit from it.

The sharpest tension is that Mark’s promise is paradoxical: the one who “saves” his own life loses it, while the one who loses it for Jesus and the gospel saves it. That is radical, not because it asks us to be empty, but because it says a self protected only by its own strength is not finally living. The best reading is not “erase yourself,” but “refuse to make your self-security the ground of your identity.” The stifling version comes when someone turns that paradox into a test of courage: only the person willing to suffer more is most saved. The liberating version asks what the self is for—not for self-defense or applause, but for service, community, and conversion.
