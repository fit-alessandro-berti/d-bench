## The two words

The verse is a Hebrew parallelism, and the pairing is deliberate:

> let the **wicked** forsake his **way** (*derek*)
> and the **man of iniquity** his **thoughts** (*maḥshĕvōt*)

*Derek* is a road — the trodden path, the direction a life is actually traveling, the settled course of conduct. *Maḥshāvāh* is not idle mental drift but design, scheme, device: the interior blueprint from which the road is built. It is the word used for the plans of craftsmen and the plots of conspirators. Sin, in this anthropology, has an architecture. It is drafted before it is walked.

So the two terms are not two separate sins to be renounced but one life taken from its two ends: the visible trajectory and the hidden workshop. "Every imagination of the thoughts of his heart" (Gen. 6:5) is the same construction — the inner forming that issues in an outer world. Psalm 139 asks God to know the *thoughts* and to search for any wicked *way*, and to lead into the way everlasting. The prophet is saying: repentance must reach as far down as sin does.

Note also the possessive: *his* way, *his* thoughts. Isaiah has already defined the human condition this way — "we have turned every one to his own way" (53:6). What must be forsaken is not merely bad behavior but self-authorship: the road I laid out, the scheme I devised, the life as my own project.

## Why both are named

Because each can counterfeit the other.

A man can change his *way* while keeping his *thoughts*. He stops the act but nurses the appetite; he abandons the theft but keeps the coveting, quits the adultery but retains the rehearsed fantasy, restrains the tongue and preserves the contempt. The road has been rerouted; the destination is unchanged. This is the whitewashed tomb, and Christ's extension of the law to anger and lust is precisely a commentary on this half-repentance.

A man can also renounce his *thoughts* in the sense of feeling badly about them while his *way* goes on untouched — remorse as a substitute for turning. Isaiah's verb is *ʿazav*, to leave behind, abandon, walk away from; and its counterpart is *shuv*, return. Something is actually departed from and something is actually walked toward. Sentiment that leaves the road intact is not what the prophet is asking for.

And the return is *unto the LORD… to our God*. Repentance is not migration to a better standard but to a Person. That is why it can be described as both a forsaking and a coming home.

One pastoral caution follows from the meaning of *maḥshāvāh*: this is not a command to achieve a mind empty of stray or intrusive thoughts, which no one can do and which would turn the verse into an engine of despair. What is to be abandoned is the governing design — the plans one consents to, harbors, and builds a life around.

## The hinge in verses 8–9

The decisive clue is what comes next, and it uses the *same two words*:

> "For my **thoughts** are not your **thoughts**, neither are your **ways** my **ways**… as the heavens are higher than the earth, so are my ways higher than your ways, and my thoughts than your thoughts."

The reason to forsake your way and your thoughts is not simply that they are wicked. It is that they are *small*. Even our calculations about repentance — how much is forgivable, how far mercy can plausibly stretch, what a person like me may reasonably hope for — are drawn from our own *maḥshĕvōt*. The sinner's last stronghold is his estimate of God.

So the forsaking is an exchange, not a demolition. You cannot lay hold of a way "higher than the earth" while gripping your own. The emptying of the hands in verse 7 is what makes the free purchase of verses 1–3 possible.

## What this implies about mercy

Two verbs answer the two forsakings: "he will have mercy" (*yĕraḥēm*, from *reḥem*, the womb — compassion of the deepest, most involuntary kind) and "he will abundantly pardon" — literally *he will multiply to pardon*.

If sin were only a matter of outward acts, an amnesty might suffice: the ledger wiped, the record cleared. But because sin reaches into imagination, mercy must reach there too — it must be creative rather than merely forensic. This is why the prophetic promise of forgiveness keeps sliding into the promise of a new heart (Jer. 31; Ezek. 36), and why this chapter ends not with a rebuke but with a changed landscape: instead of the thorn, the fir tree; instead of the brier, the myrtle. God's answer to forsaken thoughts is not a repaired old self but a new creation.

The mercy is therefore *possible* in exactly the place where it seems least possible. The man who knows his thoughts — who has seen the interior and knows what no court could ever prosecute — is the man most likely to conclude he is beyond pardon. To him the text says: your thoughts are not his thoughts. The abundance of the forgiveness is measured against the abundance of what you found in there.

## The urgency

All of this sits under "while he may be found… while he is near." Nearness is a gift and a season, not a standing condition we control. The chapter has already flung open a free invitation — come, buy without money. The summons to forsake way and thoughts is not the price of admission; it is what accepting the invitation looks like from the inside. Which is why the delay is dangerous: not because the offer will be withdrawn on a technicality, but because a man who keeps his own road long enough eventually stops believing there is any other.
