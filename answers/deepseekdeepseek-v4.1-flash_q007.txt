Luke 6:37 is not a ban on all moral evaluation. It is a warning against a particular *spirit* and *role*: the self-appointed, merciless, final condemner. Saying “moral judgments never matter” is moral indifference or relativism. Jesus is saying something much more specific: **do not stand in God’s place as the final judge of another person’s soul, and do not deal with others in a merciless, condemnatory way.**

In fact, the command itself assumes that moral judgments *do* matter. If nothing were truly wrong, there would be nothing to forgive. “Forgive, and ye shall be forgiven” presupposes real guilt, real injury, real moral order. Jesus is not saying, “Nothing is good or evil.” He is saying, “You are not the ultimate judge; God is. Therefore judge with mercy.”

### The context makes this clear

Immediately after this, Jesus speaks of the beam in one’s own eye and the speck in a brother’s eye. He does not say, “Never help your brother with the speck.” He says, “First take the beam out of your own eye, and then you will see clearly to take the speck out of your brother’s eye” (Luke 6:42). So correction and discernment are still possible—but only after humility and self-examination.

Jesus elsewhere says, “Do not judge by appearances, but judge with right judgment” (John 7:24). He also says you will know false teachers by their fruits (Matt. 7:15–20). Paul tells the church to judge those inside the church (1 Cor. 5:12) and to restore sinners gently (Gal. 6:1). So Scripture does not call for moral silence. It calls for a certain kind of moral judgment.

### Discernment vs. the appetite to condemn

**Discernment** is the ability to distinguish truth from falsehood, good from evil, wisdom from folly, and harm from health. It is necessary for love. A parent must discern danger. A church must discern false teaching. A friend must discern harmful patterns. A victim must discern abuse. Discernment says, “This is wrong, and I will seek what is good.” It can name sin without dehumanizing the sinner.

**The appetite to condemn** is different. It is a desire for a verdict. It wants to label, shame, exclude, and finalize. It feeds on outrage and moral superiority. It often disguises itself as zeal for righteousness, but its fruit is self-righteousness, division, and delight in another’s fall. Condemnation says, “You are wrong, and I am glad. You are beneath me. You are beyond mercy.”

Here is the difference in practical terms:

| Discernment | Appetite to condemn |
|---|---|
| Seeks truth and the other’s good | Seeks a verdict and self-justification |
| Humble, self-examined | Superior, blind to its own beam |
| Focuses on actions, ideas, fruits | Totalizes the person’s identity and destiny |
| Patient, proportionate, open to correction | Quick, absolute, final |
| Speaks truth in love | Gossips, labels, mocks, relishes exposure |
| Aims at protection and restoration | Aims at punishment and humiliation |
| Can forgive while naming the wrong | Withholds mercy and enjoys condemnation |
| Leaves final judgment to God | Usurps God’s throne |

### Why moral judgments still matter

Moral judgments matter because without them there is no justice, no repentance, no forgiveness, and no love. Forgiveness is not pretending nothing happened. It is naming a real wrong and releasing the debt. Discernment is what lets us say, “That is harmful,” “That is false,” “That is sinful,” without saying, “You are worthless,” “You are damned,” or “I am righteous and you are not.”

Jesus’ harshest words were often aimed at the self-righteous condemners, not at the obvious sinners. In John 8, when a woman is caught in adultery, Jesus says, “Neither do I condemn you; go, and from now on sin no more.” He does not say adultery does not matter. He names the sin and calls her to change. But he refuses to play the role of final condemner. That is Luke 6:37 in action: no condemnation, but also no moral indifference.

So Luke 6:37 is not “moral judgments never matter.” It is “you are not God.” Moral judgments matter enough that God will judge. Therefore make them humbly, truthfully, mercifully, and provisionally. Leave the final verdict to God. Discernment is a scalpel for healing; the appetite to condemn is a sword for killing. The first can forgive. The second cannot.
