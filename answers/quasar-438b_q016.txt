The elder brother’s deepest problem is **envy rooted in wounded fairness**.

He says, in effect: *I obeyed you faithfully for years, and you never rewarded me; but the moment this other son returned—after squandering your living—you bestow the fatted calf on him.* That is not merely anger at a father’s command. Nor is it simply pride in obedience, because he openly cites his obedience as the very thing that should have earned reward. His grievance is that **obedience went unrewarded, while another’s return got rewarded**.

So the deepest structure is:

- **Surface emotion:** anger  
- **Moral complaint:** unfair preferential treatment  
- **Underlying passion:** envy  
- **Root concern:** wounded fairness—“I served thee faithfully; why dost thou favor him?”

His father’s answer is important too: *“thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine.”* That tries to dissolve the grievance by saying the elder brother already possesses everything. But the elder brother’s problem remains the perception that the younger brother received a special, visible token of favor that he himself never received.
