The posture Paul requires is the opposite of the courtroom and the pedestal.

> "Brethren, if a man be overtaken in a fault, ye which are spiritual, restore such an one in the spirit of meekness; considering thyself, lest thou also be tempted."

Three words do all the work: **restore, meekness, considering thyself.** Paul keeps them together on purpose because each one keeps the other two honest.

### 1. Who is correcting, and who is being corrected?

"If a man be *overtaken* in a fault" — *prolēmphthē* — is the picture of someone surprised, tripped up, caught by a sin rather than settled defiantly in it. A *paraptōma*, a lapse, a misstep. Paul is not talking first about excommunication of a hardened rebel, but about a brother who has fallen into the ditch.

"Ye which are spiritual" does not mean the clergy, the perfect, or the doctrinal police. In Galatians 5, the spiritual are simply those walking in the Spirit and bearing His fruit — love, gentleness, self-control — as opposed to walking in the flesh.

So the qualifier is moral, not official: Only someone living under the Spirit is fit to do this work, because only he will do it in the Spirit's way.

And the work is: *katartizete* — **restore**. It's a medical and artisan word: to set a broken bone, to mend a torn net, to equip and make whole again. The goal is stated before the manner. You are not to expose him, punish him, brand him, or throw him away. You are to make him whole.

That already defines the moral posture: fraternal, medicinal, redemptive. Correction here is not retribution. It's repair.

### 2. Why meekness is the mandatory manner

"...in the spirit of meekness" — *en pneumati prautētos*.

Meekness, *prautēs*, was just listed as fruit of the Spirit in Gal. 5:23. Paul is saying: correct with the same temper that the Spirit produces in you. No harshness, no raised voice of superiority, no pleasure in being right.

Why is that mandatory for restoration? Because harshness cannot restore.

Severity hardens the fallen person into defensiveness and shame, and it changes the corrector into a judge. Meekness acknowledges: this could be me, this *is* me apart from grace, I am approaching a fellow member of the same body, not a specimen.

In 2 Timothy 2:25 Paul says the same thing: "in meekness instructing those that oppose themselves." Meekness leaves room for the person to stand up again without being crushed under your virtue.

### 3. Why self-suspicion must be present

"...considering thyself, lest thou also be tempted" — *skopōn seauton* — keep a watchful, continuous eye on yourself.

This is the safeguard Paul bolts onto the whole command. While you look at his fault, keep looking at yourself. Why?

a) **Because you share the same flesh.** 1 Cor. 10:12: "let him that thinketh he standeth take heed lest he fall." The sin that overtook him can overtake you — either the same fault, or its twin.

b) **Because the act of correcting is itself a temptation.** The greatest danger at that moment is not his lust, his temper, his dishonesty — it's your pride, your harshness, your censorious spirit. You can fall while trying to lift. Jesus makes the same point in Matt. 7:3-5: the log in your own eye disqualifies your surgery on the speck until you deal with it.

Self-suspicion is not false modesty. It's theological realism. Galatians 6:3 follows immediately: "For if a man think himself to be something, when he is nothing, he deceiveth himself."

Without "considering thyself," spiritual becomes a title of superiority, and meekness becomes a technique. With it, you remember you are a patient helping another patient, not a physician who was never sick.

### Why the three cannot be separated

Paul binds them because they form one moral logic:

**Restoration gives the aim:** We are not out to win an argument, enforce a rule, or protect our reputation. We are out to get a brother back on his feet. That aim rules out both permissive indifference — "leave him in the ditch" — and legalist severity — "beat him in the ditch."

**Meekness gives the means:** You cannot mend with a hammer. Only gentleness persuades conscience, preserves dignity, and reflects Christ, who restores Peter after his denial not with scorn but with breakfast and "Feed my sheep."

**Self-suspicion gives the guard:** It protects the corrector from becoming what he condemns, and it protects the corrected from becoming a project for someone else's ego. It fulfils the very next verse: "Bear ye one another's burdens, and so fulfil the law of Christ." The law of Christ is the cross — bearing weight, not adding to it.

Take one away and the others rot:

Restoration without meekness becomes punishment.
Meekness without restoration becomes sentimentality that never tells the truth.
Either without self-examination becomes Pharisaism.

Together they create the posture Paul wants: **a humble, Spirit-led brother kneeling beside another fallen brother, gentle with his hands because he knows his own feet are unsteady.**
