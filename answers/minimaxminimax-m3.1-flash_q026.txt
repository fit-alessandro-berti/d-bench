**All three—but with a clear order of priority.** The passage most directly presents words as **symptoms and revelations of the heart’s moral condition**. Yet because speech is itself a moral activity, words are also **choices**. The heart is the treasure-house; the mouth is where its contents are “brought forth.”

## The heart as a moral treasury

The language is deliberately economic: **treasure**, **abundance**, **bringing forth**. The heart is the inward storehouse from which speech flows. “Heart” here means more than private feeling or unspoken thought; it includes a person’s loves, convictions, loyalties, desires, and settled moral commitments.

The preceding tree saying supplies the key: a tree is known by its fruit, and **fruit does not make the tree good; the tree produces the fruit** (v. 33). Jesus therefore argues *from* speech *to* the moral orientation behind it. Religious vocabulary does not constitute good fruit if it comes from an allegiance of hostility to God. In the surrounding context, “good” and “evil” speech include confession and acknowledgment of sin on one side, and blasphemy, accusation, and slander on the other—not merely pleasant versus harsh language.

## Words as symptoms

A person’s speech functions as an observable symptom of their moral state:

> “A good man … bringeth forth good things.”

Speech can reveal what would otherwise remain invisible: what a person loves, what they justify, and whom they ultimately serve. Jesus uses the Pharisees’ words as evidence of the corruption beneath their religious profession. Their scriptural knowledge and formal speech conceal rather than disclose a good heart.

But symptoms are not always conclusive. A person can speak well occasionally, conceal motives, or counterfeit goodness. Jesus’ diagnosis therefore rests on his knowledge of the heart, while the whole pattern of speech—not one isolated sentence—provides others with evidence.

## Words as choices

Speech is not simply a passive readout of what is already inside. **Saying things is doing something.** Confessing, promising, accusing, flattering, concealing, and repeating particular words are moral acts, and the surrounding passage treats them as accountable acts (vv. 31–32, 37).

Thus, a person’s words express the priorities that govern the heart: one chooses what to voice, endorse, and repeat. The passage primarily stresses the direction **heart → speech**, so it should not be read as claiming that good words manufacture a good heart. Nevertheless, the broader biblical moral picture holds that repeated speech also deepens the disposition that produced it. In that sense, words are not only symptoms of character; they participate in forming it.

## Words as revelations

Words also make the hidden treasury partially visible. They can expose hypocrisy, disclose allegiance, and bring concealed motives into the light of others’ scrutiny and divine judgment. That is the force of the surrounding conclusion:

> “For by your words I will justify you, and by your words you will be condemned” (v. 37).

Here, revelation is not simply human inference. Jesus reads the heart and uses speech as evidence of what is concealed within it. Human listeners may be deceived by polished words, but neither outward respectability nor eloquent speech can ultimately make the inner treasure something other than what it is before God.

## How the three fit together

The passage suggests a moral sequence:

**Heart’s hidden treasure → deliberate speech → observable fruit → disclosure in Christ’s judgment.**

- **Symptoms:** speech provides evidence of the heart’s condition.
- **Choices:** speech is an intentional expression—and, over time, a reinforcement—of that condition.
- **Revelations:** speech exposes what might otherwise remain hidden, especially before Christ and at judgment.

So “all three” is the best answer. The essential qualification is that **the heart remains primary**. Words do not create moral identity by themselves; they reveal it, enact it, and make it increasingly accountable. A reservoir determines what flows from the tap—but the person is still responsible for what the tap releases.
