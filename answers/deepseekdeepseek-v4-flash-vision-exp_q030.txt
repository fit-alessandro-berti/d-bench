In 1 Corinthians 13, Paul describes love as a way of being, not just a feeling. He gives us a portrait of *agape*—the self-giving love of God. It is striking that so much of the portrait is negative: love does not envy, does not boast, is not rude, does not insist on its own way, is not easily angered, and keeps no record of wrongs.

Why these negations? Because love is so easily counterfeited by love’s opposite. Pride, envy, irritability, and suspicion are all forms of self-flattery and self-protection. They are the ways we make the world revolve around us. Envy says, “I want what you have.” Pride says, “I deserve to be seen.” Irritability says, “I demand to be treated the way I want.” Suspicion says, “I will protect myself by assuming the worst.” But Paul says that love is the one thing that is finally free of that gravitational pull. It is not puffed up with itself, because it does not need to be. It does not seek its own, because it has found a better center: the good of the beloved.

The negatives, then, are not the whole picture. They clear away the debris of self-centeredness so that love can be what love actually is. And immediately after the negations, Paul speaks positively: love “bears all things, believes all things, hopes all things, endureth all things.” That is a very different vision of strength.

The world’s idea of strength is often about dominance, invulnerability, and never yielding. But Paul’s vision is the strength of the cross. It is the power to remain kind when wounded. It is the strength to be patient instead of demanding. It is the strength to forgive without keeping a ledger. It is the strength to trust and hope even when the evidence is uncertain, not because love is naive, but because love refuses to assume the worst and instead gives room for truth and grace to work.

“Thinketh no evil” does not mean love stops thinking or refuses to judge clearly. It means love does not keep accounts of wrongs; it does not entertain suspicion as a way of life. A suspicious heart is usually a fearful, self-protecting heart. Love, by contrast, is secure enough to be vulnerable.

So this portrait of love is not weak. It is enormously strong—stronger than pride, because it can be overlooked and remain joyful. Stronger than envy, because it can rejoice when someone else thrives. Stronger than anger, because it can absorb offense and still choose kindness. Strongest of all, it is the strength of God, “who is patient with us, not wanting any to perish,” and who, in Christ, bore the world’s sin without revenge, and endured the cross without giving up love.

The end of that strength is not conquest but resurrection. Love suffers all things—and love wins.
