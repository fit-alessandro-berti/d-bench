In Matthew 6:1, the problem is not visibility itself, but **the craving to be seen**. The moral danger lies in the **motive** of the heart, which corrupts a selfless act into a selfish transaction. 

Here is a breakdown of why public goodness is morally dangerous according to this verse and its broader context in the Sermon on the Mount:

### 1. Visibility vs. Intention (The Matthew 5 Paradox)
To understand whether visibility itself is the problem, we only have to look a few verses earlier in the very same sermon. In Matthew 5:16, Jesus says: 
> *"Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father which is in heaven."*

At first glance, Matthew 5:16 and Matthew 6:1 seem to contradict each other. However, the difference lies entirely in the **ultimate goal of the visibility**:
* **In Matthew 5:16**, the goal of being seen is that people will *glorify God*. 
* **In Matthew 6:1**, the goal of being seen is that people will *glorify you*. 

A commentary tradition once summarized this paradox well: Christians should show when they are tempted to hide, and hide when they are tempted to show. Visibility is not a sin; vanity is.

### 2. The Greek Text: "Theatrical" Goodness
The phrase translated as "to be seen of them" comes from the Greek word *theathēnai* (θεαθῆναι), which is the root of our English word **theater**. 

Jesus is warning against turning righteousness into a stage performance. When goodness becomes theatrical, the actor is no longer authentically living out their faith; they are wearing a mask (the literal meaning of the Greek word *hypocrite*, which Jesus uses in the very next verse) to elicit applause from an audience. 

### 3. Why is this "Morally Dangerous"?
The craving to be seen introduces several severe moral and spiritual dangers:

* **It Exploits the Needy:** In the specific context of giving "alms" (charity to the poor), doing it to be seen reduces a suffering human being into a mere prop for your own ego. You are not actually loving your neighbor; you are using your neighbor to purchase social status and admiration for yourself.
* **It Commits Idolatry:** By craving the gaze of men rather than the gaze of God, a person makes human opinion their ultimate judge and highest priority. It replaces the fear of God with the approval of society.
* **It Breeds Self-Deception:** The most insidious danger of performative goodness is that the actor eventually believes their own act. Because the outward deed is objectively good (the poor person still gets fed), the giver becomes blinded to the rotting pride inside their own soul. 

### 4. The Economic Consequence: "Paid in Full"
Jesus warns that if you do good works to be seen, *"ye have no reward of your Father which is in heaven."* 

In the following verses (6:2, 5, 16), Jesus explains why: *"Verily I say unto you, They have their reward."* The Greek word for "have" here is *apechō*, a commercial bookkeeping term used on ancient receipts meaning **"paid in full."**

The moral danger is that you reduce a spiritual act to a worldly transaction. If you do a good deed to get likes, praise, and a good reputation, and people give you that praise, the transaction is complete. Your invoice is stamped "Paid in Full." You cannot then take that same receipt to heaven and ask God for an eternal reward, because the deed wasn't done for Him in the first place. 

### Summary
Public goodness is morally dangerous because **human applause is an addictive counterfeit for divine approval.** The problem is not that other people might accidentally see you doing good; the problem is the human heart's tendency to hijack a sacred duty and use it for self-worship.
