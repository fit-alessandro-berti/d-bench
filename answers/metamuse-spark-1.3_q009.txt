This passage defines victory in a completely opposite way from how we normally think about it.

In Romans 12:17-21, Paul is writing to Christians living under Nero — people with real enemies, not just annoying neighbors. His conclusion is verse 21:

> “Be not overcome of evil, but overcome evil with good.”

The Greek word for “overcome” in both halves is *nikao* — to conquer, to win the victory. Paul is talking about winning, not losing. But he redefines *how* you win.

### 1. Victory is not becoming what you hate

“Be not overcome of evil” means: Don’t let someone else’s evil set your agenda.

Evil wants to do more than hurt you — it wants to *infect* you. If you are insulted and you insult back, if you are cheated and you cheat back, if you are hated and you hate back, evil has won twice. It harmed you, and then it reproduced itself in you.

Retaliation feels strong, but Paul says it’s actually being conquered.

Victory starts negatively: refuse to be shaped by evil.

### 2. Victory is active good, not passive weakness

“If thine enemy hunger, feed him; if he thirst, give him drink” is a direct quote from Proverbs 25:21-22.

Notice it’s not: ignore him, avoid him, just feel nice thoughts about him. It’s feed him. Give him drink. Meet basic human need in a costly, practical way.

That’s why it’s not weak:

* Weakness would be *do nothing* because you’re afraid.
* Worldly strength would be *do harm back* because you can.
* This command is *do good on purpose* when you have every reason not to — which takes far more self-control, courage, and freedom than revenge.

It comes right after Romans 12:19: “Vengeance is mine; I will repay, saith the Lord.” Paul isn’t saying justice doesn’t matter. He’s saying: release your right to personal vengeance to God, so you are free to act with mercy.

That is the same pattern as Romans 5:8 — “while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us.” We overcome as God overcame: by answering evil with redemptive good.

### 3. What about “heaping coals of fire on his head”?

That sounds manipulative, like: be nice so God will punish him harder, or be nice to make him feel guilty.

That’s not what Paul means. Three things help:

**a) It’s not our weapon, it’s God’s outcome.** In Proverbs and in Romans, the “coals” follow a trust in the Lord to act. You do the good; God handles the heart and the judgment. Your motive must be genuine love (Rom. 12:9: “Let love be without dissimulation”), not calculation.

**b) Coals meant shame that melts, not burns.** Most scholars see this as the burning pain of a conscience awakened. Kindness to an enemy can shame him in a healthy way — not to crush him, but to expose his wrong and open the door to repentance and reconciliation.

In the ancient world fire was also a symbol of purification. Some early interpreters added: like metal melted by fire, a hard heart can be melted by persistent goodness.

In other words: your goodness may burn away his hostility.

**c) Left to God, coals can also mean judgment.** If he persists in evil, your kindness leaves him without excuse before God (see Romans 2:4 — God’s goodness leads to repentance). Either way, you don’t administer the coals. God does.

So it’s not: “Pretend to be nice in order to hurt him.” It’s: “Be sincerely good, and trust God to use it to convict him, convert him, or justly judge him.”

### So: weak, manipulative, or genuinely strong?

Genuinely strong, because:

1.  **It breaks the cycle.** Evil feeds on retaliation. Good starves it.
2.  **It preserves your humanity.** You don’t win by becoming evil yourself.
3.  **It imitates Christ.** Jesus didn’t overcome the cross by coming down from it in anger, but by saying, “Father, forgive them.”

Paul’s logic is summarized in Romans 12:2: “Be ye transformed by the renewing of your mind.” A transformed mind doesn’t ask, “How can I get even?” It asks, “How can good conquer here, even through me?”
