The sequence is deliberate. Biblical repentance is never only a turning *away*; it is also a turning *toward*.

> “Cease to do evil; **learn to do well**...”

### 1. Stopping evil is the beginning, not the goal

Isaiah is speaking to people who are still very religious — offering sacrifices, keeping feasts, praying — while their “hands are full of blood” (Isa. 1:15). God rejects worship that is separated from justice.

So first: *wash, put away, cease.*

You cannot learn a new way of life while still practicing the old one. Restraint matters. Goodness begins by refusing harm, refusing exploitation, refusing complicity.

But if repentance ends there, it produces only an empty innocence: *I didn’t do anything wrong.* Isaiah won’t settle for that.

### 2. Goodness must be *learned*

The Hebrew is *limdû hêṭêb* — “learn to do well.”

That verb is striking. Doing good is not presented as natural, automatic, or merely a matter of good intentions. It is a practice that must be acquired, like a skill or a language.

Why? Because evil itself has been learned — through habit, custom, self-interest, and a culture that normalized injustice. It is not enough to unlearn cruelty; our instincts, reflexes, spending, speech, use of power, and attention must be retrained.

In that sense, goodness is formation, not just restraint:

- Not stealing, but learning generosity
- Not lying, but learning truthfulness that heals
- Not oppressing, but learning advocacy
- Not merely feeling compassion, but learning what actually helps

A cleared field is not yet a harvest. A life that has ceased doing evil still needs to be planted with good.

### 3. Isaiah immediately defines “doing well” as justice for the vulnerable

He does not leave “well” vague or sentimental:

> “Seek judgment [justice], relieve the oppressed, judge the fatherless, plead for the widow.”

Notice the movement outward. The passage begins with *my* cleansing — “wash *you*, make *you* clean” — and ends with *someone else’s* need — the widow who has no advocate.

And the verbs are active, even costly:

- **Seek** justice — don’t just avoid injustice; pursue what is right.
- **Relieve** the oppressed — intervene to lift a burden, not merely regret it.
- **Judge** the fatherless — give the powerless a fair hearing.
- **Plead** for the widow — *rîbû*, take up her legal cause; lend her your voice where she has none.

True goodness, then, is not neutrality. A person can refrain from personally oppressing the widow and still leave her undefended. Isaiah calls that person not to cleaner hands alone, but to hands put to work for mercy.

### The suggestion

Goodness in Isaiah is not mere restraint. Restraint is the doorway; practiced love is the destination.

Holiness is not simply the absence of evil in us. It is the learned, repeated, concrete presence of justice and compassion through us — until doing good becomes, by grace and practice, our new habit.
