**Genesis 4:6‑7 – A snapshot of moral agency**

The exchange between the Lord and Cain is not simply a story of a brother killing another; it is a theological sketch of how human beings are called to make moral choices. The passage presents moral agency as a **free‑will, internally‑driven decision‑making process** that is relational (between the person and God) and consequential (the outcome depends on the choice made).

---

### 1.  What kind of “evil” is shown?

| Option | How it fits (or doesn’t) |
|--------|--------------------------|
| **Impulse / desire** | The text calls Cain’s problem an *“impulse”* of anger and jealousy. He is asked, “Why art thou wroth?” – a question that forces him to notice an inner urge. |
| **Beast** | No animal or monstrous force is introduced; the evil is not a creature that dominates Cain. |
| **Habit** | The narrative describes a single, decisive act (murder). There is no indication that a long‑standing practice has led to it. |
| **Temptation** | The phrase **“sin lieth at the door”** suggests that the temptation is right before him, waiting to be taken. It is a lure that must be either resisted or walked through. |
| **Something else** | The best single label is **temptation‑as‑internal‑desire**. Evil is presented as a *desire* that resides in the human heart and is available to be acted upon, but it is not yet sin until the person chooses to pursue it. |

Thus, the passage treats evil primarily as **an internal temptation/desire** that the individual can either obey or reject.

---

### 2.  The picture of moral agency drawn

1. **Free‑will & self‑examination** – God directly questions Cain (“Why art thou wroth?”). The dialogue invites the sinner to look inward, indicating that moral agency begins with self‑awareness.
2. **Conditional acceptance** – “If thou doest well, shalt thou not be accepted?” The promise of divine favor is **contingent** on the agent’s moral response.
3. **Moral agency as a choice between two paths** – The text sets up a binary:  
   - **Righteousness** → acceptance, blessing.  
   - **Sin** → “sin lieth at the door,” i.e., the possibility of transgression is immediate and real.
4. **Relational dimension** – The “desire” that Cain harbors is toward Abel’s offering and ultimately over him. The moral stakes are set in a relationship with God (the covenant) and with the brother he has destroyed.

In short, the narrative paints moral agency as **a conscious, volitional capacity to choose between good and evil**, with the power to either accept God’s favor or fall into sin.

---

### 3.  Responsibility placed on the person

- **Personal accountability** – The Lord does not blame an external force (the “beast,” a curse, or fate). He speaks to Cain as the origin of the anger and the subsequent murder. The text says, “If thou doest well…,” making the outcome dependent on Cain’s decision.
- **Warning of consequences** – “Sin lieth at the door” functions as a **forewarning**: the temptation is right there, but the person must decide whether to open the door. Once the door is opened, the act of sin follows.
- **Moral duty** – By asking “Why art thou wroth?” God expects Cain to **manage his emotions** and to act justly. The responsibility is both **internal** (mastering his own desire) and **external** (maintaining right standing before God).
- **Consequences are tied to the choice** – Acceptance or rejection is not arbitrary; it is the natural result of the moral path taken. Cain’s choice to let jealousy dominate leads to murder, and the narrative implies that he bears the guilt for that act.

---

### 4.  Summary

- **Evil is portrayed as an internal temptation/desire** (not a beast, habit, or independent entity).  
- **Moral agency** is depicted as a free‑willed, self‑examining capacity that must decide between righteousness and sin.  
- **Responsibility** rests squarely on Cain: he is directly addressed, warned, and offered a conditional promise of acceptance. The text holds him accountable for his anger, jealousy, and ultimate violent act, emphasizing that the choice to follow God’s will or succumb to temptation is entirely his own.

Thus, Genesis 4:6‑7 offers a classic biblical model of moral agency: a person equipped with the ability to discern, choose, and be held responsible for either good or evil, with evil appearing as a tempting internal impulse that can be resisted or embraced.
