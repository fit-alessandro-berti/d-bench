The difference is not merely “how well” someone obeys, but **where obedience comes from**.

External compliance means behaving in conformity to God’s commandments from the outside in: fear of punishment, desire for approval, social pressure, religious habit, moral effort, or self-interest. A person may obey outwardly while inwardly resisting, resenting, or using obedience to feel superior. The heart can remain unchanged, and the obedience may be fragile or selective.

A “new heart” is something God does from the inside out. In biblical language, the “heart” is not just emotion; it is the center of the will, desires, motives, and loyalty. To receive a new heart is to have one’s deepest self changed so that love for God, repentance, trust, and obedience become possible and natural. In Ezekiel, God does not say merely, “Try harder.” He says, **“I will give,” “I will take away,” “I will put.”** The transformed person walks in God’s ways because God’s Spirit is now within them, enabling and inclining that walk.

The “stony heart” metaphor is powerful because it portrays hardness as more than a bad habit. A stone is unresponsive: it cannot be molded easily, it does not feel, it does not yield. A stony heart is numb to God, resistant to conviction, incapable of producing true love. It is a vivid picture of spiritual deadness. The remedy is not merely polish or improved behavior but replacement with a “heart of flesh”—alive, sensitive, capable of receiving God’s word and returning His love.

So the material imagery emphasizes the **radical nature of the change**. This is not self-improvement but divine re-creation. Hardness must be “removed” because it cannot be reformed by external coercion. External compliance may change actions; the new heart changes the person, and therefore the actions flow from a renewed life.

For Christian readers, this promise is fulfilled in the new covenant work of Christ and the Spirit—regeneration, being “born again,” becoming a new creation.
