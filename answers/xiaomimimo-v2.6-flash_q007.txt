# Discernment vs. the Appetite to Condemn

## The Misreading

The most common misreading of Luke 6:37 flattens it into a blanket prohibition: *all* moral evaluation is forbidden, and any distinction between right and wrong is itself a sin. But this reading collapses under its own weight. If moral judgments never mattered — if no one is ever in the wrong — then "ye shall not be judged" becomes meaningless, because there would be no standard by which anyone could be judged in the first place. The verse presupposes a moral order that judges; it does not erase one.

Moreover, Jesus himself constantly made moral judgments. He called out hypocrisy, warned against greed, exposed the emptiness of outward religion. He was not morally indifferent. Something more specific is being addressed here.

## What the Verse Actually Targets

The passage does not forbid *discernment*. It forbids a particular **posture of the heart**: the eager, self-elevating appetite to pass judgment on others as a way of establishing oneself above them. The structure of the verse makes this clear:

- **"Judge not, and ye shall not be judged"** — This does not deny that judgment exists. It acknowledges that *we are all under judgment*, that a moral standard applies universally, and that the person who sits in judgment over others while ignoring that they themselves fall under that same standard is acting in bad faith. The person who refuses the appetite to condemn is not saying moral reality doesn't apply to them — they are saying it applies to them *too*.

- **"Condemn not, and ye shall not be condemned"** — Here a distinction is drawn between *judgment* (recognizing that something is wrong) and *condemnation* (declaring a person irredeemable, passing them over as beyond mercy). One can identify moral error without issuing a final verdict on a human soul.

- **"Forgive, and ye shall be forgiven"** — This presupposes that something has gone wrong — that moral reality is real enough that there is something to be forgiven. Forgiveness is meaningless in a world where moral judgments never matter.

## Discernment vs. Condemnation

**Discernment** is the capacity to perceive moral reality: to tell truth from falsehood, good from evil, and to act on that perception. It is essential. Without it, there is no justice, no correction, no love — because love requires the ability to recognize what is actually harming the beloved. Discernment is:

- **Open to self-examination.** It asks first what is wrong in the one who sees.
- **Humble.** It knows its own limits and is aware of being subject to the same standard.
- **Oriented toward restoration.** Its goal is the recovery of what is broken.
- **Proportional.** It distinguishes between recognizing error and declaring a person beyond hope.

**The appetite to condemn** is something different. It is:

- **Self-elevating.** By condemning another, the condemnor positions themselves above — as though they have no log in their own eye.
- **Closed to self-examination.** It finds it easier to catalogue the faults of others than to reckon with one's own.
- **Self-serving.** It provides a psychological reward: it replaces the painful work of self-confrontation with the satisfying work of other-judgment.
- **Disguised as moral seriousness.** It wears the clothes of discernment while lacking its humility and its mercy.
- **Oriented toward dismissal.** Its aim is not to restore the other but to write them off — to hold them permanently beneath oneself.

## The Log and the Speck

The passage that follows in Luke (6:41-42) confirms this reading directly. Jesus does not say the brother has no speck. He says: *first deal with the log in your own eye, then you will be able to see clearly to remove the speck from your brother's.* Notice what this means: **discernment about others is still expected**, but only *after* rigorous self-examination. The appetite to condemn is precisely the person who rushes to the other's eye while ignoring their own — not because they lack moral sight, but because they lack the willingness to turn that sight inward.

## The Deeper Point

Jesus is not endorsing moral apathy. He is exposing a specific inversion: the person who is eager to condemn others while being blind to themselves has **actually failed at discernment**. They have become *less* morally perceptive, not more — because their appetite for condemnation has become a blind spot. True discernment is always first self-implicating. It always begins with the recognition that the standard which condemns another also condemns you.

The appetizer to condemn says: *"I see your fault, and that settles it — you are lesser."*
Discernment says: *"I see your fault, and I see mine, and neither of us is beyond the reach of the mercy that both of us need."*

Luke 6:37 does not say that moral reality is a matter of indifference. It says that the correct response to moral reality is not the self-elevating pleasure of condemnation but the self-humbling necessity of forgiveness — knowing that you yourself stand in need of the same.
