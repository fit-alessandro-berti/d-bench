What makes public goodness morally dangerous in Matthew 6:1 is not visibility itself but the motive that visibility can conceal and corrupt: the craving to be seen. The verse's own grammar makes this clear. Jesus does not say "do not your alms before men," full stop; he says "do not your alms before men, *to be seen of them*." The Greek phrase behind "to be seen" (pros to theathenai autois) carries the sense of performing for spectators — it is the root of our word "theater." The warning is against almsgiving as theater: goodness staged for an audience, with human admiration as the intended payoff.

That reading is confirmed by the surrounding context. Only a chapter earlier, in Matthew 5:16, Jesus commands the opposite of concealment: "Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father which is in heaven." If visibility as such were the danger, these two sayings would contradict each other. They do not, because they distinguish two different ends of visible goodness. In 5:16, others see the deed and the glory flows upward, to God. In 6:1, others see the deed and the glory is meant to terminate on the doer. The deed may look identical from the outside; the moral difference lies entirely in whose reward the agent is seeking. Visibility is morally neutral — sometimes even obligatory. The craving to be seen is what poisons it.

Why is that craving so dangerous? Several strands of the verse suggest an answer.

First, it quietly changes what the act *is*. Almsgiving directed at the poor is an act of mercy; almsgiving directed at spectators is an act of self-promotion that merely uses the poor as props. The recipient becomes instrumental, the audience becomes the true addressee, and the "good deed" becomes a transaction in the economy of reputation. The performer of such alms is, in Jesus' word a few verses later (6:2), a "hypocrite" — hypokrites, literally a stage actor. The danger is that one can perform righteousness so convincingly, even to oneself, that the performance replaces the reality. Public goodness is dangerous precisely because it is the easiest place for this substitution to happen undetected: the outward act is genuinely good, so neither the crowd nor the doer's own conscience raises an alarm.

Second, the verse frames the danger in terms of reward: "otherwise ye have no reward of your Father which is in heaven." The logic, spelled out in 6:2 ("Verily I say unto you, They have their reward"), is that human praise and divine reward are alternative currencies, and the agent implicitly chooses which one the act is done for. The one who gives in order to be seen is paid in full — in applause — and the account is closed; the Greek verb apechousin is a commercial term for a receipt, "paid in full." The craving to be seen is dangerous because it is self-defeating in the deepest sense: it trades a durable good (standing before God, the formation of a genuinely generous character) for a perishable one (a moment of admiration). It settles for the smaller payment and forfeits the greater.

Third, the craving corrupts the agent over time. An act done to be seen trains the heart to need being seen. Righteousness becomes contingent on an audience: generous when watched, indifferent when not. That is why the remedy Jesus prescribes in the following verses is deliberate secrecy — "let not thy left hand know what thy right hand doeth" (6:3), give "in secret" (6:4). Secrecy is not commanded because visibility is evil, but as a discipline, a kind of moral quarantine: by removing the audience, it removes the possibility of the corrupt motive and forces the act to stand on its true purpose alone. The hyperbole of the left hand not knowing pushes further still — ideally one hides the deed even from one's own self-congratulating gaze, guarding against the inner audience as well as the outer one.

So the answer to the question's dilemma is direct: the problem is not visibility itself but the craving to be seen. Visibility is the occasion of the temptation, not the sin; Matthew 5:16 positively requires visible goodness when it points beyond the doer. What Matthew 6:1 exposes is that the same visible deed can serve two opposite masters — the glory of God (and the good of the neighbor), or the glorification of the self — and that the second motive, precisely because it wears the costume of virtue, is among the most subtle and dangerous corruptions of the moral life. Public goodness is dangerous the way a stage is dangerous to an actor who forgets he is playing a role: not because the stage is wicked, but because the applause can become the point.
