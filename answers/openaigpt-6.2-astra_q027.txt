The key is in Luke’s opening line: the parable is addressed to those who **“trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and despised others.”** Pride is the root, comparison is how it operates, and contempt is its fruit. The Pharisee’s fasting and tithing need not be false or wrong. The trouble is what he makes of them: evidence that he stands right before God, particularly when measured against “this publican.”

His prayer begins as thanksgiving, but what he thanks God for is the distance he sees between himself and another man. There is an element of self-display here, but the point does not depend on anyone overhearing him. Even in private, he is reciting his virtues as a verdict in his own favor. If there is a false belief beneath the prayer, it is that his record entitles him to deliver that verdict himself.

The tax collector’s posture is truer because his gesture and his words agree: **“God be merciful to me a sinner.”** He neither excuses his wrong nor points to someone worse. His bowed eyes are not a technique for winning favor, and Jesus does not say he is justified because he has outperformed the Pharisee in humility. The decisive contrast is that the Pharisee comes with a verdict about himself and his neighbor; the tax collector comes with a plea, leaving the verdict to God.
