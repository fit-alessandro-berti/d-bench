## Short answer

The verse says both — but the direction of causation runs from treasure *to* heart, which makes "treasure trains the heart" the primary claim, with "treasure reveals the heart" as its necessary corollary.

## The grammar decides the direction

Notice what Jesus does *not* say. He does not say, "Where your heart is, there your treasure will be." That would be the "reveals" reading alone: the heart's preexisting loves produce treasure choices, and the treasure is merely a symptom to read. Instead he says the reverse: ὅπου γάρ ἐστιν ὁ θησαυρός σου, ἐκεῖ ἔσται καὶ ἡ καρδία σου — *where the treasure is, there the heart will be*. Treasure is the subject; the heart is the follower.

Moreover, verse 21 is introduced by γάρ ("for") and is therefore grounding two *imperatives*: "Do not lay up… Lay up…" Jesus is not simply diagnosing his hearers; he is persuading them. The logic of the argument only works if treasure placement exerts a pull on the heart: *since* the heart inevitably follows the treasure, *therefore* relocate the treasure — and the heart will come along. This is why the saying functions as a lever for reformation and not merely a mirror. You cannot directly command the heart ("feel differently about money!"), but you can command the hands and the accounts — and Jesus does exactly that, treating the heart's location as a downstream consequence of a decision he expects you to make.

At the same time, the "reveals" reading is true by inversion: if the heart follows treasure, then your existing pattern of investment — where your money, time, and security actually sit — is a reliable map of where your heart has already been dragged. The verse reads your ledger, and the ledger reads you.

## How possession, attention, and love get tied together

The mechanism is embedded in the imagery of verses 19–20. Earthly treasure is defined by its *vulnerability*: moth, rust, thieves. Vulnerable possessions demand vigilance. And vigilance, sustained over time, is indistinguishable from attachment:

1. **Possession creates guardianship.** What you own, you must watch. Moth and rust and burglars are precisely the threats that require a person's mental energy — this is why the passage flows naturally into 6:25–34 ("do not be anxious"). Anxious watching is the tax that earthly treasure levies on the heart.
2. **Attention ripens into care.** Whatever regularly occupies the mind's vigilance becomes the mind's preoccupation. In biblical anthropology, the καρδία is not merely the seat of emotion but the control center of thought, will, and desire (cf. Prov 4:23). What holds the heart's attention long enough becomes what the heart *cares about*.
3. **Care hardens into love — or lordship.** This is the point of the very next verse: "You cannot serve God and mammon" (6:24). Treasure that commands attention eventually commands the heart, and a commanding heart is a serving heart. The treasure becomes a master precisely through the chain of possession → attention → love.

Heavenly treasure breaks the chain at the first link. It is incorruptible and unstealable, so it demands no anxious vigilance; investing there is the one form of possession that *frees* attention rather than consuming it — freeing it, in context, for the Father (6:1–18), whose "treasure in heaven" logic echoes the rich young ruler's invitation (Mark 10:21) and Colossians 3:1–3 ("seek the things above… set your minds on things above").

## The pastoral implication

Jesus exploits an unavoidable human fact: the heart cannot stay where the treasure does not go, and it cannot stay away from where the treasure is. So he converts a truth about desire into a discipline of desire. Do not wait to *feel* heavenly-minded before giving; give, and the feeling follows — because where your treasure goes, your heart is already on its way.
