Isaiah 55:6–7 — Why repentance touches both "way" and "thoughts"

1. The shape of the verse

Verse 7 is built as Hebrew parallelism with two matched halves on the human side and two on God's side:

  Let the wicked (rasha') forsake his way (derek),
  and the unrighteous man (ish 'awen, "man of iniquity") his thoughts (machshevotav);
  and let him return (shuv) to the LORD, and he will have mercy (racham) on him;
  and to our God, for he will abundantly pardon (yarbeh lisloach, "he will multiply to forgive").

"Way" and "thoughts" are not two different sins but two dimensions of one life. Hebrew parallelism characteristically names a thing from two angles so that the whole is described. "The wicked" and "the man of iniquity" are the same person; "his way" and "his thoughts" are that person seen from outside and from inside. Repentance ("return," shuv, the root of teshuvah) is therefore a turning of the whole person, not of a part.

2. "Way": the outward course

Derek is the road one walks, the habitual direction and pattern of conduct—what other people can see, what can be judged, what leaves a track. To "forsake his way" is to leave the road one has been travelling: to stop the concrete acts, the practices, the relationships and habits that constitute a wicked life. Isaiah has already insisted on this dimension in 1:16–17 ("cease to do evil, learn to do well") and will insist on it again in chapters 58–59. Repentance that never reaches the feet is not repentance.

3. "Thoughts": the inward imagination

Machashavah is stronger than an idle notion passing through the mind. It means devising, planning, scheming, purposing—the inner workshop where conduct is designed before it is carried out. This is the word of Genesis 6:5 ("every imagination of the thoughts of his heart was only evil continually"), of Proverbs 6:18 ("an heart that deviseth wicked imaginations"), of Micah 2:1 (those who "devise iniquity... upon their beds"), and, tellingly, of Isaiah 59:7 ("their thoughts are thoughts of iniquity") and 65:2 (a people who "walketh in a way that was not good, after their own thoughts"). The unrighteous man's thoughts are the root system of his way; the way is what his thoughts look like once they have grown into the open.

There is one further nuance. The sinner's "thoughts" include his thoughts about God—his calculation that God is far off, indifferent, or unforgiving, and so that there is no point in turning. Jeremiah 18:12 puts the mentality exactly: "There is no hope: but we will walk after our own devices [machashavot]." Despair about mercy is itself a wicked thought that must be abandoned.

4. Why both are required

(a) Because sin is a matter of the whole person. Scripture never treats wickedness as merely a set of bad acts committed by an otherwise sound interior. The acts flow from the heart (Proverbs 4:23; cf. Jesus in Matthew 15:19, "out of the heart proceed evil thoughts, murders, adulteries..."). A repentance that addresses only what is visible has not gone to where the disease is.

(b) Because each half guards against a counterfeit of the other. If only the "way" were named, repentance could become mere behavior management—outward reform with the same designs still working underneath, the hypocrisy Isaiah condemns in 29:13 ("this people draw near me with their mouth... but have removed their heart far from me"). If only "thoughts" were named, repentance could become mere sentiment—remorse, tears, good intentions, with the feet still on the same road. Naming both closes both escape routes: the turning must be as deep as the imagination and as concrete as conduct.

(c) Because God is the one being returned to. The verse does not say "return to the law" or "return to decency" but "return unto the LORD." A person cannot return to God with only his behavior; God sees the heart (1 Samuel 16:7). Nor can he return with only his heart while his life says the opposite. To come to a person, one comes whole.

(d) Because the root determines the future. If only the way is left, the thoughts will lay a new road in the same direction tomorrow. Repentance that reaches the devising faculty is the only repentance that lasts.

5. The hinge with verses 8–9

The pair "way / thoughts" is not chosen at random; it is the exact pair God uses of himself in the very next verses: "For my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways... as the heavens are higher than the earth, so are my ways higher than your ways, and my thoughts than your thoughts." The sinner is asked to abandon his ways and thoughts because there is a higher set of ways and thoughts to exchange them for. Repentance is not a void; it is the vacating of one's own road and one's own designs in order to be taken up into God's. Verses 10–11 then describe how that happens: God's word comes down like rain and does not return empty. The thoughts one gives up are replaced by a word from outside oneself that reshapes both mind and life.

6. What this implies about mercy

At first the double demand seems to make mercy harder to reach. Anyone can stop an action for a while; who can govern his imagination? If God requires the thoughts as well as the way, the honest sinner may conclude that the door is shut. The passage answers this in three ways.

First, the depth of the demand is matched by the abundance of the pardon. God does not merely "pardon"; he "multiplies to pardon." The Hebrew idiom means his forgiveness is not measured out in proportion to the sinner's success at reform. It exceeds. The two divine verbs together—racham, the warm compassion of a parent for a child, and salach, the judicial remission of guilt—show that the response is both tender and complete. The one who returns is not merely acquitted; he is pitied and welcomed.

Second, the "for" that opens verse 8 grounds this abundance in the otherness of God. Human thought would not pardon like this; a man who had been wronged as God has been wronged would calculate, defer, and demand securities. That God's thoughts are "higher than your thoughts" is precisely why he can do what our imagination cannot conceive. So the very thoughts the sinner must forsake include the thought that mercy of this size is impossible. Mercy is possible because God does not think as we do.

Third, the setting of the chapter shows that this mercy is already provided, not merely promised. Chapter 55 opens with an invitation to buy "without money and without price," and it follows directly on chapter 53, where the Servant "was wounded for our transgressions" and "the LORD hath laid on him the iniquity of us all." The abundant pardon of 55:7 rests on the abundant bearing of sin in 53. Repentance does not purchase mercy; it is the way one receives what has been made available.

Verse 6 adds the note of urgency: "while he may be found... while he is near." Mercy is abundant but not to be presumed upon. There is a season of nearness, and the appeal is to use it. The generosity of the offer and the seriousness of the summons stand together.

7. Summary

Repentance involves both "way" and "thoughts" because a person is both his conduct and his imagination, and God wants the person, not a performance. "Way" insists that repentance be real enough to change how one lives; "thoughts" insists that it be deep enough to change what one devises, values, and believes—including what one believes about God. Together they rule out hypocrisy on one side and mere sentiment on the other. The demand is total, but so is the remedy: the same God who asks for the whole self answers with compassion and multiplied pardon, and he can do so precisely because his thoughts and ways are higher than ours. The sinner who leaves his own road and his own designs does not step into emptiness; he is met by a God who is near, who pities, and who forgives beyond measure.
