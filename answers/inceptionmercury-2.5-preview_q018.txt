By commanding the removal of bitterness immediately before prescribing kindness and pardon, the passage suggests that one cannot coexist with the other. This structure implies a transformative exchange rather than a simple suggestion.

Regarding the nature of bitterness, the phrase "put away" indicates agency. If it were only a fleeting feeling, the instruction might focus on management rather than removal. Listing it alongside behaviors like "clamour" and "evil speaking" implies it often settles into a habit or a chosen posture. It is treated as something actionable, requiring deliberate effort to shed.

The context also points to a communal impact. Since the instruction applies to the community ("one to another"), resentment is portrayed as a force that disrupts unity. Forgiveness acts as the corrective measure, restoring harmony. The motivation provided—God’s prior action toward believers—suggests that overcoming such deep-seated resentment is possible through external grace, not just willpower.
