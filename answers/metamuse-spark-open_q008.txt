The logic of 1 John 4:20 is deliberately practical - and deliberately confronting.

> "for he that loveth not his brother whom he hath seen, how can he love God whom he hath not seen?"

John is not saying love for people *replaces* love for God. He is saying love for people *proves* it. He treats the visible as the test of the invisible for three reasons:

### 1. The visible is verifiable. The invisible is not.

Anyone can claim to love an unseen, perfect, undemanding God in heaven. That claim is unfalsifiable. No one can disprove it.

But a brother - in 1 John *adelphos* means first of all your fellow believer in the community, though the principle extends to any neighbor - is seen. Seen means:

* present and concrete, with real needs, irritations, and differences
* demanding something costly: time, forgiveness, money, patience - 1 John 3:17-18
* observable by others

John's argument is *a fortiori* - from the lesser to the greater: If you fail the observable, testable, closer-to-home command to love the flawed person in front of you, you have no basis to claim you have passed the unobservable, more absolute command to love the flawless God you have never seen.

As 1 John 4:12 puts it: "No man hath seen God at any time." Love for God therefore can't be mystical feeling alone. It must become embodied.

### 2. In John's theology, the two loves have the same source and nature.

For John, *agape* is not a divided thing - one horizontal love for people and one vertical love for God.

1 John 4:7-11: Love *is of God*. God loved us first by acting - sending Christ. To "abide in God" is to receive and then participate in that same self-giving, other-centered love.

Therefore to hate your brother is not just to fail a second, separate duty. It is to show you have never actually participated in God's kind of love at all. You may love *an idea* of God, but not the God who *is* love.

Jesus makes the same identification in Matthew 25:40, John 13:35 - how we treat the brother is how we treat God, because the person is made in his image and Christ identifies himself with him.

### 3. The self-deception it exposes

When John calls such a person "a liar" - *pseustes* - he is not mainly talking about intentional lying to others. He is talking about religious self-deception. It takes a few forms that are still very familiar:

**1. Abstract vs. embodied love:** "I love humanity, it's just people I can't stand." It is easy to love a distant, ideal God while avoiding the inconvenience of a real, requiring person. John says true love is never purely abstract.

**2. Spiritual bypassing:** The false teachers John opposed seemed to claim a superior spiritual knowledge of God that exempted them from ethics - a kind of early Gnosticism. They could be spiritually elite while being cold, factional, or unloving. John says: No. Illumination without compassion is not illumination.

**3. Compartmentalized piety:** Loving God in worship, prayer, doctrine, or religious emotion, but defining "hate" narrowly as only active animosity. In Johannine language, "hateth" includes indifference, contempt, refusal to help, gossip, racial or social superiority, holding a grudge. Not acting in love *is* hate.

In short, the verse doesn't mean you earn love for God by loving people. It means love for people is the necessary diagnostic: The invisible is authenticated by the visible.

If God is an ever-loving giver, a person who claims to know and love that God but lives as a closed-handed taker is deceiving himself about who he knows.

> As one commentator paraphrases it: We cannot love our way up to God in order to prove ourselves; we love our brother because we have first been loved by God - and if that second thing is missing, it proves the first claim was never true.
