These two verses are among the hardest in the New Testament to read without flinching. “Love your enemies” sounds admirable in a Sunday sermon, but strange and almost dangerous in real life. Our natural instincts say: love those who love you, and defend yourself against those who wish you harm. So why is enemy-love so difficult to take seriously?

I think it is difficult because it confronts three ordinary assumptions about love, power, and justice. But once we look closer, the saying is not sentimental, and it is not merely strategic. It is a deeper kind of moral strength.

## First, enemy-love seems “sentimental” if we misunderstand love

If love means having warm feelings, then “love your enemies” is impossible. You cannot command your emotions to feel kindly toward someone who has hurt you. That would be sentimentality—a fake, forced niceness that cannot stand in the face of real evil.

But the Greek word Jesus uses, *agape*, is not primarily an emotion. It is a deliberate action of the will: seeking what is genuinely good for another person, regardless of whether they deserve it. Notice the verbs in the verse:

- bless those who curse you
- do good to those who hate you
- pray for those who persecute you

All three are actions, not feelings. You can curse someone in your heart, or you can bless them. You can wish their harm, or you can do them good. You can fantasize about revenge, or you can pray for them. This kind of love is not gushing or indulgent. It is painfully concrete. It has more in common with lifting a weight than with falling in love.

This is also why enemy-love is not “sentimental” in the cheap sense. The cross is the ultimate example: Jesus was being murdered, and He asked the Father to forgive His killers. That is not sentimentality. That is real, costly moral strength.

## Second, enemy-love is more than strategic

It is true that enemy-love can work as a strategy. In the civil rights movement, Martin Luther King Jr. used nonviolence and love of enemies to expose injustice and win the moral imagination of a nation. Loving your enemy can break cycles of revenge. It can soften hearts. It can make peace possible.

But if we treat it as a strategy, we will inevitably ask: *Does it work?* And often, in a worldly sense, it does not. The enemy may never repent. The abuser may never change. The persecution may not stop. If enemy-love is only a tool for getting better results, it will collapse on days when results don’t come.

Jesus gives a different reason: “that ye may be the children of your Father which is in heaven.” He grounds enemy-love not in its usefulness but in the character of God. God makes His sun rise on evil and good, and sends rain on the just and unjust. He is generous without calculating merit. We are called to imitate that kind of love because we are His children. This is not a strategy for winning. It is a way of being.

## Third, and most importantly, enemy-love is a deeper form of moral strength

It is easy to hate your enemy. Hatred is reactive. It is natural to return like for like, curse for curse, blow for blow. But Jesus says there is a stronger response: to absorb an attack without becoming a mirror of the attacker.

That requires enormous strength. Consider what is happening in the verse:

- The enemy is filled with hostility.
- The disciples are cursed, hated, persecuted.
- Jesus tells them not to let the enemy control their own character. Instead, they are to respond with creative, active goodness.

This is not weakness. It is the strength to stand against evil without becoming evil. It is the power to see God’s image in someone who has committed terrible acts. It is the courage to pray for the person who wants you destroyed, because praying for someone makes it nearly impossible to keep dehumanizing them.

This is also why enemy-love is not the same as being a doormat. Loving an enemy does not mean excusing wrongdoing, refusing boundaries, or abandoning victims. You can pray for a rapist while supporting their prosecution. You can love a tyrant while working tirelessly to limit their power. Enemy-love does not say, “Everything you do is fine.” It says, “I refuse to define you only by the worst thing you have done. I will seek your repentance and the good of everyone you have harmed, and I will not let my heart be poisoned by hate.” That is a more demanding moral posture than simple retaliation.

## Why is it so hard to take seriously?

Because taking it seriously would change everything. It would require giving up our right to enjoy our hatred. It would ask us to see our enemies as human beings. It would demand that we put our trust in God’s justice rather than our own revenge.

It also feels impossible because it is impossible in our own strength. That is why the Christian tradition has always treated this command as something we must pray for before we can obey it. Only in receiving grace can we give grace. Only because God loved us while we were enemies can we love our enemies.

So is the teaching sentimental, strategic, or moral strength? It is not sentimental. It is only accidentally strategic. It is, at bottom, a radical and divine kind of moral strength—the strength to be like the Father, whose love is not earned and cannot be limited by our categories of deserving. Jesus is not describing a nice ideal. He is describing the very heart of God, and calling us to let that heart live in us. It is difficult to take seriously because it is the hardest love in the world. But it is also the only love that can break the cycle of hatred for good.
