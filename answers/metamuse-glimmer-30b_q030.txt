1 Cor 13 is written to a church obsessed with status, spectacle and self-assertion. Corinth is an honor-shame city, a commercial port where rhetoric, competition and the display of spiritual *dynamis* - tongues, prophecy, knowledge - had become the measure of worth. The Corinthians were suing each other, tolerating sexual immorality, dividing over leaders, and boasting about gifts. Paul has just told them that without love their gifts are noise.

So he defines *agape*, rendered here as charity, first by telling them what it is *not* to do because that is what they are doing. The negations are a direct polemic against Corinthian pride.

* **envieth not; vaunteth not itself, is not puffed up** - The community is envious of one another's gifts and puffed up over them. Love refuses competitive honor.
* **Doth not behave itself unseemly, seeketh not her own, is not easily provoked, thinketh no evil** - *asebmoneuetai* is disorderly, indecent conduct. *Seeketh not her own* is self-interest, the lawsuit mentality. *Easily provoked* and *thinketh no evil* are the gossip, suspicion and retaliation that feed factionalism.
* **Rejoiceth not in iniquity, but rejoiceth in the truth** - Love does not take pleasure in the other's downfall or scandal, the Corinthian taste for the spectacular, but rejoices only in what is true.

The negatives are therefore not a random list of vices. They are the exact inverse of the Corinthians' actual practice of love as dominance, self-promotion and moral surveillance.

What is proposed in place of that is a counter-cultural vision of strength. In Greek ethics strength is *arete*: self-assertion, invulnerability, control of others. Paul inverts it.

Love's strength is *makrothymia*, long-suffering patience, and *hupomone*, endurance. It is a strength that can absorb insult, bear burdens, and remain open without being destroyed by it: *Beareth all things, believeth all things, hopeth all things, endureth all things.*

That is strength as self-restraint and self-emptying, not self-exaltation. It does not need to vaunt itself because it does not need to prove its worth. It is not easily provoked because it is not fragile. It thinks no evil because it chooses trust rather than suspicion as a disciplined practice. It seeks not its own because its power is expressed in giving itself away.

The model is Christological. The strength Paul describes is the strength revealed in the cross: power perfected in humility, a love that suffers long and remains kind, that endures without retaliating, that believes and hopes against evidence. It is moral fortitude that can stay relationally vulnerable in a world that equates love with possession and power with domination.

In short, love is strong precisely because it does not need to be strong in the conventional sense. It is strong enough to be patient, kind, humble and trusting, and that endurance is what finally builds up the community.
