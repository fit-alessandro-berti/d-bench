**Luke 6:37 – “Judge not, and ye shall not be judged: condemn not, and ye shall not be condemned: forgive, and ye shall be forgiven.”**

At first glance the three imperatives look like a blanket prohibition on any moral evaluation.  Yet the passage is not a denial that right and wrong exist; it is a warning about the *attitude* and *motive* that underlie how we treat one another.  To see the difference between **discernment** (the God‑given capacity to see moral truth) and the **appetite to condemn** (the selfish urge to label, exclude, or punish), we must unpack what each term really means in the life of a follower of Christ.

---

## 1.  “Judge not” is not a ban on moral judgment

### 1.1  The original context  
In the Sermon on the Mount Jesus contrasts His teaching with the legalistic practices of the religious leaders of His day (cf. Matt 5‑7).  When He says “do not judge,” He is addressing two common Pharisaic habits:

1. **Presumption** – assuming one’s own righteousness while condemning others (the “hypocrite” who “hath no fault” – Matt 7:5).  
2. **Harsh, final condemnation** – pronouncing eternal damnation on people without compassion (the “condemn” that the later church fathers warned against as “anathema”).

Thus “judge not” means *do not presume to have the authority to pronounce eternal judgment* and *do not pass swift, unforgiving verdicts*.

### 1.2  What the command forbids  
- **Arrogance** – the belief that we alone can define sin and salvation.  
- **Hypocrisy** – criticizing others while ignoring our own faults (see Luke 6:36‑41).  
- **Permanent condemnation** – treating a sinner as beyond redemption, thereby mirroring God’s wrath rather than His mercy.

It does **not** say that we cannot *recognize* wrongdoing, *discriminate* between sins, or *offer* correction.  In fact, Jesus later says, “If your brother does something that is contrary to the truth, you must confront him” (cf. 1 Cor 5:11).  That is a *moral judgment*—a discerning act—but it is done with humility, love, and a view toward restoration, not condemnation.

---

## 2.  Moral judgments versus the appetite to condemn

| **Moral Judgment (Discernment)** | **Appetite to Condemn** |
|----------------------------------|--------------------------|
| **Goal:** Protect the vulnerable, promote justice, guide restoration. | **Goal:** Assert superiority, control, or social conformity. |
| **Method:** Private reflection, prayerful consideration of context, humility, and love. | **Method:** Public shaming, gossip, labeling, or exclusion. |
| **Attitude:** “I see a problem; how can I help?” | **Attitude:** “You are bad; you must be punished.” |
| **Outcome:** Encourages repentance, healing, community reconciliation. | **Outcome:** Breeds bitterness, division, and often the very sin it seeks to eradicate. |

The *appetite* to condemn is a **sinful desire** rooted in pride, fear, or a need for moral certainty.  It treats people as static categories (“the sinner,” “the hypocrite”) rather than as fallen, redeemable persons.  The result is often a cycle of judgment → defensiveness → further sin.

Discernment, by contrast, is a **virtue** (cf. James 1:13, “the path of the righteous is like the morning sun”).  It is the Holy Spirit‑given ability to:

1. **Perceive moral reality** – Recognize the difference between a minor lapse and a grave transgression.  
2. **Weigh motives** – See whether a person’s error stems from ignorance, weakness, or malice.  
3. **Choose the appropriate response** – Offer gentle correction, prayer, or, when necessary, a formal church discipline (as in the early‑church practice of 1 Cor 5).

Discernment is therefore *necessary* for moral living; without it we would be blind to the need for repentance or to the danger of a harmful influence.

---

## 3.  Why the verse is not a moral‑nihilist slogan

A claim that “moral judgments never matter” typically arises from a relativistic view: “All values are socially constructed; no one can claim an objective ‘right.’”  Luke 6:37 does **not** make that claim.  It acknowledges that sin exists (the “condemn” language presupposes a moral law) and that we are accountable before God (the promise “ye shall not be judged” is a warning, not a denial of judgment).  

What the text rejects is **the abuse of moral authority**—the way humans can weaponize judgment to dominate, shame, or exclude.  In other words:

- **Moral judgments can still matter** when they are **rooted in love, humility, and a desire for restoration**.  
- **Condemnation becomes a sin** when it is **self‑righteous, permanent, and detached from mercy**.

Thus the verse is a *corrective* for the *heart*, not a *negation* of moral truth.

---

## 4.  The difference in practice

### 4.1  Pastoral correction vs. public shaming  
- **Discernment in action:** A pastor notices a friend’s repeated dishonesty.  He prays, speaks privately, offers accountability, and prays with his friend.  He judges the *action* (lying) but not the *person* (who is still loved).  
- **Appetite to condemn:** The same pastor posts the friend’s failings on social media, calling him a “liar” and urging the congregation to ostracize him.  The motive is to display moral superiority, not to lead the friend back to grace.

### 4.2  Personal relationships  
- **Discernment:** You recognize that a sibling’s criticism of your parenting is motivated by concern, not malice.  You listen, ask clarifying questions, and respond with empathy.  
- **Condemnatory appetite:** You hear the same criticism and immediately label the sibling as “a judge of my family” and cut off contact, feeling vindicated.

Both parties may *identify* the same behavior, but the *inner posture* determines whether the interaction builds up or tears down.

---

## 5.  Theological nuance: Forgiveness as the litmus test

The third clause—“forgive, and ye shall be forgiven”—ties the whole passage together.  Forgiveness is only possible when we **do not cling to the desire to condemn**.  If we hold a grudge, we block the very grace we hope to receive.  The verse thus teaches a *circular* relationship:

1. **Discernment** enables us to see sin accurately.  
2. **Humility** (the opposite of the appetite to condemn) opens us to God’s mercy.  
3. **Forgiveness** restores the relationship and models the kingdom’s ethic.

When we refuse to condemn, we become participants in the very forgiveness we seek.

---

## 6.  Practical take‑aways for anyone seeking to live out this teaching

1. **Examine your motive.** Before you speak a critique, ask: *Am I trying to protect the other person, or am I trying to prove I’m right?*  
2. **Cultivate humility.** Remember that “the first to judge is a fool” (Matt 7:1).  Confess your own shortcomings (1 John 1:9).  
3. **Seek restoration, not retribution.** Use the language of “reproof with love” (cf. 2 Tim 2:24‑26).  
4. **Distinguish between sin and sinful identity.** Sin is a mistake; condemnation makes a person *into* a sinner forever.  
5. **Pray for discernment.** Ask the Holy Spirit to give you the wisdom of the Spirit (John 16:13) so that your judgments are always *discernment* in love.  

---

## 7.  Summary

- **Discernment** is the God‑given ability to perceive moral truth, to correct gently, and to pursue the good of others.  It is essential for responsible moral living.  
- **The appetite to condemn** is a selfish, pride‑driven urge to label, exclude, or punish.  It destroys community and blocks the very forgiveness we hope to receive.  
- **Luke 6:37** does not deny the existence of moral judgments; it warns against the *abuse* of judgment and the *presumption* of ultimate authority.  It calls us to a humility that lets us see sin without becoming condemners.  
- **Moral judgments can still matter** when they are offered in love, aimed at restoration, and accompanied by the spirit of forgiveness.  The verse’s true thrust is a *heart‑check*: “Do not let your judgment become a weapon of pride; let it be a tool of mercy.”

In the end, the difference lies not in *whether* we judge, but *how* we judge.  Discernment, rooted in humility and love, leads us into the forgiveness that “ye shall have” (Matt 6:14‑15); the appetite to condemn drives us away from that grace.  That is the distinction the master teacher intended for us to grasp.
