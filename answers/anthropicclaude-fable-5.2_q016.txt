I'd say each of those is present, but they are symptoms. The deepest problem is that he understands himself as a servant earning wages rather than as a son, and the rest follows from that.

**The evidence in his speech**

- **"These many years do I serve thee."** The Greek verb (*douleuō*) means to serve as a slave. He describes life in his father's house as servitude. The younger son had planned to ask to be made a hired servant, but the elder has been living as one all along.
- **"Thou never gavest me a kid."** He thinks in terms of wages owed. Obedience was his payment, and he feels shortchanged.
- **"That I might make merry with my friends."** The joy he imagines is somewhere else, with other people. Like his brother, he wants the father's goods apart from the father. The younger son got them by leaving, and the elder hoped to get them by staying.
- **"This thy son."** He will not say "my brother." A broken relationship with the father shows up as a broken relationship with the brother.

**How the other options flow from this**

- **Anger** is the surface, the first thing we see.
- **Wounded fairness** makes sense inside a wage system. If the household runs on earning, the feast really is an injustice.
- **Envy** follows, because the brother received freely what he thought he was earning.
- **Pride in obedience** is how he keeps the ledger: "neither transgressed I at any time."
- **Discomfort with mercy** is the result, since mercy bankrupts the system on which he has built his identity.

**The father's answer names the problem**

The father doesn't dispute the son's record or scold his anger. He corrects the relationship: "Son [*teknon*, 'child'], thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine." The son had counted neither of those things, his father's presence and his inheritance, as his reward. Then the father quietly gives back what the son disowned: "this *thy brother*."

So the elder brother is also lost, only at home. He is in the father's house without sharing the father's heart, which is why he can't share the father's joy.

This fits the setting in Luke 15:1–2, where Jesus tells the parable to Pharisees who are murmuring that he receives sinners. The story ends without telling us whether the elder brother goes in, which leaves the question open for those hearers.
