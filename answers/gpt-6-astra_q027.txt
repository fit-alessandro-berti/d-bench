The parable’s opening gives the controlling diagnosis: Jesus addresses those who **“trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and despised others.”** The Pharisee’s problem is therefore not one isolated fault, but a relationship between self-reliance and contempt: **he treats his virtue as grounds for confidence before God, and other people’s failings as confirmation of his own worth.**

### What, exactly, is wrong with his prayer?

- **Not primarily false doctrine.** Jesus does not accuse him of teaching an incorrect creed. Nor does the story say that his fasting and tithing are fraudulent or bad. The problem is what he makes those practices mean.
- **Pride is central**, but more precisely *self-righteousness*: he approaches God as someone whose standing is secured by his own record.
- **Comparison supplies his evidence:** “I am not as other men are.” Instead of bringing himself before God, he measures himself against people he considers worse.
- **Contempt is the relational consequence:** “even as this publican.” The other worshipper becomes a foil for his superiority rather than a neighbor who also needs God.
- **Virtue becomes self-display**, although the text does not require us to imagine him performing for a human audience. Even in prayer, he rehearses his moral distinction. He addresses God, but his attention remains centered on himself.

His opening, “God, I thank thee,” sounds properly devout. Yet the substance is a catalogue of reasons he deserves approval. **Thanksgiving has become self-congratulation.** He asks for nothing; his prayer leaves no expressed need for mercy.

### Why is the tax collector’s posture spiritually truer?

Not because tax collecting is morally preferable, nor because shame itself saves. In that setting, tax collectors were commonly associated with exploitation and collaboration with Roman power. Jesus is not simply revealing that the socially disreputable man was secretly the respectable one.

The tax collector’s prayer is truer because **he acknowledges his need without turning it into a defense**:

> “God be merciful to me a sinner.”

He does not compare himself with anyone. He does not excuse his conduct. He does not offer achievements as leverage. He asks God to do for him what he cannot establish for himself: grant mercy.

His lowered eyes and beating of his breast embody that acknowledgment. But the bodily gestures are not a technique that earns justification. A person could display humility just as self-righteously as another displays fasting. What matters is the appeal those gestures express: dependence on God’s mercy rather than confidence in one’s own deservingness.

### The decisive reversal

Jesus says the tax collector goes home **“justified”**—accepted as righteous before God—rather than the Pharisee. That is not simply a verdict about which man feels more sincerely. It concerns their standing before God.

The verse’s concluding principle makes the reversal explicit:

> “Every one that exalteth himself shall be abased; and he that humbleth himself shall be exalted.”

So, strictly speaking, the story pronounces justification on the tax collector rather than giving a comprehensive account of the Pharisee’s final condemnation.

**The contrast is not between virtue and vice, but between using virtue to establish a claim upon God and acknowledging one’s need for mercy.** Its final trap for the reader would be to pray, “God, I thank you that I am not like this Pharisee.”
