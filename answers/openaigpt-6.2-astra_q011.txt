Isaiah moves from “cease” to “learn” because stopping an injustice is necessary, but it does not by itself secure justice for someone who has been wronged. The two commands belong together: doing good cannot excuse continuing harm, and refraining from harm does not exhaust what goodness asks.

“Learn” suggests that goodness takes practice. The commands that follow show what that practice looks like. To “judge the fatherless” is to secure a fair hearing for the orphan; to “plead for the widow” is to take up the cause of someone who might otherwise go unheard. It is one thing not to cheat a widow; it is another to speak up when she is being cheated.

In the surrounding passage, Isaiah challenges people whose worship coexists with injustice. The call to “make you clean” therefore points beyond ritual observance to a change in how they live with others. **Restraint is the beginning of goodness, not its completion. Love takes the practiced form of justice.**
