## The shape of the list

The five vices aren't a random pile. They run from the inside out:

- **bitterness** (*pikria*) — a taste, a disposition, nothing visible yet
- **wrath** (*thymos*) — the hot flare, passion boiling up
- **anger** (*orgē*) — the settled, colder version
- **clamour** (*kraugē*) — it becomes audible; shouting
- **evil speaking** (*blasphēmia*) — it becomes damage; slander against a person

Then "with all malice" (*kakia*) as the sack that holds them all. Bitterness stands at the head not because it's worst but because it's first — the seedbed from which the rest germinate. By the time you reach *blasphēmia*, the private taste has become a public instrument. That trajectory is itself an argument: the text doesn't think bitterness stays where it starts.

## "Put away" is passive

The verb is ἀρθήτω — an aorist passive imperative, closer to "let it be lifted off you" than "stop feeling it." It belongs to the garment imagery running through the chapter: put off the old man, put on the new (4:22–24). Bitterness is treated as something *worn* or *carried*, an object with weight, not a mood that visits. You can't be commanded to stop having a feeling; you can be commanded to stop keeping something.

## Why forgiveness is the precise opposite

Because both are practices of accounting. The verb in v. 32 is *charizomai* — from *charis*, grace — meaning to remit, to release a debt as a gift. It presupposes a real debt. Paul is not saying nothing happened to you.

Bitterness is the other way of handling a real debt: keep it, keep it liquid, keep the interest accruing, rehearse the sum. Both bitterness and forgiveness are responses to genuine injury, and both involve memory. They differ in what the memory is *for*. This is why the pairing is so tight — they are rival dispositions toward the same wound, not a feeling versus a non-feeling.

Note also the counter-triad in v. 32: **kind** (*chrēstos*, a taste word, wholesome flavor) against *pikria*, bitter taste; **tenderhearted** (*eusplanchnos*, "good-gutted," compassion located in the viscera) against hardening; **forgiving** against malice hoarded.

## So: feeling, habit, posture, or contagion?

The text seems to say: all four, in that order, and it names the hinge six verses earlier.

> "Be ye angry, and sin not: let not the sun go down upon your wrath: **neither give place to the devil.**" (4:26–27)

*Place* is τόπος — a foothold, a room, occupied ground. The mechanism is temporal. Anger at genuine wrong isn't condemned; anger *kept overnight* is where the trouble begins. A feeling given lodging becomes a habit of thought; a habit rehearsed becomes a posture toward a person; a posture held long enough stops feeling like a choice at all and starts feeling like clear sight. That's the deceptive thing about bitterness — it doesn't present itself as an emotion. It presents itself as accurate perception. Which is exactly what a taste does: *pikria* isn't a judgment about one event, it's a flavor over everything you subsequently swallow.

So bitterness is what unforgiveness becomes when it acquires tenure.

## The contagion question

Two things support your instinct here.

First, the context is relentlessly corporate. The whole chapter is about the body — joints, ligaments, growth, "we are members one of another" (4:25). Every vice listed is one that fragments a body, and the list terminates in *speech*, which is the transmission medium. Bitterness has to be told. It recruits. It seeks the sympathetic ear and calls it processing.

Second, Hebrews 12:15 — "lest any root of bitterness springing up trouble you, and thereby many be defiled." Worth being honest about that verse, though: it's quoting Deuteronomy 29:18, where the "root bearing gall and wormwood" is a *person* who turns away to idols. So the Hebrews warning may be less about an emotion spreading and more about a bitter person becoming the root. Which, if anything, sharpens the point — the contagion isn't airborne, it's personal. Someone becomes it.

## The ground of the command

"Even as God for Christ's sake hath forgiven you" — the verb is aorist, completed. You aren't forgiving *in order to* be forgiven; you're forgiving out of an account already settled. This is the logic of Matthew 18: the unforgiving servant's sin isn't that he failed a test, it's that he received an incalculable remission and let it stop moving at him. Bitterness, on this reading, is grace arrested in transit — mercy that arrived and terminated.

And the "one another" of v. 32 is *allēlous*, reciprocal, mutual. The vice list isolates; the virtue list can only be performed in company. You cannot practice forgiveness alone, which may be the quiet reason bitterness so often prefers solitude.
