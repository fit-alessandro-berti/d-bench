In Matthew 12, Jesus is not giving general advice about manners. The Pharisees have just watched him heal and deliver people and explained it away as demonic — he casts out demons “by Beelzebul” (12:24). Jesus treats that sentence not as a slip of the tongue, but as evidence.

That is the logic of vv. 34-35, framed by v. 33 — “a tree is known by its fruit” — and vv. 36-37 — “by thy words thou shalt be justified, and by thy words thou shalt be condemned.”

He uses two related images for the heart.

### 1. The heart as an overflow

> “out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaketh”

In biblical language the *heart* is not just emotion. It is the inner control center: thoughts, loves, desires, convictions, resentments, worship.

“Abundance” is *perisseuma* — surplus, overflow. The picture is hydraulic. Whatever fills the inner life to excess eventually pressurizes and spills out through speech. You can police the mouth for a while, but under pressure, fatigue, anger, contempt, pride, or joy, what is full inside comes out.

That is why Jesus says to the Pharisees, “how can ye, being evil, speak good things?” He does not mean an evil person cannot pronounce a technically correct or polite sentence. He means sustained, genuine, life-giving speech cannot come from a reservoir set against God. Their blasphemy was not an accident; it was overflow.

### 2. The heart as a treasury

> “A good man out of the good treasure of the heart bringeth forth good things: and an evil man out of the evil treasure bringeth forth evil things.”

“Treasury” is *thesaurus* — a storehouse, vault, stockpile. This adds accumulation to the picture. The heart is not only full; it is *stocked* over time.

What a person dwells on, loves, rehearses, envies, forgives, worships, consumes, and practices gets deposited. Speech then withdraws from that account. A person stocked with gratitude brings forth thanksgiving. A person stocked with contempt brings forth mockery. A person stocked with Scripture, mercy, and truth has something good to draw on; a person stocked with bitterness and self-justification does not.

So words are connected to hidden moral stockpiles in two ways: the heart is **filled** until it overflows, and it is **stocked** until it is drawn upon.

### Symptoms, choices, or revelations? All three.

**1. Words are symptoms.**
Like fruit symptoms of a tree’s nature, speech diagnoses the inner condition. Especially unguarded speech — jokes, grumbling, quick judgments, what we say about enemies, what we call good and evil — reveals what is abundant inside. The Pharisees’ words revealed that their opposition to Jesus was not intellectual confusion but settled spiritual hostility.

**2. Words are choices.**
Jesus does not use only passive language. A man “bringeth forth” from the treasure. There is retrieval and agency. We choose what to stockpile — by attention, habit, repentance, prayer, and obedience — and we choose, ordinarily, what to bring out. That is why v. 36 holds people accountable even for “every idle word.” The treasury explains speech; it does not excuse it.

There are actually choices at two points: *stocking* the heart, and *spending* from it.

**3. Words are revelations.**
What was hidden becomes public. Speech unveils the heart to hearers, to the community, to ourselves, and finally before God in judgment. The Pharisees thought they were explaining Jesus; Jesus says they were exposing themselves. Their interpretation of his miracles revealed their own treasure.

### The point

Jesus is arguing against mere tongue-management. If speech is overflow and withdrawal, then foul, cruel, blasphemous, deceitful, or contemptuous speech is not first a mouth problem. It is a heart-inventory problem.

Change the treasure, and the fruit changes. Guard the mouth alone, and the pressure remains. According to this passage, your words are never just words: they are symptoms of what fills you, choices drawn from what you have stored, and revelations of who you actually are.
