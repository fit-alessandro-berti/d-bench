The either/or is worth complicating. Jeremiah is not offering a vague cynicism about people (“everyone is awful”), nor is he merely offering a psychological observation (“watch out for bias”). He is making a precise theological claim: **the default mode of the human “heart” is self-justifying, and therefore the one place we cannot get a neutral report from is ourselves.**

In biblical Hebrew, the *heart* (*lev*) is not just the seat of feelings; it is the integrated core of the person—will, mind, desire, and moral reasoning. The word translated “deceitful” (*‘aqov*) suggests something twisted, crooked, or treacherous. The rhetorical question “who can know it?” assumes the answer is *no one*—or rather, *no human*, including the person whose heart it is. The heart does not merely hide things from others; it hides things from its owner by narrating itself into innocence.

So in one sense it is **realistic about self-deception**, but the realism is severe enough to be pessimistic about *autonomous* moral self-knowledge. We are very good at telling believable stories about our own goodness. Because of that, the passage does not say “try harder to introspect”; it immediately turns the searchlight outward and upward: *I the Lord search the heart… according to his ways, according to the fruit of his doings* (v. 10). The standard is not better inner feelings, but visible fruit under the gaze of a reliable examiner.

**If the heart is an unreliable witness, how should we talk about motives?**

1. **Trade confidence for humility.** 
   “I think my motive was generosity, but I could be fooling myself” is more honest than “I know I acted out of pure love.” Jeremiah gives us permission—and a command—to suspect our own virtue.

2. **Anchor talk in fruit, not just feeling.** 
   The text itself supplies the hermeneutic: God examines by *ways* and *fruit*. When you or someone else claims a motive, ask what pattern of behavior and long-term consequence it produces. A heart’s sincerity is shown over time more reliably than in the moment of action.

3. **Allow for surprise.** 
   If the heart is deceitful, you may be the last person to discover why you really did something. This creates space for community, confession, and even correction: others often see our patterns before we do.

4. **Distinguish self-report from truth.** 
   Modern psychology confirms Jeremiah: we are masters of motivated reasoning and post-hoc rationalization. So when someone (including yourself) explains a motive, treat the explanation as a *lead*, not a verdict.

5. **Let the reliable Reader have the final word.** 
   The passage’s answer to the heart’s unreliability is not despair but substitution. If no one can know the heart, and God does, then the path forward is not deeper introspection but submission to God’s assessment. Talk about your motives *to* God and to trusted others, asking them to help you see what you cannot.

In short: it is realist about self-deception to the point of theological pessimism about the *self-made* self. The cure is not a more honest heart—an impossibility on this text’s terms—but trusting the One who searches it.
