The passage calls for a **restorative, humble, and self-examining moral posture**—not a posture of contempt, avoidance, or self-righteousness.

**1. Restoration, not destruction.**  
The goal of correction is to “restore” the person who has been overtaken in a fault. The word carries the sense of bringing back into right standing, not merely winning a debate or punishing wrongdoing. So the moral posture is *concupiscence*-shaped: the stronger is to be weak, the knowledgeable is to be gentle, and the one who sees the fault is to act as an agent of healing rather than exposure.

**2. Meekness, not mere passivity.**  
The correction is to happen “in the spirit of meekness.” This means the reproof is personal and humble, marked by self-control and concern for the other’s dignity. Meekness here is not weakness or indifference; it is the strength that submits to truth and refuses to dominate. It creates the space in which restoration is possible.

**3. Self-suspicion, not self-righteousness.**  
“Considering thyself, lest thou also be tempted” keeps the corrector in view. The one who corrects must watch himself, because he too is capable of the same fault and is also under temptation. This prevents the moral posture from becoming proud or using correction as a way to feel superior. It is a posture of *suspicion of oneself*, not confidence in oneself.

**Why are the three kept together?**  
Because they belong to a single logic of restoration:

- **Restoration** gives correction its goal: the hearer must be brought back into communion and wholeness.
- **Meekness** gives correction its manner: it must be gentle enough not to crush or humiliate.
- **Self-suspicion** gives correction its humility: the corrector is not a judge standing on a moral high ground but a fellow traveler who also needs grace.

Together they guard against three failures: harshness (which seeks revenge or victory), condemnation (which writes off the offender), and pride (which forgets one’s own need for mercy). The posture is therefore *restorative meekness under self-examination*—truth told gently, by a person who knows how much grace he himself needs.
