The passage is severe about human nature, but its point is not that every motive is evil. It is that we cannot take our own account of our motives as the final word. Verse 10 answers verse 9’s “who can know it?”: what we cannot fully discern, God can. And the reference to “the fruit of his doings” keeps the passage from becoming an invitation to endless introspection. What we do matters, even when why we did it is hard to untangle.

That suggests a way to talk about motives: **be honest without claiming certainty.** Instead of “I only wanted to help,” you might say, “I think I wanted to help, but I may also have wanted to be needed.” And instead of declaring what secretly drove someone else, describe what they did and how it affected you. Patterns of conduct and the observations of people who know us can challenge our self-understanding without settling every question about intent.

The practical lesson is humility, not paralysis: you don’t have to know your heart perfectly to take responsibility for what you did or to change what you do next.
