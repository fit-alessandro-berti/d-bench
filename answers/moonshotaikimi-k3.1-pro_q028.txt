All three are present, but none is sufficient by itself. The deepest disturbance is that **Jesus changes the object of the trial**. The woman is brought in as the defendant; the accusers leave having been judged by their own consciences.

**It is not a blanket prohibition of judgment.** If it meant that no imperfect person may ever distinguish right from wrong, correct another, or administer justice, it would produce moral paralysis—and the passage itself would not cohere. Jesus still says, “Go, and sin no more.” He does not declare wrongdoing insignificant. What he attacks is the movement from *“she has done wrong”* to *“therefore we are innocent, and she may be treated as nothing but her wrong.”*

**It exposes hypocrisy, but also something more dangerous than simple inconsistency.** The accusers are using the woman’s case to test Jesus and obtain grounds against him. She has become useful: a spectacle, a legal problem, and bait. Their zeal is corrupt not merely because they also have sins, but because her sin gives them a way to avoid examining their own. The crowd is seeking not only her punishment but, through it, certification of its own righteousness.

**It also breaks the moral anonymity of the mob.** “Among you” and “first” prevent anyone from hiding behind religious office, the majority, or the name of Moses. Each person must decide in his own name, before his own conscience. The crowd never answers Jesus’ argument; it simply disperses, one by one. Collective indignation had supplied a confidence that individual self-examination could not sustain.

But the scene goes deeper still. **If hypocrisy were the whole problem, a genuinely sinless executioner could solve it. If mob psychology were the whole problem, a calmer procedure could solve it.** On the Christian reading, Jesus is the one without sin—yet he does not take up the stone.

That is why “Neither do I condemn thee” and “Go, and sin no more” must be held together. He refuses the condemnation the crowd seeks without declaring sin unreal. Mercy here is not moral relativism; it is the refusal to make condemnation the woman’s whole future. He addresses her not merely as a case, but as a person still capable of change.

The passage is not a complete theory of law or punishment. It does deny, however, that eagerness to punish is evidence of holiness. It separates accountability from contempt.

Finally, it gives the reader no safe seat. I can turn this very saying into a stone to throw at “the Pharisees” or “judgmental people,” thereby reclaiming the position it unsettles. Its sharpest word is therefore not simply *“Never judge,”* but:

> **Another person’s guilt does not establish your innocence.**
