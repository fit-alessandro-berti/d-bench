The parable gives us a diagnosis, not just a description. The elder brother is **angry**, he is **envious**, he feels **wounded fairness**, and he is plainly **proud of his obedience**. All of those are really there. But they are not all equally deep.

Anger, envy, and wounded fairness are the symptoms.  
Pride in obedience is the root.  
Discomfort with mercy is the final, damning fruit.

### 1. Anger, envy, and wounded fairness are the surface
The elder brother is angry (v. 28). He will not go in. He envies the celebration and the fatted calf given to the returning wastrel. He feels treated unfairly:

> “Lo, these many years do I serve thee, neither transgressed I at any time thy commandment: and yet thou never gavest me a kid…”

His sense of injustice is real to him — but the father’s reply undercuts it completely:

> “Son, thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine” (v. 31).

He has not been cheated. He already has everything — if he would only receive it as a **son** rather than as a wage-earner.

### 2. The root: pride in obedience / a slave’s heart
Notice his language. He says, “I have **served** thee” — the Greek verb is *douleuō*, “I have been slaving for you.” He speaks like a hired servant presenting an invoice, not like a child resting in his father’s love.

He boasts:

> “Neither transgressed I at any time thy commandment…”

Outwardly, he is blameless. But his obedience has become a way of putting the father in his debt. He trusts in his moral résumé rather than in the father’s grace. He is physically in the house, but relationally he is far away. He is “lost at home.”

This is exactly the condition of the Pharisees in Luke 15:1–2: morally respectable, scrupulously obedient, and utterly unable to rejoice that sinners are being welcomed home.

### 3. The bottom line: discomfort with mercy itself
The feast is not fundamentally about a calf. It is about resurrection:

> “this thy brother was dead, and is alive again; and was lost, and is found” (v. 32).

The elder brother will not enter that feast. He even refuses kinship: “this **thy** son” (v. 30), not “my brother.” Mercy to the guilty offends him, because mercy declares that acceptance with the father is **gift**, not wage. Grace dethrones the self-righteousness he has built his whole identity on.

So mercy is not good news to him. It is an insult to his obedience.

---

## The answer

The elder brother’s deepest problem is **not** fundamentally anger, envy, or even wounded fairness. Those are what the disease *feels* like.

His deepest problem is a **self-righteous, slave-hearted “obedience” that cannot bear grace**. He relates to his father as a master and to obedience as a contract, so mercy itself becomes intolerable.

If you must choose one of your labels:

- **“Pride in obedience”** names the root.
- **“Discomfort with mercy itself”** names the final, fatal outcome.

The tragedy of the elder brother is that he kept the commandments but lacked love — love for his father and love for his brother. He was near the father’s house, but far from the father’s heart. And the parable ends with the door open and the father still pleading: *“Son… all that I have is thine. Come in and rejoice.”*
