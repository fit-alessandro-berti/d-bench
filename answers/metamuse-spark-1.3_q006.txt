In Mark 8, that call comes at a sharp turning point.

Jesus has just predicted for the first time that he will suffer and die. Peter pulls him aside to rebuke him — essentially: *No, Messiah, save yourself.* Jesus answers, “Get behind me, Satan,” and then turns to the crowd and says this to *everyone*: Following me means the same road.

That context tells you what kind of “self” he means.

### 1. What self is to be denied?

The Greek is strong: *aparnesastho heauton* — disown, renounce, say “No” to yourself as the final authority.

Jesus is not talking about:

* your personality, body, creativity, or legitimate needs
* healthy selfhood as a human made in God’s image
* self-hatred, ascetic punishment, or staying in abuse

What the New Testament elsewhere calls the “old self,” the “flesh,” the self *curved in on itself* — Augustine and Luther’s famous phrase *incurvatus in se*.

In other words, three interlocked selves:

**1. The self as god:** “I decide what is right, true, and good for me. No one will tell me who to be.” To deny this self is to dethrone it.

**2. The self as savior:** “I will justify myself by my performance, morality, success, image, spirituality.” To deny this self is to stop trying to save your own life.

**3. The self as treasure to be protected at all costs:** “My comfort, reputation, safety, control come first, even over obedience to God and love for neighbor.” To deny this self is to take up a cross — a Roman instrument not of self-improvement but of execution and shame — and follow someone else.

Peter had just modeled all three: he wanted a Messiah without a cross, and disciples without one either. Jesus says: That way of saving life is how you lose it.

Verse 35 plays on *psyche* — life/soul/self: “Whoever wants to save his *psyche* will lose it; but whoever loses his *psyche* for my sake and the gospel’s will save it.”

It’s not annihilation of the self, it’s relocation of the self. Paul puts it later as Galatians 2:20: “I have been crucified with Christ... Christ lives in me.” You lose the false, self-made, self-defended self in order to find your true self in relationship to Christ.

As C.S. Lewis paraphrased it: Give up yourself and you will find your real self. Lose your life and you will save it.

### 2. Why does this sound liberating to some?

To people who experience their “self” not as a friend but as a tyrant, this sounds like good news.

The modern project — create yourself, brand yourself, justify yourself, heal yourself, follow your heart and never let anyone question it — is exhausting. You are always performing, comparing, failing, anxious, guilty, addicted to approval or control.

For them, “deny yourself” doesn’t mean “you don’t matter.” It means:

* You don’t have to be your own god anymore.
* You don’t have to carry the burden of proving your worth.
* You are allowed to let the grasping, fearful, shame-driven ego die because you are held by someone more trustworthy.

Bonhoeffer called this “costly grace.” It costs your autonomy, but it frees you from slavery to desires, to other people’s opinions, to sin, and ultimately to death. The cross is painful, but it’s no longer pointless.

That’s why some hear an invitation: *Come after me.* It’s voluntary — “whoever *will*” — but it’s a path with a Person on it.

### 3. Why does it sound offensive to others?

For two very different reasons, one cultural and one spiritual.

**Culturally,** we live in the age of expressive individualism: Authenticity is the highest virtue. “Be true to yourself, follow your heart, you do you, don’t let anyone tell you to deny who you are.”

Heard through that lens, Jesus sounds abusive, repressive, even dangerous — a call to self-erasure, to submit to religious power, to glorify suffering. Taking up a cross sounds like the opposite of liberation, especially to anyone who has been told to deny themselves so others could control them.

**Spiritually,** Jesus offends because he doesn’t offer improvement, he demands death. Most of us, like Peter, would prefer a Messiah who affirms our plans, removes our pain, and leaves us in charge. Jesus insists the problem is deeper than bad habits or low self-esteem. The self that wants to run life without God has to be crucified, not coached.

That is scandalous if you still trust that self to save you. If you believe your autonomy *is* your dignity, surrender feels like humiliation. If you believe your life is yours to save, losing it for Christ and the gospel feels like waste.

In the end the dividing line is the one Jesus names: Do you experience the self you’re being asked to deny as life itself — or as the thing that’s losing you your life?

Those who have felt it as a burden hear freedom. Those who still see it as their salvation hear offense.
