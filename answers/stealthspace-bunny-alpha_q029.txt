The posture required is **humble courage**: correction must be firm about the fault, but gentle toward the person and free of any claim to moral superiority. Paul is not offering a choice between lenient silence and punitive severity. He is calling the church to responsible, restorative correction.

### 1. Restoration: the purpose is to heal, not to punish

“Restore” means to bring someone back—out of the damaging course of sin and into a sound relationship with God and the community. The wrongdoing is treated as something that needs mending, not as a debt to be repaid through humiliation.

Thefault must be confronted; otherwise “restoration” can become mere indulgence. But the person’s eternal and relational good, rather than the corrector’s reputation or satisfaction, governs the action.

### 2. Meekness: the manner of correction matters

Paul specifies a “spirit of meekness.” Meekness is not weakness or a willingness to leave sin untouched. It is **gentleness without self-importance**: speaking the truth without contempt, correcting without domineering, and seeking another person’s recovery rather than their humiliation.

This qualification is essential. Even a correct rebuke can be morally corrupt if it serves pride, rivalry, or public shame. Right conduct of correction is therefore not separate from the spirit in which it is done; harshness can destroy what genuine correction is meant to accomplish.

### 3. Self-suspicion: the corrector is not morally exempt

“Considering thyself, lest thou also be tempted” places the corrector under the same moral scrutiny as the person being corrected. There is no elevated platform from which one can expose another’s weakness while claiming complete immunity from it.

This is not constant self-condemnation or the assumption that one has secretly committed the same wrong. It is a sober recognition of one’s own vulnerability: **the capacity to judge another honestly and the capacity to fall are not opposites.**

### Why these belong together

They protect correction from two distortions:

- **Restoration without meekness** can become domination or revenge.
- **Meekness without self-suspicion** can drift into passivity, sentimentality, or an assumed immunity from correction.

The other side of those failures is equally important: self-righteousness without restoration becomes mere condemnation, while self-suspicion without firm correction can become moral paralysis.

So Paul holds together **purpose, manner, and posture**. The aim is restoration; the spirit is meekness; the stance of the corrector is humble, fallible solidarity. Those who correct their brothers and sisters do so not as spotless supervisors, but as fellow travelers willing to speak the truth, bear one another’s burdens, and guard themselves from the same temptation they confront in the other.
