**It is realistic about self-deception (and the limits of unaided self-knowledge), not merely pessimistic.** Jeremiah 17:9–10 diagnoses a real human condition: the heart (inner person, motives, affections, will) is “deceitful above all things” and “desperately sick/wicked” (translations vary: incurably ill, beyond cure). The rhetorical question “who can know it?” underscores that we are not reliable, exhaustive witnesses to our own motives. God alone searches the heart and tests the mind (“reins”), then evaluates by actual ways and the fruit of deeds.

This is not isolated cynicism. It sits in a chapter contrasting trust in man (cursed) with trust in the Lord (blessed, like a tree by water). The point is diagnostic and theocentric: humans are prone to self-justification, hidden idols, and mixed motives; therefore we need God to reveal and judge what we cannot fully see. Parallel texts (Psalm 139:23–24; Proverbs 21:2; Jeremiah 17:5–8; the new-heart promises in Ezekiel 36 and Jeremiah 31) treat this as reason for dependence on God rather than despair or license.

### Talking about motives when the heart is an unreliable witness
- **Practice epistemic humility.** Own that introspection is fallible. “I think my motive is X” is a hypothesis, not a verdict. People routinely rationalize, forget inconvenient data, or recast self-interest as virtue. This matches both the biblical claim and everyday observation (confirmation bias, motivated reasoning).
- **Look at patterns of action and fruit over time**, exactly as the verse does. Motives are inferred more reliably from consistent behavior, costs paid, and outcomes than from momentary feelings or self-reports. Jesus’ “by their fruits you will know them” and James’ faith-without-works point in the same direction. Isolated good intentions or one-off explanations carry less weight than trajectories.
- **Use external checks.** Scripture functions as a mirror that exposes what we miss (Hebrews 4:12). Trusted community, accountability, and even adversarial feedback can reveal blind spots. Prayer that God would search and try the heart (as in Psalm 139) treats self-knowledge as something received, not generated in isolation.
- **Distinguish levels.** Surface motives can be relatively accessible; deeper, mixed, or self-serving ones often are not. You can still speak of motives—“I intended to help,” “I was angry,” “I wanted approval”—while adding the qualifier that fuller truth may emerge later or only before God. Avoid both naïve “follow your heart” language and paralyzing suspicion that nothing can ever be known.
- **Judgment belongs to God.** The passage ends with divine searching and recompense according to ways and fruit. Humans can and should evaluate conduct and apparent character; we cannot claim exhaustive knowledge of another’s (or even our own) heart. This undercuts both self-righteousness and the demand that people endlessly prove inner purity.

In short, the text is a warning against credulity toward the inner voice and a pointer toward God’s knowledge plus observable fruit. It supports cautious, evidence-based talk about motives rather than either romantic trust in the heart or total skepticism. Different theological traditions (Reformed emphasis on remaining sin, Wesleyan emphasis on grace enabling greater self-awareness, etc.) develop the implications differently, but the core claim—that unaided self-knowledge is limited and God is the true searcher—is shared.
