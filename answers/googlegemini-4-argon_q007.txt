Luke 6:37—along with its parallel in Matthew 7:1—is perhaps the most frequently quoted, and misunderstood, verse in the modern world. It is often used as a shield to deflect any form of moral critique, implying that Jesus is advocating for absolute moral relativism where no one can ever say that an action is wrong.

However, a closer look at the text, the Greek language, and the broader context of Jesus’s teachings reveals a profound difference between **moral discernment** and **the appetite to condemn**. 

Here is how Jesus’s command differs from the idea that moral judgments never matter, followed by an exploration of discernment versus condemnation.

---

### Part 1: Why this does not mean "Moral Judgments Never Matter"

To say that moral judgments never matter is to render the rest of Luke 6—and the entire Bible—nonsensical. We know Jesus is not banning moral evaluation for three main reasons:

**1. Forgiveness requires a moral judgment.**
Notice the end of the verse: *"...forgive, and ye shall be forgiven."* You cannot forgive someone unless they have done something wrong. If moral judgments didn't matter, there would be nothing to forgive; you would simply excuse or ignore the behavior. True forgiveness requires looking at an action, rightly judging it as an offense or a sin, and then choosing to release the offender from the debt. 

**2. The immediate context requires moral evaluation.**
Just a few verses later, Jesus says, *"For every tree is known by his own fruit"* (Luke 6:44). He is telling his followers to evaluate the moral character and teachings of others based on their actions ("fruit"). In the parallel passage in Matthew 7, right after saying "judge not," Jesus says, "Do not give dogs what is sacred" and "Watch out for false prophets." How can you spot a false prophet or a "dog" without making a moral judgment?

**3. Jesus clarifies *how* to judge elsewhere.**
In John 7:24, Jesus explicitly commands moral evaluation: *"Do not judge according to appearance, but judge with righteous judgment."* Furthermore, in Luke 6:41-42, Jesus uses the metaphor of taking the "plank" out of your own eye so that you can see clearly to remove the "speck" from your brother’s eye. The ultimate goal is still to help your brother remove the speck (which requires judging that a speck is there), not pretending the speck doesn't exist.

---

### Part 2: Discernment vs. The Appetite to Condemn

Notice the structure of Luke 6:37. Jesus uses Hebrew parallelism, where the second phrase clarifies the first: *"Judge not... condemn not."* In this context, "judging" is synonymous with *condemning*. 

Understanding the difference between healthy discernment and the toxic appetite to condemn is the key to unlocking this verse.

#### 1. The Posture of the Heart: Pride vs. Humility
* **The Appetite to Condemn** is rooted in pride. It looks at another person's moral failure and feels a secret thrill of superiority. As the Pharisee prayed in Luke 18:11, *"God, I thank thee, that I am not as other men are."* We condemn others to make ourselves feel righteous. 
* **Discernment** is rooted in humility. It recognizes a moral failure in someone else, not from a pedestal, but from a place of shared human frailty. Discernment says, "That action is wrong and destructive, and were it not for the grace of God, I would be doing the exact same thing."

#### 2. The Goal: Destruction vs. Restoration
* **The Appetite to Condemn** seeks to write a person off. In the Greek, the word for condemn is *katadikazō*, which means to pass final sentence upon. When we have an appetite to condemn, we reduce a complex human being down to their worst mistake. We don't want them to get better; we want them to be punished or socially exiled so our own moral tribe feels secure.
* **Discernment** seeks restoration. It separates the person's ultimate worth from their current behavior. Like a doctor diagnosing a disease, discernment identifies the "sin" or moral failing specifically so that healing can take place. 

#### 3. The Scope: Assuming Motives vs. Evaluating Actions
* **The Appetite to Condemn** presumes to know the human heart. It looks at an action and immediately assigns the darkest possible motive to the actor. By doing this, the condemner usurps the throne of God, who alone knows the secrets of the heart.
* **Discernment** stays in its lane. It evaluates visible actions ("fruits") and measures them against an objective standard of truth, while leaving the ultimate judgment of the person’s soul and unseen motives to God. 

#### 4. The Response to the Truth: Gossip vs. Grace
* **The Appetite to Condemn** feeds on the failures of others. When someone falls, the condemning heart is eager to broadcast it, to cancel the person, and to hold their past over their head indefinitely.
* **Discernment** is grieved by moral failure. When it sees a wrong, it moves toward the boundary of love: sometimes that means setting a firm boundary to protect the innocent, sometimes it means speaking a hard truth in private, and, as Luke 6:37 concludes, it means standing ready to **forgive**.

### Conclusion
Jesus is not telling his followers to turn off their brains and pretend that good and evil do not exist. Rather, he is diagnosing a dark sickness in the human heart: our addiction to playing God in the lives of others. 

Discernment is using a flashlight to see the path clearly so you and your neighbor don't fall into a ditch. The appetite to condemn is using that flashlight as a weapon to blind your neighbor and beat them over the head. Luke 6:37 forbids the weapon, so that we might rightly use the light.
