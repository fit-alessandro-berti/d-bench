It is hard to take “Love your enemies” seriously because it seems to demand the impossible. We hear it and think: *You can’t command feelings.* You can’t decide to have warm affection for someone who has hurt you. And if love is a feeling, then Jesus’ command is unrealistic, even sentimental.

But Jesus is not commanding a feeling. He is describing a way of life. Notice what He says: *bless, do good, pray.* Those are actions, not emotions. He is talking about what love does, not what love feels like. That changes the question.

So why is enemy-love so hard to take seriously?

## We confuse love with liking

We naturally assume that love means having positive feelings toward someone. But the love Jesus speaks of is closer to willing good for someone. It means seeking the true flourishing of another person, even an enemy. Liking may be impossible; willing good is hard but possible, and it is the kind of love God has.

God makes the sun rise on the evil and the good. God sends rain on the just and the unjust. The love of God is not sentimental; it is generous and real. It does not pretend there is no difference between evil and good. But it refuses to make its kindness depend on whether someone deserves it.

## It cuts against our moral instincts

There is something in us that wants the world to be fair. We want good to be rewarded and evil to be punished. Enemy-love seems unfair. It seems to let evil off the hook. To love an enemy feels like betraying justice, or like saying the evil was not really evil.

But Jesus is not saying evil does not matter. He is saying that the way God responds to evil is not simply by returning it. God answers evil with mercy and patience, hoping for repentance. Enemy-love is not the approval of evil; it is the refusal to let evil dictate how we respond.

That is why it is hard to take seriously. It asks us to break the cycle of retaliation. And breaking a cycle always looks like weakness before it reveals itself as strength.

## Is it sentimental?

No. Sentimentality is cheap. It is a warm feeling that costs nothing and changes nothing. But Jesus’ command costs everything. It asks you to bless those who curse you. It asks you to do good to those who hate you. It asks you to pray for those who abuse you and persecute you.

That is not sweet or vague. It is specific and demanding. Praying for one’s enemies is one of the hardest things a human being can do, because it forces us to see the enemy as a person in front of God, not as a monster in our story. It does not deny the pain. It simply refuses to let hatred have the final word.

## Is it strategic?

It can be strategically powerful. Jesus’ way of love has been used by people like Martin Luther King Jr. and Desmond Tutu as a strategy for social change. Nonviolent love has moved nations. But if enemy-love is only a strategy, it is fragile. It will only last as long as it seems effective. When loving an enemy does not produce quick results, we will abandon it and call it impractical.

Jesus does not ground enemy-love in success. He grounds it in the character of God. He says: *that ye may be the children of your Father.* The motive is not “this will work.” The motive is “this is who God is, and therefore who you are called to become.”

So yes, it may have strategic effects. But it is not strategic at its root. It is sacrificial.

## It is deeper moral strength

The real answer is that enemy-love is a deeper form of moral strength.

It is easy to return an insult. It is harder to absorb it and answer with blessing. It is easy to hate those who hate you. It is harder to keep your heart from being poisoned by revenge. It is easy to enjoy the downfall of an enemy. It is harder to pray for their repentance and transformation.

Enemy-love requires:

- courage, because it risks being mocked;
- self-mastery, because it controls the impulse to strike back;
- perception, because it sees the image of God in someone who has damaged it;
- hope, because it believes people can change;
- and a deep sense of security, because it does not need other people’s pain to feel safe.

This is not weakness. It is the strength of the cross. Jesus responded to violence not with violence, but with forgiveness. He absorbed evil without returning it. That is not passive submission; it is active, creative resistance to the power of hatred.

## What it does not mean

Loving an enemy does not mean we cannot protect the innocent, resist evil, or set boundaries. It does not mean being a doormat. Justice and self-defense can be necessary. But the way we pursue them matters. Enemy-love means that even when we oppose evil, we do not dehumanize the person. We do not wish them evil. We keep open the possibility of their restoration.

This is why Jesus says it is the mark of God’s children. God’s generosity is not limited to those who are good to Him. His care reaches everyone. To be a child of the Father is to love like the Father, not just to love people we already love.

So: enemy-love is not sentimental, because it costs too much. It is not merely strategic, because it is rooted in who God is. It is a deeper form of moral strength, because it requires everything we have — our will, our imagination, our heart, and our trust in God. And it is hard to take seriously because it asks us to become more than ourselves.

But that is exactly what the Gospel offers: the power to become children of the Father who makes His sun rise on the evil and on the good.
