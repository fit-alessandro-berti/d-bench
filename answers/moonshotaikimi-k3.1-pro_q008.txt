John’s point is not simply that a visible brother is easier to love than an invisible God. It is that love for God and love for one’s brother cannot be separated without falsifying the profession.

In 1 John, God’s love is not a vague religious feeling but self-giving action revealed in Christ (4:9–10). Those who are “born of God” share in and reproduce that love (4:7). Thus John can say, “If we love one another, God dwelleth in us” (4:12), and he immediately adds the command that whoever loves God must love his brother also (4:21). Brotherly love is therefore neither a substitute for loving God nor a means of earning God’s favor; it is a necessary fruit and concrete expression of God’s love at work in someone.

The brother’s visibility makes the claim **accountable**. “I love God” can be surrounded by worship, correct doctrine, religious emotion, and sincere inward conviction. But the person before me has needs, faults, and claims on my patience, forgiveness, and assistance. There the profession meets resistance: will I actually practice the self-giving love I attribute to God? This fits John’s contrast between loving “in word” and loving “in deed and in truth” (3:18). Outward conduct is not an infallible window into every heart, but settled hatred, contempt, or indifference cannot simply be declared compatible with love for God.

The self-deception exposed is therefore **compartmentalized religion**: imagining that one can be rightly related to God while exempting actual people from love. More subtly, a person may love an abstraction called “God”—a God who confirms his religious self-image but never challenges his hostility—rather than the God John describes, the God who *is* love and who commands love. One can cherish the idea of being loving while refusing its concrete cost.

The “liar” need not be a conscious hypocrite. He may sincerely believe his own claim; that is precisely the self-deception. Sincerity does not make the contradiction coherent.

So the visible neighbor is not merely a convenient test case. He reveals whether “I love God” names a life being reshaped by God’s love, or a religious assertion insulated from obedience. John is not demanding flawless love—his letter also calls believers to confess their sins—but he denies that unrepentant hatred can peacefully coexist with the profession of loving God.
