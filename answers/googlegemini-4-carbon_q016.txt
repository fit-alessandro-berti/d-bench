All five traits you named—anger, envy, wounded fairness, pride in obedience, and discomfort with mercy—are undeniably present in the text. However, they relate to one another like symptoms to a disease. 

If we trace those symptoms down through the layers of the elder brother’s speech, we find that **his deepest problem is not merely moral or emotional, subtle though those flaws are; it is relational.** Beneath his discomfort with mercy lies a **transactional heart that views sonship as slavery**—an alienation from the Father so profound that, just like his younger brother, he wants the Father’s *things* without wanting the Father *himself*.

Here is how the layers peel back in Luke 15:28–32 to reveal that root:

---

### Layer 1: The Surface Symptoms (Anger and Envy)
> *“And he was angry, and would not go in… thou never gavest me a kid… thou hast killed for him the fatted calf.”*

Anger and envy are the immediate triggers. Anger flares because his world has been violated; envy stings because someone else received the celebration he quietly craved. Yet people only get angry when something they value is threatened. What did the feast threaten?

### Layer 2: The Moral Engine (Pride in Obedience & Wounded Fairness)
> *“Lo, these many years do I serve thee, neither transgressed I at any time thy commandment…”*

Beneath his anger is a rigid courtroom ledger. He boasts of flawless compliance (*pride in obedience*) and protests an unpaid debt (*wounded fairness*). In his mind, goodness is a wage-earning enterprise: *I put in obedience; you pay out blessings.* 

### Layer 3: The Theological Crisis (Discomfort with Mercy)
Why does the Father’s mercy offend him so deeply? Because **mercy bankrupts the currency of merit.** If a ruined rebel can come home smelling of swine and instantly receive the fatted calf—the highest honor in the household—then the elder brother’s decades of meticulous scorekeeping lose their leverage. He isn't just uncomfortable with mercy; he feels *robbed* by it, because grace renders his spiritual résumé useless as a bargaining chip.

---

### The Deepest Root: Lost in Sonship (The Heart of a Slave)

Even "discomfort with mercy" is the result of something deeper: **the elder brother does not actually know his Father.** Three clues in the text expose this foundational tragedy:

**1. He sees himself as a slave, not a son.**
When he says, *"Lo, these many years do I serve thee,"* the underlying Greek verb is *douleuō*—"I have slaved for you." He does not speak like an heir who loves his father; he speaks like a resentful day-laborer enduring a harsh boss. That is why the Father’s gentle correction begins with a title of deep affection: *"Son* [literally *teknon*, 'Dear child'], *thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine."* The elder brother was starving for a goat when he already owned the whole herd—yet his transactional mindset blinded him to his inheritance.

**2. Like the prodigal, he wants the Father’s possessions without the Father.**
Look closely at his complaint: *"thou never gavest me a kid, that I might make merry **with my friends**."* Notice who is missing from his dream party: *his Father*. 
* The **younger brother** took his share of the estate and ran to a far country to enjoy the father’s wealth apart from the father.
* The **elder brother** stayed home and obeyed every rule, hoping to earn a piece of the estate to enjoy apart from the father. 

Both sons used the Father as a means to an end. One tried to get the Father’s goods by rebellion; the other tried to get them by compliance. Neither delighted in simply *being with* him.

**3. Having lost his Father, he loses his brother.**
He refuses to say "my brother," sneering instead at *"this thy son."* When our relationship with the Father is reduced to cold merit, human relationships inevitably fracture into superiority and contempt. (Compare this with Luke 15:1–2, where the Pharisees grumble against Jesus for eating with sinners—the very audience for whom Jesus crafted this mirror.)

---

### Summary
* **Anger and envy** are his *emotional reactions*.
* **Wounded fairness and pride in obedience** are his *worldview*.
* **Discomfort with mercy** is his *theological crisis*.
* **Alienation from the Father’s love** is his *deepest problem*.

The elder brother’s tragedy is that he was lost without ever leaving home. Because he viewed obedience as a grim price paid to extract favors from a master, he missed the joy of the Father’s presence—making mercy look like an injustice, obedience feel like drudgery, and a resurrected brother look like a rival.
