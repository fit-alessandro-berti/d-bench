The problem is the craving, not the visibility, and the grammar of the verse says so. The warning is not against doing alms "before men" but against doing them "*to be seen* of them." That is a purpose clause: it names the aim of the act, not its circumstances.

This reading is confirmed by Matthew 5:16, where Jesus commands visibility: "Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father which is in heaven." The two verses contradict each other unless the difference lies in what the act is for. In 5:16, being seen points beyond the doer to God. In 6:1, being seen ends with the doer. Augustine put it this way: the fault is not in being seen, but in acting in order to be seen.

Public goodness is dangerous for three reasons:

1. **It turns a gift into a transaction.** If I give in order to be admired, the admiration is my payment. The next verse says such people "have their reward," using a commercial term that means roughly "paid in full." The verse does not describe God as withholding a reward out of spite. Nothing is left to reward, because the act has already bought what it was aimed at.

2. **It turns the recipient into a prop.** Almsgiving is supposed to be about the person in need. When the real audience is the onlookers, the poor person becomes the means to my reputation. The act looks the same from the outside, but its real beneficiary is me.

3. **It turns righteousness into theater.** The Greek word for "to be seen" (*theathēnai*) shares a root with "theater," and the "hypocrites" of verse 2 are literally stage actors. The danger is a self that exists only in performance, good only when watched.

The verse also implies that secrecy alone doesn't cure this. Verse 3 says, "let not thy left hand know what thy right hand doeth," which suggests that even being your own admiring audience is a version of the same problem. Someone can give anonymously and still savor their own humility. So the verse doesn't regulate where goodness happens. It asks who the act is for.
