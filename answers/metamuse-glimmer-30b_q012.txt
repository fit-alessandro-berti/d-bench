Jeremiah 17:9-10 is less a blanket condemnation of human nature than a statement about human self-knowledge. The context is key.

The verses come right after 17:5-8 where Jeremiah contrasts two kinds of people: the one who trusts in man and whose heart departs from the LORD, and the one who trusts in the LORD and is like a tree planted by water. The heart is deceitful because self-trust is deceitful. 

In Hebrew, *lev* does not mean just emotions. It is the center of will, intellect, perception and moral orientation — the seat of a person. To say the *lev* is *‘aqob* — deceitful, crooked, twisted — and *‘anash* — literally incurably sick, desperately diseased — is not to say humans are worthless. It is to say the human self is the worst witness about itself. We are very good at rationalizing, editing, and flattering our own motives. “Who can know it?” is a rhetorical question about *us*, not about God.

That is why verse 10 follows immediately: “I the LORD search the heart, I try the reins.” The problem of unknowability is not absolute. It is a problem for self-knowledge and for other people trying to know us from the outside. God is the only reliable reader of the heart, and he judges “according to his ways, and according to the fruit of his doings.”

That moves the passage from pessimism to realism. It is realistic about self-deception. The biblical anthropology is not that there is nothing good in people, but that the inner self is not a neutral or trustworthy courtroom for itself. Jeremiah says the same thing elsewhere: people cry “peace, peace” when there is no peace, 6:14; they say “we are wise” while rejecting the word of the LORD, 8:9.

### What that means for talking about motives

If the heart is an unreliable witness for itself, then motive talk should be marked by epistemic humility.

* **Speak provisionally about yourself.** First person language is safer than third person certainty. “I felt defensive when that was said, and I’m trying to see if that was about pride” is more honest than “I acted out of pure love.” The first admits the heart’s opacity; the second pretends it isn’t.

* **Let fruit and pattern do the talking.** Jeremiah 17:10 points to “ways” and “fruit of doings.” In practice that means we can and should discuss actions, repeated patterns, impact on others, and consistency over time, without claiming infallible access to the hidden why. The New Testament picks this up in Matthew 7:16, “by their fruit you will recognize them.”

* **Invite external discernment.** Because self-knowledge is limited, the healthiest motive talk happens in community and under God’s searching. Psalm 139:23-24 is the prayer that follows Jeremiah’s diagnosis: “Search me, O God, and know my heart... see if there be any wicked way in me.” That is confession, not self-exoneration.

* **Hold judgments about others lightly.** If your own heart is deceptive, you have little warrant to declare someone else’s motive with certainty. You can describe what you observed and how it affected you, and leave the final verdict to the one who truly searches hearts.

In short, the passage is realistic about self-deception, not a counsel of despair. It warns against self-justification and invites humble, outward-facing accountability: examine the fruit, tell the truth about your own limits, and trust the Lord to judge the heart you cannot fully know yourself.
