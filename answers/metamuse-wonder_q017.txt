Light seems like an obvious good. It helps us see, find our way, avoid danger. Why would anyone *hate* it?

John's answer is unsettling: light is hated not because of what it allows us to see, but because of what it allows to be seen *in us*.

> Men loved darkness rather than light, because their deeds were evil.

The condemnation is not simply that people do bad things. It is that, having done them, they prefer the conditions under which those things can remain unexamined.

### Truth is exposure

The Greek word for truth, *aletheia*, carries the sense of unconcealment — something brought out of hiddenness. In John, truth is not merely correct information. It is reality faced without disguise.

That is why verse 21 does not say, "he that *believes* truth," but "he that *doeth* truth cometh to the light." Truth is a way of living openly. To do truth is to let one's life be what it appears to be, and to let it be examined as it actually is.

Light, then, does more than illuminate the path ahead. It makes deeds "manifest." It ends ambiguity.

In darkness, we can maintain several comforting vaguenesses at once:

- I am not really that kind of person.
- It was not as bad as it looked.
- No one was really hurt.
- I had no choice.
- If others knew the whole story, they would understand.

Light threatens those negotiations. It does not merely accuse; it clarifies. And clarity is often what we fear most.

### Shame and the wish not to be known

This is where shame enters.

Guilt says, "I have done something wrong." Shame says, "Something is wrong with me, and if I am seen clearly, I will be diminished, rejected, or exposed as unlovable."

Shame therefore instinctively hides. After eating the forbidden fruit, Adam and Eve do not first build an argument; they sew fig leaves and hide among the trees. It is the Bible's first portrait of shame: the sudden inability to be naked — to be known — and unafraid.

We still do this. We curate what others see. We confess selectively. We admit small faults to conceal larger ones. We may even become skilled at appearing transparent while keeping the decisive things in darkness.

The desire is not always crude villainy. Sometimes it is wounded self-protection. A person may avoid the light not because he delights in evil, but because he cannot imagine being fully known and still accepted.

Yet John says this hiding becomes a love of darkness. What begins as fear becomes preference, and preference becomes habit. Darkness lets us retain both our deeds and our image. Light would force us to surrender one of them.

### We want to see without being seen

There is a paradox in us: we want truth about everything else, but managed truth about ourselves.

We want light on other people's motives, on institutions, on the world — so long as the beam does not turn. We want insight without exposure, conviction for others without reproof for ourselves.

The word translated "reproved" in verse 20 means to be exposed, convicted, shown for what something is. That is the pain of light. Before it heals, it reveals. Before it guides, it judges our hiding places.

To come to the light therefore requires more than intellectual honesty. It requires moral vulnerability: the willingness to be known by God, by others, and perhaps most painfully, by oneself.

For we hide in three directions at once. We hide from God behind religious language. We hide from others behind reputation. We hide from ourselves behind rationalization. Darkness without is sustained by darkness within.

### Why the one who "does truth" can come

John does not say that the one who comes to the light has perfect deeds to display. He says his deeds are made manifest "that they are wrought in God."

That is a crucial difference. Coming to the light is not presenting a flawless record. It is ceasing to manage the record. It is allowing God to be the source and judge of what is in one's life — the good as grace, the evil as something to be confessed and healed rather than protected.

The evil-doer hates the light because exposure would reprove him. The truth-doer comes to the light because, however painful exposure may be, he wants reality more than concealment. His deepest allegiance is not to his image, but to the truth.

This is why Nicodemus, who appears just before this passage, is such a fitting illustration. He first comes to Jesus "by night" (John 3:2) — interested, sincere, but not yet willing to be publicly associated with him. By the end of the Gospel, he helps bury Jesus openly. His story is a slow movement from night toward light.

That movement is the movement of conversion itself: not from being unseen to being shamed, but from hiding to being known.

Light is hated, finally, because it offers us a choice we would rather avoid. We can keep the darkness and preserve the illusion of control. Or we can step into the light and risk being seen as we are — which is terrifying, but it is also the only place where we can be changed, forgiven, and truly known.
