

It would be a mistake to read Luke 6:37 as meaning that moral judgments never matter. In fact, the verse presupposes that moral judgments do matter: it speaks of judging, being judged, condemning, and forgiving. If moral judgments never mattered, there would be nothing to forgive. There would be no guilt, no offense, no debt, no mercy. So the text is not saying, “Right and wrong do not exist,” or “It makes no difference whether an action is good or bad.”

Rather, Jesus is warning about the way we exercise moral judgment.

## 1. The verse is not moral indifference

To say “moral judgments never matter” would be to deny that there is any meaningful difference between kindness and cruelty, truth and lying, justice and injustice, love and selfishness. But Luke 6:37 assumes the opposite.

Jesus says:

> “Judge not, and ye shall not be judged: condemn not, and ye shall not be condemned: forgive, and ye shall be forgiven.”

This only makes sense if judgment, condemnation, and forgiveness are morally significant. The verse is not saying that moral perception is useless. It is saying that moral judgment must be exercised with mercy, humility, and self-awareness.

The context of Luke 6 is very clear. Jesus has just been teaching love for enemies, doing good to those who hate you, giving without expecting return, and being merciful as your heavenly Father is merciful. So the warning is not, “Stop seeing moral reality.” It is more like this:

> “Do not use moral judgment as a way to make yourself feel superior, to harshly sentence others, or to withhold the mercy you yourself stand under need.”

The verse is a regulation of judgment, not the abolition of moral discernment.

## 2. “Judge” can mean discernment, but it can also mean harsh verdict

The word “judge” can cover a wide range of things.

At one level, to judge means to perceive moral reality:

- “That was kind.”
- “That was cruel.”
- “That was true.”
- “That was dishonest.”
- “That practice is unjust.”
- “That action harmed someone.”

That kind of judgment is necessary. Without it, we cannot love well, live morally, repent, forgive, or pursue justice.

But “judge” can also mean something stronger: to stand over someone as a final authority, to pronounce a guilty verdict, to impose punishment, to exclude, to declare someone unworthy. That second kind of judging is what Jesus warns against here.

So the verse is not saying:

> “Never notice moral evil.”

It is closer to:

> “Do not make yourself the final, merciless judge of another person.”

There is a difference between saying, “That action was wrong,” and saying, “You are a bad person and you deserve to be treated harshly.”

## 3. “Condemn” is stronger than “discern”

The text says both “judge not” and “condemn not.” The word “condemn” carries a legal and final quality. To condemn someone is to pronounce them guilty, often with a sense of finality. In religious language, condemnation also carries the weight of being under judgment or wrath.

Discernment can name sin without making the sinner wholly disposable.

For example:

- Discernment: “That lie hurt trust.”
- Condemnation: “You are a liar, and people like you can’t be trusted.”

- Discernment: “That action was selfish.”
- Condemnation: “You are selfish, and you always do this.”

- Discernment: “That policy is unjust.”
- Condemnation: “Those who defend it are morally dead.”

Discernment can hold someone responsible while still leaving room for repentance, growth, mercy, and restoration. The appetite to condemn often closes those doors.

## 4. Moral judgments matter, but they can become idols

This is an important point: moral judgment can become an idol.

When moral language is used mainly to rank people, punish them, protect our own purity, or make us feel righteous, it may have started as moral discernment but hardened into something else. It becomes less about truth and more about power.

The appetite to condemn often serves the self:

- “I am better than that person.”
- “I am not like them.”
- “If I can show them to be guilty, I can feel safe.”
- “If I condemn them, I do not have to examine myself.”
- “If I make them the problem, I can avoid my own complicity.”
- “If I am the one who sees clearly, I hold moral authority.”

That is not the same thing as moral discernment.

Discernment is not merely about finding fault. It is about seeking the truth in such a way that it leads to healing, justice, repentance, and mercy.

## 5. Discernment and the appetite to condemn are different

We can distinguish them like this:

| Discernment | Appetite to condemn |
|---|---|
| Asks, “What is true here?” | Asks, “How can I prove this person wrong?” |
| Seeks clarity | Seeks a guilty party |
| Is humble | Is often self-assured |
| Is merciful | Is often harsh |
| Is willing to be corrected | Wants finality |
| Names the action without exhausting the person | Labels the whole person |
| Seeks restoration | Seeks punishment or exclusion |
| Examines the self as well as the other | Protects the self from self-examination |
| Can say, “This is wrong, and I still love you” | Often says, “You are wrong, and therefore you are lesser” |

Discernment does not avoid moral seriousness. It takes moral seriousness responsibly.

The appetite to condemn may feel serious, but it is often less concerned with truth than with triumph.

## 6. Forgiveness proves that moral judgment matters

One of the most telling words in the verse is “forgive.”

Forgiveness only makes sense if something morally went wrong. If nothing morally matters, there is no debt to forgive. If an action was not really an offense, there is no need for pardon. If there is no guilt, no harm, no brokenness, no need for mercy.

So when Jesus says, “Forgive, and you will be forgiven,” he is assuming that moral injury is real. The point is not that offenses do not count. The point is that when offenses do occur, they should not automatically become occasions for harsh judgment or unforgiving retaliation.

Forgiveness is one of the most serious moral acts a person can perform. It requires moral perception. It requires recognizing that something was wrong, that someone was hurt, that a debt or breach existed — and then responding with mercy rather than retribution.

That is very different from moral indifference.

## 7. The danger is not moral judgment, but merciless judgment

Jesus is not saying that moral judgment is always wrong. He is warning against a particular posture: the posture of the person who stands over another as though they are beyond the reach of God’s mercy.

This is especially important because all human beings are under judgment. We are not perfect moral authorities. We do not see all motives, histories, consequences, or hidden wounds. We have our own sins, blind spots, and failures.

So when we judge others harshly, we often forget our own condition.

That is why the following warning makes sense:

> “With the judgment you judge you will be judged.”

This is not merely a magical rule. It is a spiritual and moral principle. If we develop a habit of being harsh, unforgiving, and final with others, that habit will shape us. It will make us hard to forgive, impatient with mercy, and resistant to grace.

In other words, judgment is not neutral. It forms the judge.

## 8. The difference between condemning a sin and condemning a person

A major part of the distinction is this: we can discern that a sin, action, or pattern is wrong without condemning the whole person as irredeemable.

We can say:

> “That was wrong,”

without having to say:

> “Therefore you are wrong in every way, and you deserve to be treated as though you are finished.”

We can hold someone accountable without making them into a category of guilty person.

For example:

- “That decision was unfair.”
- “That habit is harmful.”
- “That comment was unkind.”
- “That choice brought suffering.”

These are moral judgments. They matter.

But they are different from:

- “You are unfair.”
- “You are harmful.”
- “You are unkind.”
- “You are the problem.”

The first set of statements can open the door to repentance. The second set often closes it.

Discernment can be true and merciful at the same time. The appetite to condemn often sacrifices mercy for the sake of certainty, and truth for the sake of dominance.

## 9. Jesus does not forbid moral perception

If “judge not” meant “never make moral distinctions,” it would create many problems.

Would we be unable to say that murder is worse than kindness? That lying is not the same as truthfulness? That exploitation is not the same as generosity? That a friend’s broken promise matters? That injustice needs correction?

Obviously not.

Jesus himself makes moral distinctions. He praises and condemns. He calls some actions righteous and others wicked. He distinguishes between mercy and harshness, purity and hypocrisy, faith and unbelief. He does not live as though moral reality does not exist.

But he also warns against hypocritical, self-righteous judgment. The issue is not that we see moral truth. The issue is whether we see it in a way that leads to love, humility, and mercy.

## 10. The practical difference

A discerning person might say:

> “I think what happened was wrong. I was hurt. I may not understand the whole situation. But I do not want to make you into a guilty object. I want to speak the truth in a way that leaves room for repentance, repair, and mercy.”

A person driven by the appetite to condemn might say:

> “That proves what kind of person you are. You always do this. People like you are not trustworthy. I will remember this and measure you by it from now on.”

Both may be reacting to the same moral event. But the first response is trying to judge well. The second is using judgment as a weapon.

## 11. A good test

A useful question is:

> “Is this moral judgment increasing truth and mercy, or is it mainly increasing my sense of superiority?”

Or:

> “Am I trying to see what is right, or am I trying to prove someone wrong?”

Or:

> “Am I willing to be corrected by this judgment, or am I using it to protect myself?”

Discernment can change us. The appetite to condemn often only changes the other person’s status in our eyes.

## 12. The deeper point

Luke 6:37 is not saying that moral judgments never matter. It is saying that moral judgments matter so much that they must be handled carefully.

If moral judgments did not matter, there would be no danger in judging badly. But because moral judgment has power — it can heal, clarify, convict, restore, punish, exclude, and forgive — it must be governed by mercy.

The verse is not against moral seriousness. It is against the spirit of merciless finality.

Discernment is the moral perception of a heart that knows both truth and mercy. It can say, “This is wrong,” and still say, “This person is not beyond grace.”

The appetite to condemn is the moral sense of a heart that wants an enemy, a guilty party, a verdict, and a reason to feel superior. It may use true words, but it often uses them for the wrong ends.

So the difference is this:

> Moral discernment says, “What is the truth, and what is the mercy?”  
> The appetite to condemn says, “Who can I show to be wrong, and how can I make that work in my favor?”

Jesus is not calling us to stop seeing moral reality. He is calling us to judge with the same mercy we hope to receive.
