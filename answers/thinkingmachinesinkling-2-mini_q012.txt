It is better read as **realistic about self-deception**—though that realism is darker than a simple “self-knowledge is limited.” Jeremiah is not saying only that people are sometimes mistaken; he is saying the inner person is fundamentally unreliable as a self-witness. “Deceitful above all things” and “who can know it?” make human access to motive insecure at its root. So there is a strong note of pessimism about autonomous self-knowledge—but not a flat claim that every human act is therefore meaningless or that all inward motives are equal.

The key move in v. 10 is that the criterion is not confident introspection but divine search: God “searches the heart,” “tries” the inner self, and gives each man “according to his ways, and according to the fruit of his doings.” Motives matter, but they are not provable from inside the heart alone. The visible pattern of what a person does—and what it produces—becomes a more trustworthy evidence base, while the inward self remains partly unknowable to itself.

So the passage rules out two opposite ways of talking:

- **Self-flattering confidence**: “I know my own heart,” “my real motive is pure,” “I don’t need to judge by results.”
- **Cynical diagnosis**: “people are basically deceitful,” “everyone is lying inwardly,” “motives are unknowable, so moral talk is worthless.”

A healthier way to speak is:

1. **Confess uncertainty about motive.** Say, “I do not fully know my own motives,” rather than pronounce an absolute inward verdict on self or others.
2. **Test by fruit, not by feeling.** Motives become partly visible through durable patterns of speech, choice, and consequence. This does not make outcomes a perfect test of inward purity, but it makes them necessary evidence.
3. **Distinguish motive, action, fruit.** A questionable motive can coexist with a good act; an apparently noble motive can still be self-deceived. The passage condemns the heart’s inward deceit, not merely its bad judgments.
4. **Ask for discernment and correction.** Since the heart is unreliable, honest self-examination needs anchors: Scripture, conscience without arrogance, honest accountability, community, and prayer.
5. **Avoid both excuse and condemnation.** “The heart cannot be known” is not a license for resignation or for attacking others’ motives with certainty. It is an invitation to intellectual and moral humility.

In short: Jeremiah gives a pessimistic appraisal of human self-knowledge, but a sober and useful one. It does not license despair; it disciplines judgment. A person should talk about motives as real but hidden, seldom legible inwardly, and therefore referred—with humility—to conduct, fruit, and God’s sovereign knowledge.
