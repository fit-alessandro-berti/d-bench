## The pairing is deliberate: a repentance of the whole person

Isaiah's parallelism is doing careful theological work. "The wicked" balances "the unrighteous man," and "his way" (*derek*) balances "his thoughts" (*machashavot*). This is a merism — two poles that together name the totality of a human being. The *way* is the visible, public, directional thing: conduct, habits, the road one is walking. The *thoughts* are the invisible engine room: plans, motives, imaginations, the inner narrative that generates the way.

In Hebrew anthropology these are not separable compartments. "As he thinketh in his heart, so is he" (Prov. 23:7). Ways are the fruit; thoughts are the root. Genesis 6:5 locates the corruption of the whole earth in "every imagination of the thoughts of his heart." So when Isaiah demands that both be forsaken, he is simply saying: repentance must reach the whole person, because sin inhabits the whole person.

## Why half-repentance fails in both directions

Notice the verb: *forsake* — active renunciation, not mere regret. And notice what must be forsaken:

- **Conduct without thoughts** produces moral reformation with an unchanged heart — the Pharisaic pattern Jesus condemns, whited sepulchres. Behavior can be cleaned up while the imagination still rehearses, covets, resents.
- **Thoughts without conduct** produce inward piety that never changes a step — the "faith without works" James dismantles. Feeling sorry in the heart while continuing down the same road is not repentance; it is sentiment.

The text refuses both shortcuts. And the word "return" (*shuv*, the root of *teshuvah*) shows repentance is not only negative renunciation but positive turning — toward a Person, not merely away from a habit.

## The literary key: verses 8–9 immediately follow

This is the most striking feature of the passage. The very next verses say:

> "For my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways, saith the Lord... as the heavens are higher than the earth, so are my ways higher than your ways, and my thoughts than your thoughts."

Isaiah has engineered the connection. The repentance of verse 7 — forsaking *your* way and *your* thoughts — is precisely what makes room for *God's* way and *God's* thoughts. Repentance is not a behavior-adjustment program; it is an exchange of entire thought-worlds. You surrender your categories, your self-justifications, your scale of values, and receive His. Verse 11 confirms it: "my word... shall not return unto me void." The forsaken thoughts are replaced by His word.

## What this implies about outward conduct

Outward conduct matters — but it is never sufficient, and never the *starting point* God treats as final. Conduct is the required evidence that the inner turn is real; a "repentance" that leaves the feet walking the same road has not happened. But equally, God does not accept reformed behavior as a substitute for a returned heart. The demand is total because God does not deal with a partitioned self.

## What this implies about inward imagination

The text quietly demolishes the modern assumption that the thought life is private and morally neutral. Isaiah names the imagination as territory God claims and judges — and therefore as territory where repentance must operate. Hatred, lust, pride, unbelief all begin there (as Jesus insists in the Sermon on the Mount). If mercy is to be real, it must reach the place where sin is *authored*, not merely performed. Nothing in the inner workshop is exempt from the call to forsake.

## What this implies about the possibility of mercy

Here is the astonishing balance. The demand is total — way *and* thoughts, the whole self. But the promise is more than total: "he will *abundantly* pardon" — in Hebrew, literally, *he will multiply to pardon* (*yirbeh lisloach*). The rigor of the condition is matched by the superabundance of the offer. And note what is *not* demanded: no payment, no price, no penance. The chapter opens, "buy... without money" (55:1). What God asks for is not compensation but surrender — renunciation and return.

Two further implications:

1. **The urgency** — "while he may be found" — frames mercy as a present window, not a permanent standing offer. Grace is real, but it has a *now* to it; postponement is the one thing the text treats as dangerous.
2. **The abundance is explained by the context**: precisely because God's thoughts and ways are "higher than the heavens," His forgiveness operates on a scale our sin cannot exhaust. The higher thoughts that demand our surrender are the same higher thoughts that promise a pardon beyond proportion.

The Prodigal Son is this verse narrated: he "came to himself" (thoughts changed), "arose and came to his father" (way changed) — and the father ran to meet him, long before the apology was finished. The whole person turns; the whole pardon answers.
