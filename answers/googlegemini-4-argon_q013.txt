In Amos 5:21–24, the rejection of worship is absolute because the prophet is not diagnosing a mere "imbalance" in the Israelites' religious life; he is diagnosing a fundamental corruption of what worship actually is. 

To ask for "better balance"—such as suggesting they do a little less sacrificing and a little more charity—would imply that their worship was valid on its own terms, just incomplete. Instead, God rejects the worship entirely because, in the absence of justice, **the worship itself has become an instrument of evil.**

Here is why the rejection is so sharp, and the specific nature of the fraud being exposed.

### 1. Why a "Balance" is Impossible
In the theology of the Hebrew prophets, ritual worship and social justice are not two separate departments of life that need to be balanced. Rather, social justice (how one treats the vulnerable) is the **fruit** and the **proof** of a right relationship with God, while liturgy (sacrifices, songs, festivals) is the **celebration** of that relationship. 

If a society is actively oppressing the poor (as detailed earlier in Amos 5:11–12), their relationship with God is already severed. Therefore, showing up at the sanctuary to celebrate that relationship is a lie. God does not want them to "balance" their oppression of the poor with nice music; He wants the oppression to stop. Unaccompanied by righteousness, the worship is not merely insufficient—it is offensive.

### 2. The Moral and Spiritual Frauds Being Exposed
Amos is exposing a sophisticated system of religious hypocrisy that was rampant in the affluent Northern Kingdom of Israel during the 8th century BCE. Several specific types of fraud are on display:

**A. The "Transactional" Fraud (Treating God as Bribable)**
The Israelites were treating the Covenant like a pagan protection racket. They believed that as long as they paid their religious taxes—providing the most expensive "fat beasts" and throwing lavish festivals—God was contractually obligated to bless and protect them, regardless of how they treated one another. By rejecting the offerings, God exposes the fraud that He can be bought off. To offer a sacrifice paid for with money extorted from the poor is to try to make God an accomplice to theft.

**B. The "Anesthetic" Fraud (Using Religion to Soothe the Conscience)**
The people were using elaborate, noisy, sensory-rich worship ("smell of assemblies," "noise of songs," "melody of viols") as a spiritual anesthetic. By immersing themselves in intense religious piety on the weekends, the wealthy elites could convince themselves that they were "good, godly people," effectively blinding themselves to the cruelty they practiced during the week. God strips away this anesthetic so they are forced to look at their actual moral state.

**C. The Fraud of Stolen Goods**
Earlier in the book, Amos notes that the wealthy are drinking wine bought with fines unjustly levied against the poor, and sleeping on garments taken in pledge from the needy *right beside the altars* (Amos 2:8). Therefore, the very animals they are sacrificing and the harps they are playing were funded by exploitation. The worship is rejected because it is being performed with stolen goods.

**D. The Fraud of Seasonal vs. Perpetual Morality**
Notice the contrast in the imagery at the end of the passage: *"Let judgment [justice] run down as waters, and righteousness as a mighty [ever-flowing] stream."* 
In the ancient Near East, most streams were *wadis*—dry gullies that only filled with water during flash floods in the rainy season, then quickly dried up. The Israelites' religion was like a wadi: it surged up seasonally during "feast days" and "solemn assemblies," and then vanished. God demands a righteousness that is like a perennial, unstoppable river—a way of life that flows constantly, not a religious performance that happens on a schedule.

### Summary
Amos 5:21–24 rejects worship so sharply because **performative piety is the ultimate enemy of genuine morality.** It allows people to feel righteous without *being* righteous. God rejects their songs and sacrifices not because He hates music or liturgy, but because He refuses to participate in a charade that uses the beauty of religion to mask the ugliness of human cruelty.
