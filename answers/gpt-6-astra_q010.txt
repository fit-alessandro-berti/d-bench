The power praised here is **greatness that does not need to secure itself by diminishing others**.

The movement of the passage is downward: from “the form of God” to “the form of a servant,” from divine equality to human vulnerability, from vulnerability to an obedience that accepts even a shameful death. Yet Paul presents this descent not as a failure of greatness, but as its revelation. Christ’s status becomes the ground for self-giving rather than a claim to exemption from suffering.

The older wording “thought it not robbery to be equal with God” can obscure this point. Many modern translations render the phrase as not treating equality with God as something to exploit for his own advantage. On that reading, the question is not simply whether Christ possesses power, but **what he does with it**.

Humility joined to greatness can feel more unsettling than open domination for several reasons:

- **Domination is familiar.** It tells us where we stand: someone commands, someone submits. We can fear it, bargain with it, or resist it. Greatness that serves refuses that predictable arrangement.
- **It exposes our defenses.** We often justify ambition, pride, and control as necessities: *Anyone with real power would protect their position.* Here is power that does otherwise. Its existence challenges the excuse.
- **It asks for more than compliance.** A ruler can compel outward obedience while leaving our desires untouched. Paul’s opening—“Let this mind be in you”—asks for a transformation of what we admire and how we treat others.
- **It makes us recipients.** Being served by someone we recognize as great can unsettle our self-sufficiency. We cannot easily dismiss the gift as weakness, or comfortably assume we have earned it.

There is an important distinction, though: this is not praise for humiliation imposed on the powerless. The passage portrays Christ’s self-giving, not a superior’s entitlement to demand someone else’s abasement. Using it to sanctify domination would reverse its movement.

The unsettling claim is that **the highest power may be most fully itself when it does not insist on its privileges**. Open domination threatens our freedom. This kind of greatness challenges our very picture of what freedom and greatness are for.
