The controlling sentence is the introduction:

> “Certain which **trusted in themselves that they were righteous**, and **despised others**” (Luke 18:9).

So the root condemnation is **self-righteousness**: treating one’s own moral and religious record as the basis of one’s standing before God, and then looking down on others from that imagined height.

The other items are symptoms of that root.

### The Pharisee is not condemned for fasting or tithing

Jesus does not say the Pharisee’s practices are false or evil. Fasting and tithing were legitimate religious disciplines. The problem is what he does with them.

His prayer is structured around “I”:

- “I thank thee”
- “I am not as other men”
- “I fast twice in the week”
- “I give tithes of all that I possess”

He begins, “God, I thank thee,” but the prayer is really a self-presentation. Luke says he prayed “with himself” or “to himself” — the prayer has become a monologue. God is almost only the witness before whom he displays his own goodness.

### What exactly condemns him?

Not one item only, but the whole complex:

1. **Pride** — he exalts himself as righteous.
2. **Comparison** — “I am not as other men… or even as this publican.”
3. **Contempt** — the introduction explicitly says he “despised others”; the publican is his human foil.
4. **Virtue as self-display** — his fasting and tithing become credentials, not acts of devotion.
5. **Practical false doctrine** — not false doctrine in the sense that fasting and tithing are wrong, but a false view of justification: “I am righteous because of my religious performance.”

So the answer is: **his self-trusting, contemptuous self-righteousness**. His virtues are not condemned in themselves; they are condemned because he uses them to justify himself and to condemn another.

### Why the tax collector is spiritually truer

The publican’s posture is true because it corresponds to the truth of his condition before God.

- He stands “afar off”: he makes no claim to approach God on the basis of merit.
- He will not lift up his eyes: he is ashamed and contrite.
- He smites his breast: he acknowledges his own guilt.
- He says, “God be merciful to me a sinner”: he asks for mercy rather than presenting a résumé.

He does not compare himself with the Pharisee. He does not say, “At least I am honest.” He simply tells the truth: **“I am a sinner; God, have mercy.”**

That is why Jesus says,

> “This man went down to his house justified rather than the other.”

The tax collector is not justified because tax collectors are morally better, nor because humility is a clever technique for earning favor. He is justified because he receives God’s mercy in repentance. The Pharisee, meanwhile, already treats himself as justified and therefore has no need of mercy.

The parable’s summary is the key:

> “Everyone that exalteth himself shall be abased; and he that humbleth himself shall be exalted.”

In short: the Pharisee’s sin is not that he has virtues, but that his virtues have become his identity, his claim on God, and his weapon against others. The publican is truer because he comes to God not with a record, but with need.
