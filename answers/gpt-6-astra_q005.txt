Enemy-love is difficult to take seriously because it challenges something we often regard as moral common sense: **good should be answered with good, and harm with harm.** To love an enemy can feel not merely impractical but unjust—as though we were betraying the victim, excusing wrongdoing, or surrendering the self-respect needed to resist it.

Jesus does not resolve that difficulty by pretending enemies are harmless. The passage names hatred, abuse, and persecution. The command concerns people who actually do wrong, not merely people we find irritating.

### Not primarily sentimental

“Love” here cannot depend on affection. Jesus gives it concrete forms: blessing rather than cursing, doing good rather than retaliating, praying for those who persecute you. These are practices one might undertake while still feeling anger, grief, or fear.

Enemy-love therefore does **not** require declaring evil good, restoring trust without evidence, or remaining available for abuse. You can protect yourself, defend others, and seek accountability while refusing to make another person’s suffering your ultimate goal.

The distinction is between **opposing someone’s wrongdoing and willing their destruction**.

### More than strategic

Loving an enemy can sometimes interrupt hostility. Refusing retaliation may expose injustice or make reconciliation possible. But if enemy-love is only a strategy, it becomes dispensable whenever it fails to produce cooperation.

Jesus grounds it elsewhere: God gives sun and rain to both the just and the unjust. These gifts do not declare everyone equally righteous; they show that God’s generosity is not confined to those who deserve it.

The point of “that ye may be the children of your Father” is family resemblance: acting in a way that reflects the Father’s character.

### A deeper moral strength

Enemy-love requires the strength to resist two things at once: **the enemy’s injustice and the temptation to become governed by it.** The enemy does not get to determine the limits of your humanity.

That is harder than either passive submission or uncomplicated revenge. It asks us to pursue justice without needing hatred to sustain us, and to leave open the possibility that a wrongdoer might repent rather than merely suffer.

None of this makes the command easy, especially for someone deeply harmed. It should never become a weapon for silencing victims or shielding perpetrators.

Its radical claim is that goodness need not be a transaction. We need not wait for another person to become worthy before refusing to dehumanize them. Enemy-love is not moral softness; it is the difficult freedom to oppose evil without allowing evil to dictate how we love.
