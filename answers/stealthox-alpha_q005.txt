Enemy-love is difficult to take seriously for a reason worth noticing up front: **we suspect that if we took it seriously, it would cost us something we're not ready to pay.** Cheap commands are easy to honor. This one, taken at face value, dismantles things we quietly depend on — our grievances, our sense of righteous injury, the way our enemies give shape and meaning to our moral world. Nietzsche understood this better than many Christians have; he attacked the command ferociously precisely because he recognized it was serious. Dismissal and outrage are both ways of not obeying.

But let's test your three hypotheses directly.

**Sentimental? No — and the verse itself refutes this reading.** Notice that Jesus defines love entirely in verbs: *bless, do good, pray.* Nothing in the command asks you to feel warmth toward someone who has harmed you. That would be dishonest and impossible — emotions don't obey orders. What is being commanded is a practice: willed goodwill enacted in speech, action, and intercession. C.S. Lewis made the point sharply: love, in this sense, "is not a feeling but a state of the will." The sentimental reading is largely a modern distortion — we psychologized love into emotion, then declared the command absurd because we can't manufacture feelings on demand. Jesus never asked anyone to feel tenderly toward a persecutor. He asked them to act toward the persecutor's good.

**Strategic? Partially — and it deserves partial credit.** Unilateral goodwill genuinely does de-escalate; Martin Luther King Jr. built a movement on the observation that "hate multiplies hate," and even game theory confirms that generous, non-retaliatory strategies often outperform tit-for-tat over time. Proverbs' image of heaping burning coals on an enemy's head is best read not as covert revenge but as the awakening of shame that leads to repentance. But strategy alone corrupts the command from within. If I love my enemy *because it works*, I am still using him as a means to my ends — the self remains at the center. And strategy is conditional: when the enemy escalates anyway, strategic love collapses into bitterness. Conditionality is exactly what this command suspends.

**Moral strength? Yes — and the text's own logic explains why.** Two features of the passage matter:

First, the grounding clause: *"that ye may be the children of your Father... for he maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good."* Enemy-love is not arbitrary asceticism; it is imitation of a specific divine pattern. God's goodness is non-reciprocal and indiscriminate — rain falls on the unjust, and this is presented not as weakness but as the most generative force in creation. Verse 47 drives the point home: loving those who love you is nothing; tax collectors manage that. Enemy-love is the distinguishing mark of sonship precisely because it exits the economy of exchange. It is harder than retaliation, not easier — retaliation is reflexive, while this must be chosen against every instinct.

Second, notice what the command does with justice: it doesn't abolish judgment, it *relocates* it. Praying for persecutors hands the case to the only Judge qualified to hold it (Paul draws the inference explicitly in Romans 12:19). Retaliation pretends I can administer perfect justice; enemy-love admits I can't, and frees me to will the enemy's good — including his repentance. Crucially, love is not approval. You can oppose someone's evil *because* you love him; hatred, by contrast, opposes evil while secretly savoring it. Hatred corrodes the hater first.

It's also worth remembering the immediate context. "An eye for an eye" was already a *limit* on vengeance — a proportionality constraint. Jesus doesn't relax the law; he intensifies it, moving from restraining retaliation to active goodwill. And the "hate your enemy" half of verse 43 isn't in Torah at all — it's a human inference, a narrowing of "neighbor." Jesus simply refuses the narrowing.

**So why does it remain so hard?** Because it asks for a kind of strength we rarely train: the strength to surrender the role of victim and avenger simultaneously. History's witnesses suggest how costly it is — Stephen praying for his killers, early Christians nursing the plague-stricken whom everyone else had abandoned (the pagan emperor Julian complained bitterly that "the Galileans" fed even their enemies' poor), Bonhoeffer wrestling with the command until it killed him. These were not sentimentalists. Niebuhr called the Sermon's ethic an "impossible possibility" — impossible as a social program, possible only as a grace-sustained discipline. Which suggests the practical form it takes: not a heroic achievement but a direction walked in small refusals of contempt, in prayers that begin honestly with "I don't want to love them — help my unwillingness," in one concrete good done where none was owed.

Perhaps the final word is this: the very fact that we *can't* take it lightly — that it provokes either glib domestication or fierce rejection — is evidence of what it is. Sentimental things are easy to take seriously. This one costs everything, which is usually the signature of the real thing.
