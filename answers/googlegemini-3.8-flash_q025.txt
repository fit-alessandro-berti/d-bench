Based on the text of Matthew 6:1 and its broader context within the Sermon on the Mount, the moral danger lies **not in visibility itself, but entirely in the craving to be seen**—the internal motive and intended audience of the act.

Here is a breakdown of why this distinction matters and what makes public goodness so morally perilous:

---

### 1. The Crucial Distinction: Purpose vs. Mere Exposure
The Greek phrasing translated as *“to be seen of them”* (*pros to theathenai autois*) denotes **deliberate intent and purpose**. It shares its root with the English word *theater*. 

Jesus is not condemning goodness that happens to be witnessed; He is condemning **righteousness performed as a stage-play**, where the community serves as the audience and human applause is the objective.

This is proven by looking just one chapter earlier, where Jesus commands visibility:
> *“Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father which is in heaven.”* (Matthew 5:16)

* **Matthew 5:16 (Healthy Visibility):** Done before men $\rightarrow$ so that *God* is magnified.
* **Matthew 6:1 (Perilous Visibility):** Done before men $\rightarrow$ so that *the self* is magnified.

The physical act in both cases is visible, but the **moral orientation** is fundamentally reversed.

---

### 2. What Makes Public Goodness Morally Dangerous?

The verse warns that public virtue carries distinct moral hazards:

#### A. It Camouflages Pride as Virtue
Righteousness (such as giving alms) is supposed to be an act of mercy toward a neighbor and devotion toward God. When poisoned by the desire for admiration, it becomes a tool of **spiritual narcissism**. It allows pride—the very thing goodness is meant to counteract—to disguise itself as holiness. The giver uses the poor person's need as a prop for their own reputation.

#### B. It Exhausts the Moral Act in a Shallow Transaction
Jesus warns: *“otherwise ye have no reward of your Father which is in heaven.”* 
In the verses that follow (e.g., 6:2), Jesus notes that those who seek public acclaim *“have their reward.”* 
* The transaction is completed on earth: you gave money, and you bought social status. 
* Because human validation was the target, the moral and eternal value of the act is completely depleted. You cannot demand a reward from God for a performance delivered strictly for human onlookers.

#### C. It Replaces Reality with Image
Seeking to be seen naturally tempts the moral agent to prioritize what is *observable* over what is *true*. One begins to care more about maintaining the reputation of being generous, just, or pious than actually being transformed within. It divorces outward conduct from inner integrity.

---

### Summary
The moral danger in Matthew 6:1 is not the **eye of the witness**, but the **appetite of the performer**. Goodness can be public without being theatrical; but the moment the craving for human approval enters the heart, virtue ceases to be an act of love and becomes an act of self-promotion.
