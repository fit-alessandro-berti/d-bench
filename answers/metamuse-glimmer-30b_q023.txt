Isaiah 55 is the invitation of the Servant to the exiles: the covenant God who has been near in judgment is now near in mercy. The turn of v.7 is therefore the condition of the mercy that is promised in v.7b, not its purchase.

*Way* and *thoughts* are a Hebrew merism for the whole person.

*derekh*, “way”, is the path you walk, the habitual life-style, the deeds that are visible and repeatable. To forsake it is to abandon the practiced course of the wicked, the socially and religiously sanctioned patterns of idolatry, exploitation and self-reliance that Israel has made its home.

*machsheboth*, “thoughts”, is not a private daydream but the plans, counsels and imaginations by which a man justifies that way. It is the inner logic, the world-view, the reasons you tell yourself for what you do. In Hebrew anthropology the heart is the seat of thinking, willing and imagining. The unrighteous man can be unrighteous even when his *way* looks respectable, because the inner scheme that governs him is crooked.

That is why the verse pairs two figures: the *wicked* whose sin is in action, and the *unrighteous man* whose sin is in intention. Repentance in Isaiah is not a change of behavior alone and not a change of mind alone. The inner imagination produces the outward way, and the outward way confirms the imagination. To return to YHWH is therefore a forsaking *both* the track and the map.

The implication is radical holism.

Outward conduct matters because God sees what is done. A forsaken way means real renunciation, not rebranding. The prophet elsewhere contrasts God’s ways with man’s ways, Is 55:8-9. Mercy is not given to a man who keeps his idolatrous practices while praying with new words.

Inward imagination matters because God judges the heart. The unrighteous man must give up his *thoughts* - his self-justifying schemes, his calculations that God can be used, his assumption that his own devices are sound. Without that surrender the changed conduct will collapse again into the old way, because the mind that formed the way remains.

That is exactly what makes pardon possible. Abundant pardon, *yarbeh lisloach*, is God’s character, not a reward for partial reform. It is *abundant* because the sin is both external and internal, and God’s mercy is wide enough to cover both. He will have mercy *upon him* who returns, and to our God for he will abundantly pardon. The initiative is God’s nearness, “seek ye the Lord while he may be found, call ye upon him while he is near,” but the meeting point is a whole-person turning: abandon the road you walk and the counsel you live by.

In short, Isaiah refuses a repentance that is only moralism and refuses one that is only introspection. True shuv, return, is the outward forsaking of a wicked way produced by the inward forsaking of unrighteous thoughts, and it is the only posture in which God’s abundant, not grudging, mercy can be received.
