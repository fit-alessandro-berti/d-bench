

The image is sharp because it turns a moral failing into a grotesque visual scene.

A person notices a tiny speck—“the mote”—in someone else’s eye, while ignoring the massive wooden beam in his own. The humor is severe: one man offers to perform delicate eye surgery on another while his own eye is blocked by something absurdly large. The metaphor exposes not merely fault-finding but distorted perception. The hypocrite cannot see rightly because his own condition prevents it.

Several things make it cutting:

- **Disproportion:** the other person’s fault is tiny; his own is enormous.
- **Blindness disguised as insight:** he claims to see the speck clearly, yet cannot see himself.
- **Intrusiveness:** he wants to remove the speck from another’s eye without first dealing with his own.
- **Hypocrisy:** the very act of offering correction reveals a failure of self-examination.

Yet the passage does not forbid moral correction. It says: *“first cast out the beam out of thine own eye; and then shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote out of thine brother’s eye.”* The command is about order and humility, not silence.

Jesus condemns self-righteous judgment, not all discernment. The problem is not helping a brother with a speck; the problem is doing so while ignoring one’s own greater fault. Once the “beam” is removed, the person can “see clearly”—that is, judge rightly, gently, and credibly.

So the passage attacks hypocrisy by insisting that correction must begin with self-correction. It does not abolish moral responsibility; it purifies it.
