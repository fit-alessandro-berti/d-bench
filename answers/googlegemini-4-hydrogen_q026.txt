### 1. How the Passage Connects Speech to Hidden Moral Stockpiles

Matthew 12:34–35 links external speech to internal character using two complementary metaphors for the **heart** (which, in biblical usage, refers not just to emotion, but to the hidden core of a person’s mind, will, desires, and moral orientation):

* **The Overflowing Vessel (v. 34):** *"Out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaketh."*  
  The word translated *"abundance"* carries the sense of surplus or overflow. The image is of a container filled to capacity: whatever is inside inevitably spills over the rim. Speech is portrayed not as a detached surface performance, but as the spillover of whatever saturates a person's inner life.
* **The Hidden Storehouse (v. 35):** *"Out of the good treasure of the heart bringeth forth good things: and an evil man out of the evil treasure bringeth forth evil things."*  
  The word *"treasure"* refers to a treasury, vault, or storehouse where valuables or supplies are accumulated and kept out of sight. In this picture, the heart acts as an inner archive of values, thoughts, attitudes, and intentions. When a person speaks, they are reaching into that private stockpile and bringing its contents out into the public world.

Together, these metaphors assert a strict continuity between inner accumulation and outer expression: speech is the outward delivery system for what has been privately built up inside.

### 2. Are Words Symptoms, Revelations, Choices—or All Three?

The passage points most explicitly to words as **symptoms** and **revelations**, while also presupposing **choice** and moral agency—making **all three** true, though in distinct ways:

* **Words as Symptoms (Inevitable Byproducts):**  
  Jesus asks rhetorically, *"how can ye, being evil, speak good things?"* (v. 34). This frames speech as a symptom of an underlying moral condition—just as, in the immediately preceding verse (v. 33), bad fruit is the natural symptom of a bad tree. Because the mouth speaks from the *overflow* of the heart, sustained speech cannot consistently contradict a person's true nature. What comes out is driven by what is already there.
* **Words as Revelations (Diagnostic Evidence):**  
  Because the "treasure" of the heart is hidden from other people, speech functions as an unveiling. Bringing something *"forth"* from an unseen storehouse exposes what kind of stockpile a person has been keeping. In this sense, words act as reliable diagnostic evidence of character—an idea Jesus reinforces in the very next verses (vv. 36–37), where words serve as the basis of judgment.
* **Words as Choices (Moral Agency and Responsibility):**  
  The text qualifies the role of choice carefully. On one hand, v. 34 challenges the idea that an evil person can simply *choose* on a whim to produce genuinely good speech while remaining corrupt at the core (*"how can ye...?"*). On the other hand, v. 35 uses active language—a person *"bringeth forth"* from their treasure—and the very existence of a "treasure" implies something accumulated over time through habits, loyalties, and decisions. Moreover, the surrounding rebuke holds the speakers morally accountable for their words (including *"every idle word"* in v. 36), which only makes sense if speech involves human agency rather than purely mechanical determinism.

### Summary
In Matthew 12:34–35, words are **symptoms** of an inner moral state, **revelations** that expose a hidden inner stockpile, and **choices** in the sense that humans actively cultivate what they store in the heart and bear moral responsibility for what they bring forth from it.
