## What kind of power is being praised?

Not power as the ability to take, but power as the freedom to *give up*.

**1. Power that doesn't grasp.** "Thought it not robbery" translates the Greek *harpagmos*, most plausibly "something to be seized" or "something to exploit." The claim isn't that Christ lacked equality with God. It's that he didn't treat that status as a possession to clutch or leverage. That was precisely what the Roman world expected of the powerful: emperors claimed divine honors, and generals displayed their rank. The hymn presents someone with the highest status who refuses to use it for himself.

**2. Power shown by voluntary descent.** The verbs move steadily downward: *made himself of no reputation* (literally "emptied himself"), *took the form of a servant*, *was made in the likeness of men*, *humbled himself*, *became obedient unto death*, *even the death of the cross*. Scholars like Joseph Hellerman have noted that this reads like an inverted *cursus honorum*, the ladder of public offices ambitious Romans climbed. Here the ladder runs to the bottom, ending in crucifixion, the death reserved for slaves and rebels and designed to erase dignity.

**3. Power that is chosen, not forced.** Every verb is active. He *made himself*, *took*, *humbled himself*. Weakness is not the virtue here, since the weak have nothing to lay down. The point is that someone who could have done otherwise chose this. The humility is an exercise of freedom, not a symptom of its absence.

**4. Power that reveals rather than disguises.** A strong theological reading, argued by Michael Gorman and others, is that this isn't God temporarily hiding his real nature. It is God *showing* it. On this view self-giving is not the exception to divine power but its truest expression.

## Why can humble greatness be more unsettling than domination?

**Domination is legible.** A tyrant is easy to understand. You can fear him, resist him, flatter him, or rationalize your submission ("I had no choice"). Open force gives you a clear enemy and a clear excuse. Humble greatness gives you neither. You can't overthrow someone who isn't seizing anything, and you can't blame coercion for your response when none was applied.

**It removes our alibis.** We often tell ourselves that grasping is simply how the world works, that anyone with power would use it this way. A figure who has everything and refuses to exploit it breaks that story. If the highest being does not grasp, our own grasping can no longer pass as natural or necessary. It shows up as a choice.

**It judges without accusing.** A dominator condemns you, and you can resent the condemnation. Someone who kneels to serve you condemns nothing, yet your pride feels exposed anyway. Peter's protest in John 13, "Thou shalt never wash my feet," captures this. Being served by a superior can be harder to bear than being ordered around, because it overturns the hierarchy you had quietly accepted and asks something of you in return.

**It can't be bargained with.** Domination runs on exchange: obedience for safety, loyalty for reward. Self-emptying love operates outside that economy. It offers nothing to negotiate and wants nothing you can pay. That leaves us oddly powerless, not because we are overpowered but because our usual tools of leverage don't apply.

**It makes a demand.** The passage opens with "Let this mind be in you." It isn't only a portrait to admire but a pattern to imitate. Admiring a conqueror costs nothing. Being told to share the mind of one who descended to a cross costs everything, and it's disturbing to realize the ideal being held up is one you would naturally flee.

**It invites suspicion.** Humility joined to power also unsettles us because we know it can be faked. Manipulative humility, the powerful posing as servants in order to disarm, is real, and Nietzsche argued that Christian humility was itself a covert will to power. The hymn's continuation (verses 9–11, where God exalts Christ) could even be read as strategic self-lowering for later gain. The text resists that reading by making the descent genuine and total, all the way to death, with exaltation as God's response rather than Christ's calculation. Still, the tension explains part of the discomfort. We don't fully trust power that doesn't act like power, because we have rarely seen it done honestly.

## In short

The passage praises a power secure enough to let go, one that expresses itself by serving rather than seizing. It unsettles because it takes away our categories for power, our excuses for grasping, and our means of control. Domination we know how to handle. Greatness that kneels leaves us exposed, and asks us to become something we are not yet.
