

Luke 6:37 is not saying, “Moral judgments never matter.” It is saying, “Do not make condemnation your habit, your posture, or your final verdict.”

The difference is crucial.

## 1. The context is mercy, not moral indifference

Luke 6:37 comes immediately after Jesus says:

> “Be ye therefore merciful, as your Father also is merciful”  
> — Luke 6:36

So “judge not” and “condemn not” are commands shaped by mercy. Jesus is not abolishing moral discernment. He is forbidding a merciless, self-exalting readiness to condemn others.

In the same chapter, Jesus also speaks of:

- a blind man unable to lead another blind man,
- a person trying to remove a speck from another’s eye while ignoring the log in his own,
- trees being known by their fruit.

All of those images require discernment. They assume that some paths are blind, some faults are real, some fruit is bad. Jesus is not teaching that nothing may be evaluated. He is teaching how we evaluate.

## 2. “Judge not” does not mean “never distinguish right from wrong”

The word translated “judge” can mean several things: to decide, to evaluate, to accuse, to condemn, to pass final sentence. In Luke 6:37, paired with “condemn not,” it carries the sense of sitting in judgment over someone as though you have the ultimate authority to define them and dispose of them.

That is why the passage is not a charter for moral relativism. If moral judgments never mattered, then:

- forgiveness would be meaningless, because there would be no real wrong to forgive;
- repentance would be unnecessary;
- warnings against false teaching would be unloving;
- justice would be impossible;
- Jesus’ own calls to “bring forth fruits worthy of repentance” would collapse.

Forgive, and ye shall be forgiven” presupposes that real offenses exist. Forgiveness does not say, “What you did was not wrong.” It says, “What you did was wrong, but I will not keep holding it against you as a debt of personal vengeance.”

So the passage is not against moral judgment as such. It is against judgment that has become merciless, proud, and final.

## 3. Discernment sees clearly; condemnation wants to punish

Discernment and the appetite to condemn may look similar on the surface, but they come from very different hearts.

Discernment says:

- “This is wrong.”
- “This path is dangerous.”
- “This behavior is harming someone.”
- “I need to be careful, wise, and truthful.”
- “My own sin must be examined first.”
- “The goal is restoration, not destruction.”

The appetite to condemn says:

- “You are worthless.”
- “I am superior to you.”
- “I want you exposed and punished.”
- “I will not consider my own faults.”
- “Mercy is weakness.”
- “Your worst act defines your whole identity.”

Discernment is concerned with truth. Condemnation is often concerned with power, superiority, revenge, or self-justification.

A discerning person can say, “That is sinful,” without delighting in the sinner’s downfall. A condemning person turns moral judgment into a weapon and treats the other person as beyond mercy.

## 4. The log and the speck show that judgment must begin with humility

Jesus goes on to say:

> “Why beholdest thou the mote that is in thy brother’s eye, but perceivest not the beam that is in thine own eye?”  
> — Luke 6:41

This is not saying, “You have no right to notice the speck.” It is saying, “Do not pretend to be a pure judge while ignoring your own greater need for mercy.”

In fact, Jesus adds:

> “Thou hypocrite, cast out first the beam out of thine own eye; and then shalt thou see clearly to pull out the mote that is in thy brother’s eye.”  
> — Luke 6:42

Notice the goal: “then shalt thou see clearly.”

Clear sight is discernment. Jesus does not say, “After removing the log, never help your brother.” He says that once your own sin is dealt with, you may be able to help someone else rightly. The problem is not moral perception. The problem is hypocritical, self-righteous perception.

So Luke 6:37 does not forbid helping others remove sin from their lives. It forbids doing so with a condemning spirit while refusing to face our own need for grace.

## 5. Condemnation makes itself ultimate; discernment remains under God

One of the deepest differences is this:

- Discernment evaluates under God’s authority.
- Condemnation acts as though it owns the final verdict.

When we discern, we may say, “This is contrary to God’s law,” or “This person is doing harm,” or “This teaching is false.” But we still leave the final judgment of the soul to God. We do not pretend to know every circumstance, every wound, every degree of knowledge, every hidden motive, or the final state of a person before God.

The appetite to condemn, however, wants to close the case. It says, “I have seen enough. This person is finished. There is no hope. They deserve nothing but rejection.”

That is why Jesus links judgment with forgiveness. A person who refuses to forgive has usually moved from discernment into condemnation. Discernment may still recognize wrong; forgiveness refuses to let wrong have the last word.

## 6. Moral judgment matters when it serves truth, justice, and restoration

There are times when moral judgment is necessary:

- A church must discern whether teaching is faithful.
- Parents must discern whether behavior is harming a child.
- Courts must judge crimes.
- Victims must name abuse as wrong.
- Friends may need to confront dangerous sin.
- Society must distinguish justice from injustice.

None of this violates Luke 6:37, provided the judgment is not turned into a merciless appetite to destroy the person.

Even in Scripture, God commands:

> “Judge not according to the appearance, but judge righteous judgment.”  
> — John 7:24

That proves Jesus is not teaching that all judgment is wrong. He is teaching that unrighteous judgment is wrong. Righteous judgment is discerning, truthful, proportionate, humble, and aimed at good.

## 7. The appetite to condemn feeds on fault; discernment grieves over it

Another way to tell the difference is by the emotional spirit behind the judgment.

The appetite to condemn often feels eager. It enjoys finding fault. It looks for the worst interpretation. It is quick to label, mock, expose, or dismiss. It may even disguise cruelty as righteousness.

Discernment is often sorrowful. It does not delight in the failure of others. It is slow to accuse. It seeks facts. It considers its own weakness. It asks, “What is true? What is just? What is merciful? What will heal?”

A condemning spirit says, “Look how terrible you are.”

A discerning spirit says, “This is wrong, and it must be faced, but mercy is still possible.”

## 8. Forgiveness does not mean pretending evil is not evil

“Forgive, and ye shall be forgiven” does not mean:

- approving what is wrong,
- pretending harm did not occur,
- abandoning justice,
- trusting someone without wisdom,
- refusing to set boundaries,
- saying abuse is acceptable.

Forgiveness means releasing personal vengeance. It means refusing to keep the debt alive in one’s own heart as hatred. It means leaving final judgment to God. It means being willing, where possible and wise, to seek reconciliation.

This is why forgiveness actually requires moral judgment. You cannot forgive what you refuse to call wrong. True forgiveness looks the offense in the face and says, “This was real, this was sinful, this caused harm, but I will not be ruled by bitterness.”

## 9. A simple distinction

| Discernment | Appetite to condemn |
|---|---|
| Sees sin clearly | Feeds on sin eagerly |
| Begins with self-examination | Begins with self-righteousness |
| Hates the evil | Hates or despises the person |
| Aims at restoration | Aims at humiliation or punishment |
| Leaves final judgment to God | Acts as final judge |
| Is slow and careful | Is quick and harsh |
| Makes room for mercy | Refuses mercy |
| Can forgive | Keeps the offense alive |

## Conclusion

Luke 6:37 is not moral skepticism. It is moral wisdom under the rule of mercy.

Jesus is not saying, “Never call anything wrong.” He is saying, “Do not become a merciless judge. Do not delight in condemnation. Do not forget your own need for forgiveness. Do not treat others as if they are beyond mercy. Evaluate, discern, warn, and correct — but do so as one who has also received grace.”

The difference between discernment and the appetite to condemn is ultimately a difference of spirit:

- Discernment seeks truth and restoration.
- Condemnation seeks fault and finality.

So moral judgments do matter. But when we make them, we are called to remember the measure we use will be measured back to us, and mercy is the way of those who have themselves been forgiven.
