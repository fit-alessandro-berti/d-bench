The elder brother’s **deepest problem is discomfort with mercy itself**—and, at the root, a proud, self-righteous view of his relationship with the father.

His anger, envy, and complaint about fairness are real, but they are symptoms. His opening words reveal his heart: *“Lo, these many years do I serve thee.”* He thinks in terms of service, merit, and reward: *I obeyed; therefore I deserve.* The father’s grace to the younger brother threatens that whole way of thinking because it is unearned. If the father can rejoice over a rebellious sinner, then the elder brother’s obedience is not a currency for special treatment.

That is why the father answers both the practical complaint and the deeper issue: *“Son, thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine.”* He was not deprived materially; what he lacked was the father’s own heart—the ability to rejoice that *“this thy brother was dead, and is alive again.”*

So the best answer is: **his core problem is not merely bad behavior, but a merit-based pride that finds the free mercy of grace offensive.**
