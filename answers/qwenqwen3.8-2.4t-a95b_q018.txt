In the immediate context, Ephesians 4 is about “putting off” the old self and “putting on” the new self. The passage is not merely giving a list of bad attitudes and good attitudes. It is describing the moral shape of the old life and the new life. Bitterness belongs to the old humanity; forgiveness belongs to the new humanity created after God’s likeness.

### 1. Bitterness is more than a passing feeling

The word translated “bitterness” is the Greek *pikria*. It can suggest something acrid, harsh, poisonous, or resentful. It is not simply the first pang of hurt or anger. Earlier in the chapter Paul says, “Be ye angry, and sin not” (Eph. 4:26), so the presence of anger is not automatically condemned. The problem begins when anger is nursed, rehearsed, and allowed to settle into the soul.

So bitterness is not merely a feeling. It is often what happens when a feeling is indulged until it becomes a stance. It begins with pain, but it turns pain into resentment. It begins with a wound, but it lets the wound become a weapon.

In that sense, bitterness is a **chosen posture**, though not always chosen in a single dramatic moment. More often it is chosen by repetition: by replaying the offense, by refusing mercy, by keeping the other person’s debt active in one’s heart.

### 2. The list shows bitterness as a root that spreads

Paul does not mention bitterness alone. He says:

> “Let all bitterness, and wrath, and anger, and clamour, and evil speaking, be put away from you, with all malice.”

There is a kind of progression here. Bitterness is the root; the other sins are its branches.

- **Bitterness** — the inward poison, the settled resentment.
- **Wrath** — the passionate outburst of anger.
- **Anger** — the deeper, more settled hostility.
- **Clamour** — loud quarreling, shouting, strife.
- **Evil speaking** — slander, abusive speech, character-assassination.
- **Malice** — the broad desire or disposition to harm another.

This suggests that bitterness is **contagious**. It does not stay quietly inside a person. It leaks out. It affects speech. It affects relationships. It creates noise, conflict, and suspicion. It turns communities into courts where everyone is keeping record of wrongs.

That is especially important in Ephesians because Paul is writing about the church as one body. Bitterness is never merely private. It grieves the Holy Spirit (Eph. 4:30) and threatens the unity of the body. A bitter person may think, “I am only holding this against one person,” but bitterness usually spreads. It infects conversations, friendships, families, and congregations.

This is why Scripture elsewhere speaks of a “root of bitterness” that can spring up and defile many (Heb. 12:15). That image fits well here. Bitterness is like poison in the bloodstream of a community.

### 3. Forgiveness is the direct opposite because it cancels the debt

Forgiveness is placed directly opposite bitterness because it attacks bitterness at the root.

Bitterness says:

> “You owe me. I will not let this go. I will keep this alive. I will make you pay, whether openly or inwardly.”

Forgiveness says:

> “The wrong was real, but I will not keep it as a living debt. I release it to God. I refuse to let resentment rule my heart.”

The word translated “forgiving” in verse 32 is related to grace. It carries the idea of granting favor, releasing someone, letting a debt go. It is not saying the offense did not matter. It is saying the offended person will no longer hold the offense as leverage.

That is why forgiveness is not merely a feeling. It is an act of grace. Sometimes forgiveness must be obeyed before it is felt. The command is not “feel tenderly toward those who hurt you.” The command is to be kind, tenderhearted, and forgiving — to take the posture of mercy even when the emotions have not yet caught up.

### 4. The ground of forgiveness is God’s forgiveness in Christ

Paul does not simply say, “Forgive because it is healthy.” He says:

> “Forgiving one another, even as God for Christ’s sake hath forgiven you.”

The basis is the gospel. God’s forgiveness is the pattern, motive, and power behind Christian forgiveness.

This is crucial. The text is not commanding people to minimize abuse, deny injustice, or pretend that wounds do not matter. It is not saying, “Just be nice.” It is saying that those who have been forgiven an immeasurable debt by God must not become people who clutch the debts of others.

God did not forgive us because our offenses were small. He forgave us in Christ. The wrong was real, but mercy answered it. So Christian forgiveness is not the denial of evil; it is the refusal to let evil have the final word in our hearts.

### 5. Kindness and tenderheartedness are the opposite of bitterness’s hardness

Paul does not only command forgiveness. He says:

> “Be ye kind one to another, tenderhearted.”

The word “tenderhearted” suggests inward compassion, mercy, sensitivity. This is the opposite of the hardness produced by bitterness. Bitterness hardens the heart. It makes us cold, suspicious, cruel, or self-righteous. Kindness and tenderheartedness soften the heart again.

So Paul is not merely saying, “Stop being bitter.” He is saying, “Be transformed.” The old posture must be put away, but a new posture must be put on. Forgiveness is not only the absence of resentment; it is the presence of mercy.

### 6. So is bitterness a feeling, habit, posture, or contagion?

The text treats bitterness as all of these, but especially as a **settled posture with contagious effects**.

- It may begin as a **feeling** — hurt, disappointment, anger.
- It becomes a **habit** when the mind keeps returning to the offense.
- It becomes a **chosen posture** when the heart refuses to release the debt.
- It becomes **contagious** when it expresses itself in clamor, slander, and malice.

Paul’s concern is not merely inner emotional management. He is concerned with the kind of people we are becoming and the kind of community we are creating. Bitterness creates a culture of accusation. Forgiveness creates a culture of grace.

### In short

Bitterness and forgiveness are placed directly against each other because they are rival ways of living after being wronged. Bitterness clings to the wound and lets it define the relationship. Forgiveness acknowledges the wound but entrusts the debt to God. Bitterness spreads hostility. Forgiveness spreads mercy. And the reason Christians are called to forgive is not that they are naturally good at it, but that God in Christ has already forgiven them.
