Matthew 12:34–35 presents speech as the visible overflow of a hidden moral center. In context, Jesus is confronting the Pharisees after they have attributed the Spirit’s work to Beelzebul. Their accusation is not treated as a mere verbal slip; it is treated as evidence of what their hearts are full of.

### 1. Speech as the mouth of a stored heart

The key metaphor is “treasure.” In Greek, *thesaurou* suggests a storeroom, an accumulated reserve. The heart is not merely emotion; in biblical language it is the center of desire, will, understanding, loyalty, and moral orientation. So “the abundance of the heart” means the inner reservoir of what a person has long loved, valued, resented, believed, and practiced.

The mouth does not speak from nothing. It draws from what the heart has accumulated.

That is why Jesus says:

> “out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaketh.”

Words are like liquid from a vessel: what has filled the vessel will pour out. A heart full of pride will likely pour out contempt. A heart full of fear may pour out cruelty. A heart full of faith may pour out peace. A heart full of resentment may pour out slander.

The passage therefore connects speech with hidden moral stockpiles by saying that words reveal what has been stored up beneath the surface.

### 2. Are words symptoms?

Yes — strongly so.

In this text, speech is symptomatic in the sense that it exposes the condition of the heart. It is not a neutral report; it is a diagnostic outflow. Jesus implies that if the words are poisonous, there is poison already stored within.

This fits the broader Matthean motif of trees and fruit. Just as good trees bear good fruit and bad trees bear bad fruit, good hearts produce good speech and evil hearts produce evil speech.

So when Jesus rebukes them for saying that He casts out demons by Beelzebul, He is not only condemning their words. He is using their words to reveal their hearts.

Their speech is a symptom of unbelief, hard-heartedness, and spiritual blindness.

### 3. Are words choices?

Yes — though the emphasis here is more on revelation than on the mechanics of free choice.

The passage does not present speech as a casual accident for which one bears no responsibility. Jesus condemns the Pharisees precisely because they spoke. Their words were not forced out of them by necessity; they were the product of a settled moral disposition.

There is an implicit responsibility: people choose to speak from the treasure they have stored up. They do not create the treasure in a single moment, but they do reveal it by what they utter.

We might say:

- The heart stores what it loves.
- The tongue releases what the heart has stored.
- The speaker is morally accountable for what the tongue releases.

So words are choices, but they are choices that flow from character. The choice is not merely “I will say X now”; it is also “I will let my heart be shaped by Y, and then I will speak from it.”

### 4. Are words revelations?

Yes — perhaps this is the passage’s primary point.

Words reveal what is hidden. They make the heart visible.

The Pharisees thought they were merely arguing with Jesus. But Jesus says their argument betrays them. Their accusation reveals the treasure of their hearts.

This is why the passage is so uncomfortable: speech is not only communication; it is confession. What we say says something about what we love, what we fear, what we resent, what we worship, and what we consider possible.

A word can reveal a hidden judgment, a secret contempt, an unconfessed jealousy, a stored-up bitterness, or an inner devotion to God. The mouth is the outward language of the inward man.

### 5. So: symptoms, choices, revelations, or all three?

All three, but with a specific shape.

They are **symptoms** because they disclose the condition of the heart.  
They are **choices** because they are moral acts for which we are responsible.  
They are **revelations** because they make hidden treasure visible.

Yet the passage’s strongest emphasis is on **revelation through symptom**. Jesus is not merely saying, “You should control your words more carefully.” He is saying something deeper: “Your words show what you truly are.”

That is why mere correction of speech is not enough in this text. If the treasure is evil, polishing the outward words will not make the heart good. Jesus demands a change of the hidden storehouse itself.

### 6. The paradox of “being evil” and “speaking good things”

Jesus asks:

> “how can ye, being evil, speak good things?”

This may seem absolute, but in biblical rhetoric it is not necessarily saying evil people can never utter a true, kind, or useful sentence. Rather, it is saying that full goodness in speech — speech that comes from right motive, right heart, and right relation to God — cannot be produced by a heart that is fundamentally evil toward God.

An ungodly person may say a true thing, but may say it falsely, maliciously, pridefully, or destructively. The outward sentence may be superficially “good,” but its origin in an evil treasure makes it morally compromised.

In context, the Pharisees may have recognized that Jesus had cast out a demon, yet they spoke against Him in order to destroy His authority. Their words were not merely incorrect; they were evil, because they revealed a corrupted heart.

### 7. The deeper moral principle

The passage teaches that speech is not superficial. It is rooted in character.

Every serious word we speak is connected to something already stored within us:

- What we have meditated on.
- What we have longed for.
- What we have forgiven or refused to forgive.
- What we have worshipped.
- What we have justified.
- What we have quietly cherished.
- What we have refused to repent of.

The mouth is the overflow valve of the heart.

So the passage implies a solemn discipline: if you want to know your heart, listen to your words. If you want to change your words, you must change the treasure of your heart.

In short: words reveal, because words flow; words flow because the heart is full; and the heart becomes full through what it loves.
