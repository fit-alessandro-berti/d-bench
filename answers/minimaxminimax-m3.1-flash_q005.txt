Enemy-love is difficult to take seriously because it contradicts nearly every moral instinct we have. We normally define love by reciprocity: we love those who love us, forgive those who repent, and repay hostility with hostility. Jesus instead commands love toward people who may never reciprocate—and describes it in concrete terms: bless them, do good to them, and pray for them.

That makes enemy-love **more than sentimental**. Sentimentality seeks a pleasant feeling or a harmonious outcome. Biblical love is covenantal and practical: it involves goodwill, speech, prayer, and action. One may fulfill this command without suddenly feeling affectionate toward a persecutor. Love here is not the absence of justified anger or the denial that wrongdoing occurred; it is a refusal to let hatred govern one’s conduct.

Nor is enemy-love primarily **strategic**. Refusing retaliation can sometimes be prudent, and restraint may preserve witness, relationships, or peace. But Jesus’ reasoning is not that loving enemies is useful because they might later become useful. The basis is the character of God: “He maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good.” God’s gifts are not limited to those who deserve them. The enemy does not deserve love; nevertheless, the disciple is to become the kind of person who loves without keeping score.

At its deepest, enemy-love is **moral strength made possible by grace**. It requires:

- self-command instead of reflexive retaliation;
- confidence that life does not depend on receiving the enemy’s approval;
- a conception of goodness broader than personal benefit;
- the willingness to bless and pray for someone from whom one may receive nothing.

The verse “That ye may be the children of your Father” is therefore the key. The point is not merely that love is nice, but that it forms resemblance to God. Just as God’s goodness reaches beyond the boundaries of merit, so must the disciple’s goodness. This is not passivity. It can coexist with confronting evil, protecting the vulnerable, pursuing justice, and refusing to compromise one’s conscience. But justice must not become revenge, and protection must not become hatred.

So enemy-love is neither mere sentiment nor a calculated tactic. It is costly, often unglamorous moral strength: doing good to the undeserving because God has first done good to us, and refusing to be defined by the enemy’s hatred.
