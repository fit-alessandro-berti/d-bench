**Both—but first the verse says that heart and treasure inevitably belong together.** From that follow both a diagnostic and a formative implication. The formative implication is especially strong because Jesus has just commanded a change in treasuring, and his order is **treasure → heart**: “where your treasure is, there will your heart be also.” Treasure is not merely evidence of an existing attachment; it exercises a pull that gathers the heart around itself.

- **Possession becomes invested security.** “Treasure” is more than something legally owned. It is what one “lays up” and relies upon—what one treats as securing one’s future and cannot bear to lose. The contrast is therefore not simply owning things versus owning nothing, but anchoring life in what is perishable and violable versus what is secure with God. Matthew elsewhere makes heavenly treasuring concrete through mercy and generosity, not through purchasing heavenly status (cf. 6:1–4; 19:21).

- **Invested security organizes attention.** People watch, protect, calculate, plan around, and worry about their treasure. The heart can therefore be “with” it even when the body is elsewhere. In biblical language, the *heart* includes thought, desire, and choice, not emotion alone. The surrounding sayings about the eye, serving God or mammon, and anxiety show that Jesus has one’s whole orientation in view.

- **Attention and dependence deepen into love and allegiance.** What I continually guard and rely upon increasingly defines what matters to me. What began as something I possess can become something I serve; verse 24 explicitly joins love, loyalty, and service to rival masters. Conversely, directing resources and hope toward God’s purposes can help gather one’s concern and affection there.

The relationship is therefore reciprocal:

**Existing love directs treasuring; treasuring concentrates attention; attention and dependence deepen love; deepened love directs further treasuring.**

This is not a mechanical promise that any act of giving automatically produces devotion—Matthew 6 has just condemned religious acts performed for human applause. Such an act may simply be an investment in reputation. The question is where one is actually placing trust, hope, and loyalty.

So the passage permits the diagnostic question, **“What does my pattern of accumulating, guarding, and fearing to lose reveal about my heart?”** But it also presses the formative question, **“What is my present way of treasuring training me to love?”**
