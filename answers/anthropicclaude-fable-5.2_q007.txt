The verse can't mean that moral judgments never matter, because the verse itself makes one. "Forgive" presupposes that there is something to forgive, and you can't pardon a wrong you refuse to recognize as wrong. A world without moral judgment would have no forgiveness, only indifference.

The surrounding passage confirms this. A few verses later, Jesus says a tree is known by its fruit (6:43–45), which is an instruction to evaluate. And in the image of the mote and the beam (6:41–42), he doesn't say to leave your brother's eye alone. He says to remove your own beam first, and "then shalt thou see clearly to pull out the mote." The goal is better sight, not blindness.

So what is forbidden? The pairing with "condemn not" suggests it is the posture of a judge passing sentence, as opposed to the act of telling right from wrong. The differences between that posture and discernment can be drawn in several ways:

- **Discernment evaluates acts, while condemnation fixes a verdict on persons.** "That was cruel" is discernment. "He is nothing but cruel" closes the case and reduces a person to their worst moment.

- **Discernment includes the self, while condemnation exempts it.** The beam comes first. The discerning person knows they stand under the same standard, while the condemner needs the other's guilt as a contrast to their own innocence.

- **Discernment serves an end beyond itself.** It serves protection, repair, or helping the brother with the mote. Condemnation is its own reward, which is why "appetite" is the right word: there is a pleasure in it, a small feast of superiority.

- **Discernment leaves the future open.** It can say "this is wrong" and still hope for change. Condemnation claims a finality that requires a knowledge of hearts, histories, and outcomes that no human has.

The verse's symmetry ("and ye shall not be judged") isn't just a threat. It describes a measure: the standard you apply becomes the one you live under (6:38). Someone who condemns without mercy builds a world without mercy and must then live in it. The verse just before sets the frame: "Be ye therefore merciful, as your Father also is merciful." The command is to judge as someone who will also need mercy.

A practical test is to ask what you want when you notice a wrong. If you want the wrong to end and the person restored, that is discernment. If you would be disappointed to learn the person wasn't guilty after all, the appetite has taken over.
