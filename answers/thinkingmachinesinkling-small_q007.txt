Luke 6:37 is not a verse about moral relativism. If Jesus meant that moral judgments never matter, he would be contradicting himself—he spends this same sermon calling poverty “blessed” and wealth / cruelty “woe,” he demands that disciples “turn the other cheek” because violence is wrong, and he tells the woman caught in adultery, “Go and sin no more.” Moral truth is real and urgent. What Jesus forbids is a *posture*—the human appetite to condemn.

Here is the distinction.

### 1. What the verse actually says in context
In Luke 6:37–42, Jesus couples “judge not” with “condemn not” and then immediately uses the image of the log and the speck: “Why do you see the speck in your brother’s eye, but do not notice the log that is in your own eye?” The word translated “judge” (*krinō*) can mean to evaluate, but paired with “condemn” (*katadikazō*)—to sentence, to declare guilty with finality—it refers to **the judicial act of pronouncing someone irredeemable.** Jesus is not saying “never notice sin”; he is saying “do not play executioner with other people’s souls, especially while ignoring your own.”

John 7:24 actually commands the opposite of blind neutrality: “Do not judge by appearances, but judge with right judgment.” Discernment is required. The sin Jesus attacks is **hypocritical, self-righteous condemnation**—the kind that uses moral language to assert superiority rather than to seek truth.

### 2. Discernment vs. the Appetite to Condemn
| **Discernment** | **The Appetite to Condemn** |
|---|---|
| **Purpose**: Diagnostic and restorative. It sees what is broken so it can be healed. | **Purpose**: Punitive and final. It sees what is broken so it can be punished, excluded, or destroyed. |
| **Scope**: Starts with oneself (the beam). It is humble because it knows the judge is also guilty. | **Scope**: Starts with the other (the speck). It is arrogant because it assumes exemption. |
| **Response to sin**: “That is destructive; it harms you and others; God forgives, and I must warn / correct / turn away.” (See his “Woe to you” to Pharisees—clear moral discernment.) | **Response to sin**: “You are trash; I have no obligation to you; I relish your downfall.” |
| **Emotion**: Sorrow, urgency, grief for the sinner (like a doctor diagnosing cancer). | **Emotion**: Contempt, vindictiveness, the pleasure of being right. |
| **Outcome**: Opens space for repentance (the woman at the well is told to stop sinning, not cast away). | **Outcome**: Closes space for repentance; it seals a verdict. |

Discernment asks, *“Is this true? Is this harmful? What is good here?”* The appetite to condemn asks, *“Can I use this person’s failure to elevate myself? Can I finalize them, declare them beyond grace?”*

### 3. Why moral judgments absolutely matter
If moral judgments never mattered, forgiveness would be meaningless—there would be nothing to forgive. The cross only makes sense because sin is real, because falsehood is destructive, and because God’s judgment is just.

Jesus’ ethic is intensely moral:
- He distinguishes sheep from goats.
- He names specific sins: lust, greed, hypocrisy, hatred.
- He sends disciples to “test spirits” and to practice church discipline when needed (Matthew 18).

What he refuses is **human condemnation as a weapon.** He reserves final judgment to God because human beings are too corrupted by self-interest to wield it righteously—especially while carrying a log in our own eye.

### 4. The reciprocity: “Judge not, and ye shall not be judged”
The logic is not “do not evaluate”; it is “the standard you apply to others becomes the standard applied to you.” The one who condenses others with a spirit of finality will find themselves judged by that same spirit. The one who forgives—who refuses to seal someone’s doom—opens the door for God to forgive them in turn.

Forgiveness does not deny the debt of sin; it refuses to collect it permanently. Discernment says, “You have sinned, and sin is serious.” Condemnation says, “You are defined by your sin, and I will ensure you remain so.”

### 5. The practical difference in action
- **Discernment** might say: “That policy is unjust; that behavior is destructive; that teaching is false. I must not participate, and I may need to speak clearly.”
- **Condemnation** might say: “That person is a monster. They are beyond hope. I will not extend grace because they do not deserve it, and I take satisfaction in exposing their failure.”

The first seeks to protect truth and possibly restore the sinner; the second seeks to secure the judge’s moral superiority.

### Conclusion
Luke 6:37 is a command to **moral seriousness without moral arrogance.** It demands that we see clearly—because love cannot be blind to harm—but it forbids us from becoming little gods who hand down final sentences. Discernment is a duty; condemnation is an appetite. Jesus wants us to judge *justly* (John 7:24) but never with the heart that delights in destroying another’s hope. In the end, the verse is not saying “morality doesn’t matter.” It is saying: **Because morality matters so much, do not corrupt it with your own pride—judge truth, but leave final condemnation to God, and make room for forgiveness instead.**
