The proverb treats pride not only as a fault but as a threat to judgment. Someone who needs to appear superior has a reason to dismiss whatever calls that superiority into question. A warning sounds like an insult; admitting a mistake feels like a loss of standing. Pride thus helps bring about the fall it prevents a person from foreseeing: the chance to change course is there, but taking it would require humility.

The second verse sharpens the point by asking whom one would rather be *with*. To “divide the spoil with the proud” is to share in an apparent victory. The temptation is not merely to enjoy the reward but to take it as proof that the victors were right. To be “of an humble spirit with the lowly” is to refuse that equation of success with worth. The proverb does not say that low status automatically makes anyone wise; it praises the spirit that can keep company with people of little standing without feeling diminished. Such a person is freer to hear an unwelcome truth, even from someone the proud would discount.
