**Take the five candidates in turn, because the parable rules some out.**

*False doctrine* is the easiest to dismiss. The Pharisee's theology is unobjectionable. He addresses God, and — crucially — he attributes his condition to God: "I thank thee that I am not as other men." That is formally a prayer of grace, not of self-help. There are rabbinic prayers built on exactly this pattern (the Talmud preserves one thanking God for a portion among the students of Torah rather than among the idlers). Nothing he says is false, either. He fasts twice weekly, which the Law nowhere requires; he tithes everything he acquires, guarding against other people's laxity. He is not a hypocrite in the ordinary sense of claiming a virtue he lacks. If we read him as a liar, the parable collapses into a story about a bad man and a sad man, and teaches nothing.

*Pride*, as a name for the failure, is true but too soft — it suggests the problem is an inflated feeling, curable by feeling worse. But a man could recite this same inventory in a tone of anxious scrupulosity and be in identical trouble.

*Comparison* gets closer, and it is structurally revealing. Note what the Pharisee's righteousness requires: it is positional. He is not righteous simply; he is righteous *compared to* extortioners, the unjust, adulterers, and above all "this publican" — the man standing across the court. His standing depends on that man's remaining where he is. Should the tax collector be forgiven, the Pharisee is diminished. He has built a self whose value is parasitic on other people's failure, which means he now has a hidden stake in their not being saved.

*Contempt* is what Luke himself names in the frame — *exouthenountas*, literally "counting as nothing," reducing to a zero. And it is the specific injury to the neighbor.

*Virtue as display* is present, but oddly: there is no audience mentioned. Unlike the trumpet-blowers of Matthew 6, no one is watching. The display is internal. Luke's phrase is ambiguous in Greek — *pros heauton*, "he prayed thus with/to himself." He may have stood apart; he may have prayed *to* himself. The ambiguity is the point. The prayer never leaves the room.

**What underlies all of them**

The decisive thing is structural, and you can see it by asking a simple question: *what does the Pharisee want?*

Nothing. He asks for nothing. There is no petition in his prayer at all. It is an audit submitted to a party assumed to agree. He has already reached his verdict before entering the temple and has come to have it countersigned. Luke's frame says it exactly: they "trusted in themselves that they were righteous." The trust is what's wrong, not the fasting and the tithes, which remain good things. He has taken genuine goods and used them as collateral — as grounds for a claim rather than as fruit of a relation.

This is why the parable is hard rather than merely satirical. It does not say that his righteousness is fake. It says that righteousness held as an asset stops functioning as righteousness. It becomes a wall exactly where a door was needed. Contempt and comparison are the symptoms of a closed accounting system: once you are your own judge, other people become your evidence.

**Why the publican's posture is truer**

Not because self-abasement is itself a virtue. If it were, we could all adopt it, and it would become the new work — which is precisely the trap the parable sets, and I'll come back to that.

It is truer for four reasons.

*It is accurate.* The tax collector is a collaborator and an extortioner. The Pharisee's description of him is not slander; it is correct. So when he says "God be merciful to me the sinner" — and the Greek definite article gives it force, *the* sinner, as if he were the only one — he is not performing humility. He is describing his situation. He accepts the label the other man assigned him, because it fits. Truth about oneself before God is not a technique; it is the only ground on which mercy can be requested at all.

*It is addressed.* It has a vocative, a petition, a recipient. It goes out. Whatever else prayer is, it is speech that leaves the speaker, and only one of these two prayers does.

*It asks for the right thing in the right place.* The verb is *hilasthēti* — the passive of the atonement word, cognate with *hilastērion*, the mercy seat. It is not "be nice to me." It is closer to "let atonement be made for me." He is standing in the temple, quite possibly at the hour of sacrifice, asking that what happens there apply to him. He wants something done *to* him that he cannot do for himself.

*It contains no one else.* He does not say "at least I'm not a hypocrite like that Pharisee." His universe has two beings in it: God, and himself as sinner. That is the minimum and sufficient population of prayer.

And note the grammar of the verdict: *dedikaiōmenos* — he went down having *been* justified. Passive. Something was done to him. The Pharisee pronounced his own verdict; the publican received one. Note also what the parable pointedly does *not* say: nothing about restitution, reform, or amendment. (Contrast Zacchaeus one chapter later, who offers fourfold repayment.) The justification is not conditioned on the repair. That is scandalous, and it is meant to be.

**The trap**

The parable is constructed to catch its own readers. The moment you finish it and think, "thank God I am not like that Pharisee," you have said the Pharisee's line with the terms swapped, and the parable has closed on you. This is not a clever accident; it is a test of whether you understood. If humility becomes the new criterion by which you rank yourself above the ranked, nothing has changed but the vocabulary. The only exit is the one the tax collector takes — to stop assessing your position relative to anyone and simply ask for something you need.

Two footnotes worth keeping. First, "Pharisee" here is a literary type, not an ethnography; Luke needs a figure of *recognized and real* piety for the story to bite, and reading the word as a synonym for "hypocrite" both misreads the parable and has done historical damage. Second, the parable does not commend the tax collector's *life*. Extortion is not being praised over fasting. What is being praised is an empty hand — and the Pharisee's tragedy is that his hands were full of good things.
