## The Posture: Healer, Not Judge

The controlling image in the verse is the verb **"restore"** (*katartizete*)—a craftsman's word used elsewhere for mending torn fishing nets (Matt 4:21) and setting what has come apart. It is a *present* imperative: keep restoring, patiently, until the work is done. The posture required, then, is not that of a magistrate pronouncing sentence, nor a prosecutor building a case, nor even a spectator offering commentary—but something closer to a physician setting a broken bone: close enough to touch the injury, careful enough not to make it worse, committed enough to stay until the person can bear weight again.

## Why Meekness Belongs Here

Paul says the restoring is to be done by "ye which are spiritual"—and immediately specifies *how*: "in the spirit of meekness" (*prautētos*), which he has just listed as fruit of the Spirit in 5:23. This creates a quiet test embedded in the command: **if your correction is harsh, gleeful, or domineering, you have disproven your own claim to spirituality**, whatever the accuracy of your diagnosis. Correction done in the flesh simply reproduces the flesh's works—enmity, strife, faction (5:20)—even while wearing religious clothing.

Notice also what precedes this verse: 5:26 warns against "vainglory, provoking one another, envying one another." Paul seems acutely aware that *correction is precisely where vainglory sneaks in*—the moment when feeling morally superior feels most justified.

## Why Self-Suspicion Belongs Here

"Considering thyself, lest thou also be tempted" (*skopōn seauton*—keep watching yourself) demolishes any remaining pedestal. The restorer stands on the same ground as the restored. This echoes Matthew 7's log-and-speck, Romans 2:1, and 1 Corinthians 10:12: "Let him that thinketh he standeth take heed lest he fall." The truly spiritual are not those least aware of their fragility but *most* aware of it.

## Why the Three Must Stay Together

Each element guards against a characteristic corruption of the other two:

- **Restoration without meekness** becomes punishment—the fallen person is "handled," not healed, and driven further away.
- **Meekness without restoration** becomes mere niceness—a gentleness so delicate it never actually addresses the fault, leaving the brother in the ditch while everyone speaks kindly.
- **Self-suspicion without restoration** becomes paralysis—"who am I to say anything?"—which abandons the fallen under a mask of humility.

Held together, they form a single coherent stance: *I go to you because you must be restored; I go gently because only gentleness restores; and I go humbly because the same trap that caught you waits for my foot.* The logic is circular in a healthy way—self-knowledge produces meekness, meekness makes restoration possible, and the aim of restoration keeps humility from collapsing into silence.

Finally, the whole posture mirrors the gospel itself: we restore because we have been restored; we are gentle because grace was gentle with us; we watch ourselves because we never earned our standing. The verse assumes that the community of the forgiven will be recognizable chiefly by *how it treats its fallen members*.
