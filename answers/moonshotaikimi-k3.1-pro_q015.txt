The image is sharp because it turns hypocrisy into something physically absurd. A “mote” is a tiny speck or splinter; a “beam” is a massive timber. The would-be corrector is trying to perform delicate work on another person’s eye while something enormous protrudes from his own. The repeated language of sight—*beholdest*, *considerest*, *see clearly*—heightens the irony: the person who claims to see another’s fault with such precision is blind to his own condition. His moral scrutiny is really a form of self-avoidance.

The passage therefore attacks not moral concern as such, but **self-exempting moral concern**. The hypocrite’s problem is not simply that he has faults; it is that he uses his brother’s fault to occupy the position of superior judge while refusing to examine himself. The outrageous disproportion exposes both his lack of self-knowledge and his unfitness, in his present state, to correct others well.

Yet the passage does **not** conclude that the brother’s speck is imaginary or that it must be left alone. The crucial words are **“first”** and **“then”**:

> first cast out the beam … and *then* … cast out the mote …

That sequence preserves correction while transforming its spirit. Self-examination and repentance come first, so that correction can proceed from clearer vision rather than blindness, superiority, or evasion. The other person remains a “brother,” someone to be helped, not merely an opponent to be condemned.

So this is not a command to suspend all moral judgment. It is a discipline for those who exercise it: **do not ignore evil, but do not use another person’s evil as a way of ignoring your own.**
