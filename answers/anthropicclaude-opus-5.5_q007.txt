# Discernment vs. the Appetite to Condemn

## The text itself refuses moral indifference

Read in context, Luke 6:37 cannot mean that moral judgments don't matter, because the surrounding passage is full of them.

- **Just before (6:24–26)**, Jesus pronounces woes on the rich, the complacent, and those whom everyone praises. Those are sharp moral assessments.
- **Just after (6:43–45)**, he says, "every tree is known by his own fruit." He is telling his hearers to evaluate character by its results.
- **The mote and beam (6:41–42)** ends not with "leave your brother's mote alone" but with "then shalt thou see clearly to pull out the mote that is in thy brother's eye." The speck is real, and removing it is still the goal. What changes is who is qualified to do it and how.

Most tellingly, the verse's own final clause, **"forgive,"** presupposes a moral judgment. You cannot forgive what you have not first recognized as wrong. Forgiveness is not the denial of wrongdoing; it is a response to wrongdoing. A world where moral judgments didn't matter would have no need of forgiveness at all.

So the verse is not abolishing judgment. It is diagnosing a particular way of judging.

## What the words point to

The Greek sharpens this. *Krinō* ("judge") has a wide range, from "discern" to "pass sentence." Luke pairs it with *katadikazō*, "condemn," a legal term for handing down a verdict against someone. Then comes *apolyō*, "forgive," which literally means *release*, the word used for freeing a prisoner or cancelling a debt.

The movement runs from verdict to sentence to release. Jesus forbids the posture of the self-appointed magistrate who issues final rulings on other people's standing, and commands its opposite: loosening the bonds.

## Discernment and condemnation compared

| | **Discernment** | **The appetite to condemn** |
|---|---|---|
| **Object** | Actions, patterns, fruit | The person's ultimate worth or standing |
| **Purpose** | To act wisely: protect, correct, choose, warn | To establish one's own superiority |
| **Posture** | Provisional, open to new evidence | Final, closed, uninterested in revision |
| **Self-reference** | Includes the self ("first cast out the beam") | Exempts the self |
| **Hoped-for outcome** | Restoration: the mote removed, the brother healed | Exposure: the brother diminished |
| **Emotional tone** | Grief, sobriety, sometimes reluctance | Relish, eagerness, a certain pleasure |

Discernment asks, *"What is true here, and what should I do?"* Condemnation asks, *"What does this prove about them, and what does it prove about me by contrast?"*

## Why it is an "appetite"

Condemnation is less a conclusion than a hunger. It feeds something in us: the need to feel righteous, safe, or on the correct side. You can recognize it by its symptoms:

- **Speed.** It reaches verdicts before the facts are in.
- **Relish.** News of another's failure brings a small, guilty satisfaction.
- **Generalization.** One act becomes the whole person: "that's just who they are."
- **Circulation.** It wants an audience, which is why it so often travels as gossip.
- **Asymmetry.** My failures have context; theirs reveal character.
- **No path back.** It has no interest in what repentance would look like, because repentance would spoil the verdict.

Discernment can reach exactly the same factual conclusion ("this person lied, and I shouldn't trust them with this") without any of these features. You can refuse to lend money to a known swindler, testify truthfully against an abuser, or name a wrong publicly, all without claiming the right to pronounce on the person's soul.

## The measure principle

The next verse gives the logic: "with the same measure that ye mete withal it shall be measured to you again" (6:38). This is less a threat than a description of how judgment works on the one who judges.

The person who constantly condemns lives inside the standard they apply. They come to expect condemnation from others and from God, because that is the only moral universe they know. Mercy, by contrast, creates the atmosphere one will someday need to breathe. The verse immediately before this passage names the model: "Be ye therefore merciful, as your Father also is merciful" (6:36). The goal is not to judge less accurately but to judge the way God does, with full knowledge of the wrong and a persistent will toward restoration.

## Testing yourself

Some questions that help separate the two in practice:

1. **Would I be relieved or disappointed to learn I was wrong about them?**
2. **Am I aiming at their good, or at my standing?**
3. **Have I applied this standard to myself first, and honestly?**
4. **Is this judgment necessary for some action I must take, or am I just enjoying it?**
5. **Do I leave room for their story to have another chapter?**

## Summary

Moral judgment matters a great deal in this passage. Trees are known by their fruit, specks should be removed, and wrongs must be forgiven, which means they must first be named. What Jesus forbids is the appetite that turns moral perception into a weapon, a pedestal, or a final verdict we have no authority to issue. Discernment serves love and remains humble about itself. Condemnation serves the self and is certain of its own position. The first sees clearly in order to heal. The second sees just clearly enough to wound.
