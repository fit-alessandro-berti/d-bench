## The grammar decides the case

Everything hangs on the purpose clause: *πρὸς τὸ θεαθῆναι αὐτοῖς*, "in order to be seen by them." Jesus does not forbid doing righteousness *before* men; he forbids doing it *for* their eyes. The preposition marks a telos, an aim. The danger is lodged not in the fact of being observed but in the observation being the point.

This has to be right, because five verses earlier the same teacher said: "Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father which is in heaven" (5:16). Read as rules about visibility, the two sayings contradict each other flatly. Read as rules about intention, they fit perfectly: in 5:16 the sightline runs through the deed to God; in 6:1 it terminates on the doer. Same public act, opposite vectors.

(A textual note worth having: the better manuscripts read "your *righteousness*" rather than "your *alms*" in 6:1, which makes the verse a heading over the whole triad that follows — almsgiving, prayer, fasting. The warning is not a quirk of charity but a structural feature of all piety.)

## Why the craving corrupts

**It closes the transaction.** The word for reward, *misthos*, is a wage. Of the trumpet-blowing almsgivers Jesus says they *apechousin* their reward (6:2) — a commercial term for a receipt marked "paid in full." This is not arbitrary divine pique; it is a description of how payment works. You are compensated by whoever you were working for. If the audience was human, the applause *was* the wage, and the account is settled. There is nothing punitive here, only an accounting.

**It converts the neighbor into scenery.** This is the sharpest edge of it. Alms exist because someone is hungry. If the deed is aimed at spectators, the hungry person has been demoted to an occasion for display — a prop in someone else's moral theater. The poor are used twice: once by the poverty, once by the benefactor. A gift that requires a witness has quietly made the recipient serve the giver.

**It hollows the self.** The word Jesus uses in 6:2 is *hypokritēs*, the stage actor, the man behind a mask. The peril is not merely that others are deceived; it is that a self built for viewing becomes a self that exists only while viewed. Character thins into reputation. One ends up unable to be good in an empty room, which is to say, unable to be good.

**And it hides.** Note the opening imperative: *prosechete* — watch out, be on guard. You do not need to be warned about dangers you can see. Vainglory is warned against precisely because it counterfeits the thing it destroys; it feels, from the inside, exactly like generosity. The deed is real, the money is really given, the poor really eat. That is what makes the corruption so hard to detect and so worth guarding against.

## But visibility is not simply innocent

Here I would resist too clean an answer. If the problem were *purely* interior, the practical counsel would be indifference to exposure — and that is not what Jesus counsels. He prescribes the closed door (6:6), the unwashed appearance of ordinary days (6:17), the left hand kept ignorant of the right (6:3). He treats concealment as *medicine*, not as law.

The reason is that motives are not sovereign; they are formed by conditions. Being seen reliably breeds the wish to be seen. Praise is not neutral input — it teaches. So while the moral fault lies in the craving, the moral *wisdom* lies in not marinating oneself in the conditions that manufacture it. Hence the practice, in the Jewish tradition Jesus stood within, of anonymous giving: the Temple's chamber where donors deposited unseen and the poor withdrew unseen; and later Maimonides ranking highest that gift in which neither party knows the other. Secrecy is a training regimen for the affections, not a superior venue for merit.

## The regress, and the way out of it

There is an obvious trap. If purity of motive is the standard, then scrutinizing one's motives becomes the new arena for pride — one preens over one's humility, and the mirror multiplies. Motive-hunting has no floor; you can always find a further self-interest under the last one, and the search itself becomes a species of self-absorption.

Verse 3 is the escape hatch, and it is not more introspection: *let not thy left hand know what thy right hand doeth.* That is a picture of self-forgetfulness, not self-audit. The cure for performing is absorption — in the person in front of you, in the work, in God — such that the question of how one looks simply loses its grip. You do not defeat vanity by staring at it. You defeat it by looking at something else.

And a mercy to keep alongside all this: the rabbis said that one who acts *lo lishmah*, not for its own sake, may yet come through the acting to act *lishmah*, for its own sake. Motives are nearly always mixed, and the verse is a warning against a *governing* aim, not a demand that we withhold bread until our hearts are audited clean. Give while the motive is impure; let the giving purify it. But do not mistake the applause for the wage you actually wanted.
