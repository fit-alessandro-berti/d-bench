Matthew 7:3–5 is not a blanket ban on moral judgment; it is a diagnosis of **censorious hypocrisy**. Its power comes from a single, ridiculous picture.

## What makes the image so sharp?

**1. The disproportion is extreme.**  
A “mote” (a tiny splinter or speck of sawdust) is almost invisible; a “beam” (a roof rafter or log) is huge. By putting both in an eye, Jesus creates a physical absurdity: a man with a plank projecting from his eye offers to perform delicate surgery on someone else’s eye. The contrast is so exaggerated that it exposes the moral absurdity immediately.

**2. The eye is the organ of moral sight.**  
Jesus places the fault in the eye—the very thing we need in order to see and judge. In the picture, sin is not only something we *do*; it is something that **distorts our vision**. A speck irritates; a beam blinds. The man with the beam cannot see well enough to remove anyone else’s speck.

**3. The would-be healer is comic.**  
“Let me pull out the mote out of thine eye,” he says—while, as the hearer suddenly “sees,” “a beam is in thine own eye.” It is like a person with a two-by-four stuck in his forehead offering microsurgery. The humor is the point: hypocrisy is not just wrong, it is ridiculous.

**4. The irony is personal.**  
The fault-finder has microscopic vision for other people’s sins and total blindness to his own. He can detect a speck across the room but has not noticed a plank in his own face. The image turns our normal self-deception inside out.

## How does it criticize hypocrisy?

A “hypocrite” was originally an actor—someone playing a role. The man in the saying plays the role of a concerned moral healer, but his own condition disqualifies him.

Notice what Jesus does **not** say: He does not say the brother has no speck. The speck is real. The sin is the **double standard**—being rigorously critical of another person’s small fault while ignoring or excusing a massive fault in oneself.

The hypocrisy is also in the *manner* of the correction: “Let me pull out the mote” can sound helpful, but it is really officious and self-exalting. It uses “helping” the brother as a way of advertising one’s own supposed clarity of vision.

## How does it avoid forbidding moral correction altogether?

The key is the last line:

> “First cast out the beam out of thine own eye; and then shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote out of thy brother’s eye.”

The goal of the command is still **to remove the brother’s speck**. Jesus does not say, “Leave him alone.” He says, “Deal with your own blindness first, and then you will be able to help him.”

So the passage:

- **Commands self-examination first.** “Cast out the beam” means repent of the flagrant, unrepented sin that is blinding you.
- **Does not require sinless perfection.** The “beam” represents serious, unacknowledged sin, not every human imperfection. If absolute sinlessness were required, no one could ever correct anyone.
- **Restores correction as love, not domination.** After the beam is removed, one can “see clearly”—that is, judge humbly, gently, and truthfully. This fits with Matthew 18:15–17, where Jesus commands fraternal correction, and Galatians 6:1, which says to restore a fallen brother “in the spirit of meekness.”

In short: the image is sharp because it makes the hypocrite’s posture physically ludicrous—a man with a plank in his eye offering eye surgery. But it does not forbid correction; it forbids **hypocritical correction**. The target is not loving restoration, but the self-ignorant, superior, fault-finding spirit that tries to heal others while refusing to be healed itself.
