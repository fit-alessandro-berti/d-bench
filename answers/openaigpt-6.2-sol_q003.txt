Micah 6:8 is demanding because it gives us no way to substitute one virtue for another. We cannot excuse cruelty by saying we stood up for what was right, excuse indifference by saying we showed mercy, or excuse inaction by saying we were humble. The verse is short enough to memorize, but it takes a lifetime to practice.

**Justice gives mercy a backbone; mercy keeps justice from becoming cruel.** To *do justly* is to ask who has been wronged and what is owed to them. Mercy cannot mean overlooking harm or asking the injured person to bear its cost quietly. But to *love mercy* is to seek more than punishment. It means holding someone accountable without reducing them to the worst thing they have done. A merciful response can insist on restitution and still leave room for repentance and repair.

**Humility corrects both by reminding us that we are not above judgment.** We may be quick to name an injustice that costs us nothing to oppose and slow to see one from which we benefit. We may congratulate ourselves for forgiving a wrong that was not ours to forgive. To *walk humbly with thy God* is to remain willing to be corrected, especially when correction costs us something. And justice and mercy, in turn, keep humility from becoming an excuse to stand aside: we do not have to know everything before doing the good we can see.

The verbs carry much of the demand: **do**, **love**, **walk**. Justice must become action; mercy must become something we want, not merely something we grant under pressure; humility must become a way of life. The test is whether we can name a wrong plainly, seek the good even of the person who did it, and still ask what needs to change in us.
