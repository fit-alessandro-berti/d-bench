Short answer: **Luke 6:37 does not say moral judgments never matter.** It warns against a condemnatory, hypocritical, merciless, self-appointed posture of judgment. It actually assumes that moral reality matters: forgiveness presupposes that an offense is real, and the warning about being judged assumes that God’s judgment is real.

### 1. What Jesus is *not* saying

“Judge not” is not:

- moral relativism (“nothing is really wrong”),
- epistemic nihilism (“we can never know or say what is good”),
- the abolition of discernment (“never test, evaluate, or disagree”),
- or a shield against accountability.

The same Jesus elsewhere says, “Judge with right judgment” (John 7:24), warns people to recognize false prophets “by their fruits” (Matt 7:16–20), and provides a process for confronting sin and reconciliation (Matt 18:15–17; Luke 17:3). So the command cannot mean “never make moral evaluations.”

### 2. What Jesus *is* forbidding

In context, Luke 6:37 is paired with “condemn not” and “forgive.” It is part of the Sermon on the Plain’s teaching on mercy: “Be merciful, just as your Father is merciful” (Luke 6:36). It warns against the human appetite to sit in God’s seat, pronounce final verdicts, and withhold mercy.

The speck-and-log image makes this clear. Jesus does not say, “Never help your brother with the speck.” He says, first remove the log from your own eye, “and then you will see clearly to take out the speck” (Luke 6:42). So Jesus expects moral discernment and even correction—but purified by self-awareness, humility, and mercy.

### 3. Moral judgment vs. discernment vs. appetite to condemn

**Moral judgment** is the necessary act of recognizing what is true, good, just, and loving—and what is false, evil, unjust, and harmful. Christians cannot avoid this; love itself requires moral discernment. But human moral judgment must be humble, provisional, self-implicating, and open to correction.

**Discernment** asks: *What is true? What is faithful to God? What will protect the vulnerable? What will lead to repentance, healing, and reconciliation?* It recognizes evil but does not delight in condemning the evildoer. It distinguishes the deed from the person, leaves final judgment to God, and speaks truth with love. It can say, “This is wrong,” while still praying, “Lord, have mercy,” and while remembering, “I too am a sinner.”

**The appetite to condemn** is different. It asks: *Who is wrong, and how can I be the one to declare it?* It relishes exposure, shame, and superiority. It makes final labels, assumes motives, withholds mercy, and treats another person’s failure as an opportunity for self-righteousness. It uses truth as a weapon rather than as a means of healing. It is the spirit of the Pharisee in the temple: “God, I thank you that I am not like other people” (Luke 18:11).

### 4. A practical test

Ask:

- Am I seeking truth and the good of the other person, or my own vindication?
- Am I aware of my own sin and need for mercy?
- Is my aim restoration, protection, and justice—or shame and superiority?
- Do I distinguish the sin from the sinner, and deed from final identity?
- Do I leave final judgment and eternal destiny to God?
- Would I want to be measured by the same standard?
- Am I willing to forgive as I have been forgiven?

### Conclusion

Luke 6:37 does not create moral indifference. It creates a people who have been shown mercy and therefore can name evil truthfully without becoming cruel. **Moral judgments matter; discernment is necessary; but the appetite to condemn is exactly what Jesus forbids.** The verse calls us not to stop making moral distinctions, but to stop playing God—to judge with humility, mercy, and the awareness that we ourselves stand under God’s grace and judgment.
