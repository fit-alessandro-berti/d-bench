The elder brother’s deepest problem is **a sense of entitlement that keeps him from sharing his father’s joy**. His anger and envy are real, and his complaint about fairness is understandable. But his words reveal something deeper: he describes years with his father chiefly as service for which he has not been rewarded. He calls the returning son “this thy son,” refusing to call him *brother*.

The father does not dismiss him. He comes out to him too, assures him, “all that I have is thine,” and restores the relationship the elder brother is denying: “this thy brother.” The issue is not that obedience is bad or that the younger son’s actions did not matter. It is that the elder brother cannot bear to celebrate mercy for someone he thinks deserves less than he does.

So, of the choices, **discomfort with mercy** comes closest—but it is rooted in a wounded, transactional view of his own place in the family. The story ends without saying whether he goes in, leaving that invitation open to him and to the hearer.
