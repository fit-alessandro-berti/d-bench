The image is sharp because it makes a spiritual contradiction physically absurd. A “mote” is a tiny speck; a “beam” is a huge plank. Jesus pictures someone trying to perform delicate eye surgery on another person while a plank is sticking out of his own eye. The scene is almost comic, but the rebuke is severe: the person is not just inconsistent; he is morally disabled by the very fault he refuses to see.

The “eye” imagery matters too. Sin, especially pride and self-righteousness, distorts vision. The hypocrite can spot a small fault in someone else but cannot perceive a much larger fault in himself. He sees clearly enough to criticize, but not clearly enough to examine.

Yet Jesus does **not** say, “Never correct anyone.” He assumes the brother really has a mote. The problem is not the existence of the mote but the condition of the person trying to remove it.

The key words are **“first”** and **“then”**:

> “First cast out the beam out of thine own eye; and then shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote out of thy brother’s eye.”

So the passage does not forbid moral correction; it orders it rightly. Self-examination and repentance come first. After that, correction becomes possible—“then shalt thou see clearly.” The hypocrite is condemned not for caring about another’s sin, but for having a double standard: strict toward others, blind toward himself.

In short, Jesus is not saying, “Don’t remove the mote.” He is saying, “Don’t try to remove the mote while the beam is still in your own eye.” Moral correction is legitimate only when it comes from humility, clear sight, and the same truth applied first to oneself.
