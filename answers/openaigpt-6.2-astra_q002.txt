Pardon answers the question, “Can what I have done be forgiven?” A clean heart answers another: “Can the desires that led me to do it be changed?” The psalmist asks for both. He does not want his worst act to have the last word, but neither does he want to be at peace with the impulses that gave rise to it.

Wanting relief from guilt is understandable. Yet if relief is all I want, my attention can stay fixed on my own distress. After speaking cruelly to someone, for instance, I might ask for forgiveness mainly so I can stop feeling ashamed. To ask for a clean heart is to care about what my words did to the other person, to make amends where I can, and to want to speak differently the next time I am angry—even if no one would blame me.

The word *create* matters: the psalmist asks God for something deeper than a stronger resolution. And the verses that follow show what he hopes that change will make possible: to remain in God’s presence and to know the joy of that nearness again. He wants not merely to leave guilt behind, but to live differently with the God whose forgiveness he seeks.
