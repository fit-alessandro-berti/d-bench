In Matthew 6:1, the problem is not **visibility** itself, but the **craving to be seen** - *why* you are visible.

> "Take heed that ye do not your alms [acts of righteousness] before men, **to be seen of them**" - *pros to theathenai autois*

The Greek is crucial. It is not *"if you happen to be seen"* but *"with a view to being gazed at."* The verb *theathenai* is where we get *theater* - to put on a spectacle.

That distinction solves the apparent contradiction in the Sermon on the Mount itself. Just one chapter earlier Jesus says:

> "Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works" Matthew 5:16

So goodness *can* and *should* be seen. The moral danger is when **being seen becomes the motive and the reward.**

### What Makes It Morally Dangerous?

Jesus goes on in Matthew 6 to give three examples - almsgiving [6:2-4], prayer [6:5-6], and fasting [6:16-18] - all public Jewish piety. In each case he contrasts two audiences and two rewards:

**1. It Inverts the Purpose of Goodness**

Almsgiving was called *righteousness* itself in Judaism - *tzedakah*. Its telos is love for neighbor and obedience to God. When done *to be seen*, you instrumentalize the good act. The neighbor is no longer the object of compassion, but a prop for your performance. The act remains good externally, but is morally hollowed out internally.

**2. It Substitutes the Reward**

> "otherwise ye have no reward of your Father which is in heaven."

This is transaction logic. If you do good for human applause, you *get* human applause - and that is all you get. In Jesus' terms, you have been "paid in full" by men, so there is nothing left from God. The danger is not that God withholds a reward out of spite, but that you have already chosen a lesser, fleeting reward - reputation - over a greater one - formation of character before God.

**3. It Corrupts the Self**

Public goodness done for show creates what Jesus calls *hypocrisy* - literally *hypokrites*, an actor wearing a mask. Morally, this is dangerous because:

*   **It breeds vainglory and pride:** You begin to measure your worth by the gaze of others.
*   **It breeds self-deception:** You can convince yourself you are generous or pious while never actually loving anyone; you love the *image* of yourself as generous.
*   **It makes virtue fragile:** If no one is watching, or if applause stops, the "goodness" stops with it, because the audience was sustaining it.

In short: Visibility tests goodness; the craving to be seen *uses* goodness.

That is why the remedy Jesus gives is not to never do good in public - which is often unavoidable and sometimes necessary for witness - but to re-orient the intention so radically that even your left hand doesn't know what your right hand is doing, Matthew 6:3. Secrecy is prescribed as a discipline to purify the motive, not as a rule that goodness must always be hidden.
