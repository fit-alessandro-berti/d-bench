The parable itself tells us how to read it.

Luke says Jesus spoke it to people who **“trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and despised others”** (Luke 18:9). That is the key. So the Pharisee is not condemned mainly because he is devout, moral, disciplined, or theologically educated. He is condemned because his religion has become a way of **establishing himself before God and above his neighbor**.

If we sort through the options you list:

- **False doctrine?** Not primarily, if by that we mean a formal intellectual heresy. He believes in God, prayer, fasting, tithing, the temple. But there is a false theology hidden in the prayer: the assumption that he stands right with God on the basis of his own moral and religious performance.
- **Pride?** Yes. This is the root. It is self-exalting pride, but not mere vanity. It is spiritual self-sufficiency.
- **Comparison?** Yes, but not comparison in general. He compares himself in order to elevate himself.
- **Contempt?** Yes, explicitly. The parable is addressed to those who “despised others.”
- **Use of virtue as self-display?** Yes. His fasting and tithing become credentials in his own self-glorification.

So the most precise answer is this: **the Pharisee is condemned for self-righteous pride — trusting in himself for righteousness and despising others.** The other things are expressions of that root sin.

---

## 1. The Pharisee’s real problem is not his discipline but the use of it

The Pharisee says:

> “God, I thank thee, that I am not as other men are, extortioners, unjust, adulterers, or even as this publican. I fast twice in the week, I give tithes of all that I possess.”

Notice what is happening.

He is not saying, “Lord, thank you for your mercy.”  
He is not saying, “Lord, keep me from sin.”  
He is not saying, “Lord, give me compassion on this tax collector.”  
He is not even asking God for anything.

His prayer is essentially a report of his own excellence. He mentions God, but God is not really the center. The center is the Pharisee himself. In the KJV, it says he **“prayed thus with himself.”** That is a striking phrase. His prayer has become a kind of monologue. He is not truly communing with God; he is admiring himself before God.

And the content of his thanksgiving is revealing. He thanks God not because he has received mercy, but because he is **not like other people**. His righteousness is defined negatively and competitively:

- not like extortioners,
- not like the unjust,
- not like adulterers,
- not like **this tax collector**.

He is not measuring himself against the holiness of God. He is measuring himself against another human being. And the purpose of the comparison is not gratitude or humility but superiority.

His virtue has become a mirror. He is looking at his fasting, his tithing, his moral separation, and saying, “This proves that I belong to a higher class of person.”

That is why the parable is so severe. Jesus is not attacking moral effort as such. Fasting and tithing are not condemned. What is condemned is the transformation of piety into self-exaltation. His obedience has become a pedestal.

---

## 2. His pride is religious, not merely moral

There is a difference between ordinary arrogance and religious arrogance. Ordinary arrogance says, “I am better than you because I am smarter, richer, stronger, or more successful.” Religious arrogance says, “I am better than you because I am more holy, more disciplined, more orthodox, more separated.”

The Pharisee’s sin is especially dangerous because it uses the language of gratitude and devotion. He says, “God, I thank thee.” But the thanksgiving is not really about God’s grace. It is about his own attainments.

He does not say, “God, I thank you that you have preserved me from these sins.”  
He says, “I thank you that I am not as other men.”

There is a subtle but enormous difference.

A humble person might say:  
“Lord, thank you for keeping me from these sins. Have mercy on me still, and have mercy on those who are enslaved to them.”

The Pharisee says:  
“Thank God I am not like them.”

One is gratitude for grace. The other is congratulation for status.

His righteousness has become something he possesses and displays, not something received from God and reflected in love. He has made religion into a system of self-justification.

That is why, in a deeper sense, there is false doctrine here. Not in the form of a denial of God, but in the form of a denial of grace. He lives as if God’s favor must be earned by religious performance. He lives as if he can stand before God on the strength of his own record. He lives as if he has no need for mercy.

And that is fatal, because the one who thinks he has no need of mercy does not receive it.

---

## 3. Contempt is not incidental; it is central

The introduction says these people **“despised others.”** That is not a small detail. It defines the spiritual disease.

The Pharisee’s prayer does not merely say, “I am different from the tax collector.” It says, in effect, “I am above him. He belongs to a lower moral order. I am near to God; he is far from God. I am acceptable; he is contemptible.”

The tax collector is not treated as a person to be loved, but as a foil for the Pharisee’s piety. He becomes a prop in the Pharisee’s self-admiration.

This is why comparison becomes sinful here. Comparison itself is not always wrong. But here comparison is used to create contempt. The Pharisee does not compare himself with God’s holiness. He compares himself with another sinner, and the result is not compassion but scorn.

Biblical righteousness is never only vertical. It is not merely “I stand right before God.” It also bears the fruit of mercy, justice, humility, and love toward others. The Pharisee has religious discipline without mercy. He has separation without compassion. He has moral seriousness without love.

That is why Jesus elsewhere says:

> “These ought ye to have done, and not to leave the other undone” — tithing, yes, but not neglecting “judgment, mercy, and faith” (cf. Matthew 23:23).

The Pharisee’s problem is not that he tithes too much. It is that he loves too little. His religion has become a weapon of self-elevation rather than an expression of love toward God and neighbor.

---

## 4. The tax collector’s posture is spiritually truer because it is honest

Now look at the tax collector:

> “And the publican, standing afar off, would not lift up so much as his eyes unto heaven, but smote upon his breast, saying, God be merciful to me a sinner.”

His posture is full of meaning.

### “Standing afar off”

He does not presume nearness. He feels the distance between himself and God. He is not performing humility; he actually feels unworthy.

### “Would not lift up so much as his eyes unto heaven”

This is not a technique. It is the bodily expression of shame and reverence. He knows something is wrong in him. He cannot come before God with a confident résumé.

### “Smote upon his breast”

In that culture, beating the breast was a gesture of grief, contrition, and self-accusation. He is not blaming others. He is not saying, “God, I am bad because of my upbringing, my job, the system, or other people.” He strikes his own breast. He owns his condition.

### “God be merciful to me a sinner”

This is the heart of the matter. The Greek word for “be merciful” is *hilasthēti*, which carries the idea of God being propitious, appeased, favorable, merciful. It is a prayer for atoning mercy. He is not merely asking for generic kindness. He is asking God to deal with him according to mercy, not according to what he deserves.

And he says, “to me a sinner.” Literally, it could almost be rendered, “to me, the sinner.” He does not dilute his condition. He does not say, “God, be merciful to me, though I have some good points.” He says, “God, be merciful to me, a sinner.”

He brings no comparison.  
He brings no religious résumé.  
He brings no claim of superiority.  
He brings no contempt for anyone else.  
He brings only his need.

That is why his posture is spiritually truer. It corresponds to reality. Before God, he is a sinner in need of mercy. He does not pretend otherwise. He does not try to cover himself with religious achievement. He stands in the truth.

The Pharisee’s posture is false because it pretends that he has no need of mercy. The tax collector’s posture is true because it admits that mercy is his only hope.

---

## 5. The tax collector is justified not because humility is a better work

This is very important.

The tax collector is not justified because his humility is superior to the Pharisee’s morality. If we say, “The tax collector was justified because he was more humble,” we can easily turn humility into another merit. Then humility becomes just another religious achievement: “Thank God I am not proud like that Pharisee.”

That would repeat the original error.

The tax collector is justified because he stops justifying himself. He does not offer God a virtue to be rewarded. He asks God for mercy. His humility is not the price of his justification; it is the truthful posture of one who receives justification as a gift.

This is why Jesus says:

> “I tell you, this man went down to his house justified rather than the other.”

“Justified” means declared righteous, set right with God, accepted, forgiven. The tax collector did not go home justified because he had a better moral record. He had the worse moral record. He went home justified because he approached God in the only way that leads to justification: as a sinner needing mercy.

The Pharisee asked nothing, so he received nothing.  
The tax collector asked for mercy, so he received mercy.

The Pharisee presented his virtue, but not his sin.  
The tax collector presented his sin, and therefore received forgiveness.

The Pharisee wanted to be justified by his own righteousness.  
The tax collector was willing to be justified by God’s mercy.

That is the difference.

---

## 6. The final principle: self-exaltation versus humility

Jesus concludes with the governing principle:

> “For every one that exalteth himself shall be abased; and he that humbleth himself shall be exalted” (Luke 18:14).

The Pharisee exalted himself. He exalted himself in prayer. He exalted himself against others. He exalted himself before God. Therefore he was abased.

The tax collector humbled himself. He did not claim status. He did not compare himself favorably. He did not demand recognition. He confessed. Therefore he was exalted — not by self-promotion, but by God’s justifying verdict.

This does not mean humility is a magic posture that earns God’s favor. It means that God resists the proud and gives grace to the humble. Pride closes the soul to grace because pride says, “I do not need mercy.” Humility opens the soul to grace because humility says, “I need mercy more than I need approval.”

---

## 7. So what exactly condemns the Pharisee?

The deepest answer is: **self-righteous pride before God.**

But that pride takes a specific form. It is not just a private feeling. It expresses itself in several ways:

1. **He trusts in himself.**  
   He believes his own moral and religious performance secures his standing before God.

2. **He despises others.**  
   His righteousness does not produce compassion; it produces contempt.

3. **He compares himself in order to exalt himself.**  
   He uses the tax collector as a negative reference point for his own superiority.

4. **He turns virtue into self-display.**  
   Fasting and tithing become evidence of his spiritual superiority rather than acts of devotion done in dependence on God.

5. **He refuses mercy.**  
   This is the most fatal element. He does not ask for mercy because he does not believe he needs it.

So if one must choose a single word, the answer is **pride** — but not shallow pride. It is theological pride: the pride of the person who thinks he is righteous in himself and therefore has no need of grace.

If one wants a fuller phrase, it is **self-righteousness that trusts in its own virtue and despises others.**

---

## 8. Why is the tax collector’s posture spiritually truer?

Because it tells the truth about God and about himself.

He knows three things:

1. **God is holy.**  
   He cannot stand casually before Him.

2. **He is a sinner.**  
   He cannot justify himself by comparison with other people.

3. **God is merciful.**  
   His only hope is to appeal to that mercy.

His posture is spiritually truer because it does not pretend. It does not bluff. It does not bargain. It does not exalt itself. It simply says, “God, be merciful to me.”

And that is the prayer God answers.

The Pharisee was religious, but he was not justified.  
The tax collector was sinful, but he was justified.

Why? Because the Pharisee came to God with his own righteousness, while the tax collector came to God asking for mercy. And in the kingdom of God, the one who exalts himself is humbled, but the one who humbles himself is exalted.
