Matthew 12:34–35 builds its argument on a single controlling image: the heart as a storehouse or treasury ("thesauros" in the Greek), a hidden reserve into which a person has been depositing moral content over a lifetime. Speech, in this picture, is not generated on the spot; it is withdrawn from stock. Three features of the passage develop this connection between words and the hidden stockpile.

1. The principle of abundance ("out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaketh"). The word rendered "abundance" (perisseuma) means overflow — what spills out because the vessel is full past its brim. Jesus is claiming that speech is not primarily a performance managed at the lips but an overflow phenomenon: whatever the heart is fullest of will eventually run over into words. This is why he can pose the rhetorical question to the Pharisees, "how can ye, being evil, speak good things?" A poisoned spring cannot deliver clean water at the tap. The vipers image sharpens this: venom is not something a serpent decides to have in the moment of striking; it is what the creature carries within, and the bite merely delivers it.

2. The principle of the treasury ("a good man out of the good treasure of the heart bringeth forth good things"). The treasure metaphor adds the idea of accumulation and prior deposit. A treasury contains only what has been stored in it; you cannot bring out of it what was never put in. This makes moral character something built up over time — by habits, loves, choices, and attention — long before any particular utterance. The verb "bringeth forth" (ekballei, literally "casts out" or "puts out") pictures the speaker as reaching into that reserve and producing from it. Speech is thus the retrieval end of a long supply chain whose warehouse is the heart.

3. The diagnostic consequence. Because words are overflow from a hidden reserve, they function as evidence of what is otherwise invisible. The immediate context confirms this: the Pharisees have just attributed Jesus' exorcism to Beelzebub (12:24), and Jesus treats that utterance as a symptom exposing the state of their hearts. The very next verses (12:36–37) press the point to its judicial conclusion — "by thy words thou shalt be justified, and by thy words thou shalt be condemned" — because words are admissible evidence of the inner person.

Are words symptoms, choices, or revelations? The passage supports all three, and it is truest to the text to hold them together rather than choose one.

Words as symptoms: The overflow language makes speech an involuntary indicator, like a fever revealing infection. Especially in unguarded moments — anger, surprise, casual talk (the "idle word" of v. 36) — the mouth betrays what the heart contains, regardless of what the speaker intends to display. On this reading, you cannot ultimately fake your speech, because sooner or later the reservoir determines the flow.

Words as choices: The treasury metaphor preserves agency at two levels. First, the speaker "brings forth" — an active verb; the utterance itself is an act for which one is accountable, which is precisely why judgment attaches to words in vv. 36–37. Symptoms are not judged; choices are. Second, and more deeply, the stockpile itself is the sediment of prior choices: what a person has treasured, dwelt on, and loved. So even the "involuntary" overflow traces back to responsibility — not necessarily for the momentary word, but for the long curation of the heart from which it came.

Words as revelations: Finally, speech is epistemically disclosive. It makes the hidden public. In Matthew's narrative this is the function the saying performs: the Pharisees' accusation has unveiled them, and Jesus reads their words as a window into their nature ("the tree is known by his fruit," v. 33). Words reveal character to others, to the speaker himself if he is willing to listen, and ultimately to God as the material of judgment.

The three categories are not rivals but stages of one process. Because words are choices accumulated (the treasure), they become symptoms in the moment of overflow (the abundance), and therefore serve as revelations of the person (the judgment). The practical thrust of the passage follows from this integration: it is futile to reform speech at the mouth alone. Since the mouth is only the spout, moral effort must be directed at the reservoir — the heart must be made good if the words are to be good. Conversely, anyone wanting to know what a heart contains, including his own, need only listen carefully over time to what the mouth keeps bringing forth.
