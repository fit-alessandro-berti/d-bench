The elder brother’s **deepest problem** is **pride in obedience**, which in turn produces the other symptoms—anger, envy, wounded fairness, and even discomfort with mercy. But to understand it well, it helps to see how these layers connect:

### 1. The surface: anger and resentment
His opening words show him outside the celebration: “I have been serving you… and yet you never gave me a kid.” His anger is real, but it’s a **symptom**, not the root. He hasn’t actually repented or confessed; he’s defending his own sense of fair treatment.

### 2. The engine: wounded fairness + pride in obedience
He measures his relationship with his father by **transaction**: *service = reward*. In his mind, his long, rule-keeping obedience earned him a share of the father’s joy, yet the father “gave” the prodigal everything without requiring anything in return. This reveals a deep conviction that:
- Love must be **earned** through obedience
- Honor should be **proportional** to performance
- Grace is only fair if it follows a “scorecard”

The elder brother doesn’t just dislike the celebration; he can’t **understand** it because his theology of relationship is built on merit, not grace. That’s why Jesus places this parable in response to the Pharisees and scribes who “grumbled” about Jesus welcoming sinners (Luke 15:1–2). The elder brother is a mirror of that attitude: ‘I’ve kept the rules; why should I share joy with those who broke them?’

### 3. The underlying issue: discomfort with mercy itself
His real objection isn’t that the father was “unjust” in a legal sense; it’s that the father’s mercy **threatens his worldview**. If grace can freely restore the lost son, then his obedience didn’t earn him special access, and his “fairness” is exposed as self-righteousness. He says, “All that I have is yours” (v. 31), but he never actually **lives in** the father’s love. He wants the father’s recognition, not his presence. And he resents that mercy doesn’t follow his rules.

### Which term best fits?
If I must choose one, the deepest problem is **pride in obedience**, because it is the root that produces anger, envy, wounded fairness, and discomfort with mercy. But it’s important to note that “pride in obedience” here is less about being virtuous and more about **turning obedience into a claim on love**. The elder brother volunteers his labor but never asks for the father’s heart. He trusts in his record, not his relationship.

### A pastoral note
This parable isn’t only about the younger brother. It confronts readers who are devout, dutiful, and even morally upright but spiritually distant. The elder brother’s tragedy is that he is **close to the father physically but far in posture and heart**. The father’s first words to him—“Son, you are always with me, and all that I have is yours”—offer him a correction he can’t yet accept: he doesn’t have to earn the family joy; he already has it. The call is not to stop obeying, but to stop turning obedience into a barrier to grace.

So the answer is: the deepest problem is **pride in obedience**, but its real consequence is **an inability to receive and enjoy mercy**—which is why the father has to call him “son” before he can bring him in.
