The image is sharp because it is deliberately absurd, physical, and humbling.

- **“Mote” versus “beam”** is hyperbole. In Greek it is a tiny speck/splinter (*karphos*) versus a massive log or roof timber (*dokos*). A man with a plank in his eye offering to remove a speck from someone else’s eye is almost comic—but the comedy stings.
- The eye is a tender, intimate organ and also a symbol of moral perception. So the “beam” is not just a big sin; it means your own vision is distorted. You cannot see well enough to help someone else.
- The rhetoric forces the hearer to picture the scene: “How wilt thou say to thy brother, Let me pull out the mote…?” It exposes the absurdity before the accusation lands.
- Then the direct address—“Thou hypocrite”—unmasks the role being played. The person acts like a spiritual surgeon while ignoring his own worse condition.

But the passage does **not** forbid moral correction altogether. This is crucial. Jesus says:

> “First cast out the beam out of thine own eye; and then shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote out of thy brother’s eye.”

The words **“first”** and **“then”** show sequence, not prohibition. The goal is not to ignore your brother’s fault forever. The goal is to become a trustworthy helper:

1. Examine yourself.
2. Repent of your own greater sin.
3. Then, with humility and clearer vision, help your brother with his smaller fault.

So the passage condemns **hypocritical correction**—correcting another while being blind to your own sin—not correction as such. It allows imperfect people to help others, but only after honest self-judgment. The criticism is aimed at self-righteousness, not at moral care for a brother.
