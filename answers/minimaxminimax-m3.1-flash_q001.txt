Genesis 4:6–7 presents **sin as a would-be lord over the person, rather than as an irresistible force beyond the person’s responsibility**. Its imagery combines a predatory animal, an approaching temptation, and a rival claimant to authority.

### Moral agency begins before the deed

In context, Cain is angry over his rejected offering and his brother’s accepted offering (4:1–5). God’s questions—“Why are you angry? Why has your face fallen?”—address Cain’s inward state without treating anger as an excuse. The conditional language, “if you do well … if you do not do well,” also keeps Cain’s moral future open. He is not portrayed as a victim of impulses that mechanically determine what he will do.

The passage therefore distinguishes feeling from action without separating them. Anger and envy are movements within Cain that reveal the disposition of his heart, but they do not make murder inevitable. What happens in the field follows upon a moral conflict already present within him.

### What is sin pictured as?

The Hebrew verb here, **רבץ**, means “to crouch,” as a predator crouches waiting to spring; the KJV’s “lieth at the door” loses some of that force. Moreover, sin has a “desire”—“to you is its desire”—and that desire is directed toward possessing or mastering Cain.

Compared with the categories in the question:

- **Impulse:** Anger is present, but the passage does not reduce sin to a passing impulse. Sin has intention and persistence: it waits, desires, and seeks control.
- **Beast:** This is the central image. Sin crouches like a predator at the entrance. Yet the beast-like figure is **sin itself**, not simply the human person. The imagery dramatizes how dangerous the desire can become.
- **Temptation:** There is a temptation-like structure: sin lies in wait and desires Cain. But there is no external tempter speaking, nor any suggestion that Cain is an innocent bystander to his own desire. The dangerous movement of the heart is his own.
- **Habit:** The passage does not describe accumulated habit, repeated practice, or a “second nature.” Sin is already active in Cain’s inclination, but the text does not yet portray it as an entrenched habit.

The best summary is therefore **a predatory, quasi-personal desire seeking lordship over the person**. The passage uses vivid personification, but it does not teach that evil is an independent monster or demon standing outside the human being. It externalizes sin’s agency to dramatize the conflict: what originates in Cain’s own envy appears before him as a creature with its own appetite and ambition.

That personification must not become an excuse. “Sin desires you” does not mean “Sin made me do it.”

### What responsibility does the text assign?

The central command is: **“You shall rule over it.”** “Rule” is not presented as a prediction that Cain will automatically master sin; it is a moral demand. He must refuse to let anger, envy, or resentment determine his conduct.

Nor does ruling mean eliminating every inclination or emotion. The problem is not desire as such, but desire set loose as a rival power over the person. To rule over sin is to govern one’s desires so that they do not rule one’s actions.

Two qualifications matter:

1. **Responsibility remains with Cain.** God confronts him rather than excusing him, and the narrative proceeds to show him choosing murder and then acknowledging, “I slew my brother” (4:10). Sin’s dangerous desire does not cancel his agency.
2. **Moral agency does not mean self-sufficiency.** Cain is responsible for what he does and how he responds, but acceptance before God is not something he can manufacture by his own resources. The passage holds together human responsibility and dependence upon God: Cain must govern his desires, while God remains the judge who grants or withholds acceptance.

### The passage’s overall picture

The text does not offer a philosophical theory of free will. It depicts a person who is neither a detached rational chooser nor a helpless victim of inner urges. It portrays a moral agent whose heart and desires are genuinely in play, who must resist the claim of sin upon his loyalty, and who cannot transfer responsibility for his failure to “sin” or to the desire that sin exploits.

In short, **the beast imagery belongs to evil; the responsibility belongs to the person.** Sin crouches with predatory intention, but it crouches at the door: it must be encountered and resisted, not treated as an inevitable destiny.
