Repentance in Isaiah 55:6–7 involves both **“way”** and **“thoughts”** because the passage is dealing with the whole person, not merely with external behavior. Sin, in the biblical view, is not only something we *do*; it is also something we *think*, *imagine*, *plan*, and *cherish* inwardly. Therefore, repentance must reach both the outward path of life and the inward movement of the mind.

## 1. “Way” and “thoughts”: outward life and inward direction

The text says:

> “Let the wicked forsake his way, and the unrighteous man his thoughts.”

The parallelism is important. In Hebrew poetry, the second line often deepens or complements the first. Here, **“way”** and **“thoughts”** together describe the full scope of human rebellion.

### “Way” refers to outward conduct

The word translated **“way”** means a road, course, habit, or pattern of life. It refers to the visible direction of a person’s actions: what they do, where they go, how they live, the practices they repeat.

So “let the wicked forsake his way” means that true repentance cannot remain a mere feeling. It must involve a real turning away from sinful conduct. If a person says, “I have repented,” but continues walking in injustice, cruelty, immorality, deceit, or idolatry, the outward “way” has not been forsaken.

This implies that repentance is not only private devotion; it has moral consequences in real life. It affects how one treats others, how one uses power, how one speaks, what one practices, and what kind of life one builds.

### “Thoughts” refers to the inward imagination

The word translated **“thoughts”** refers to plans, designs, intentions, imaginations, and inner reasoning. It is not merely a passing idea. It points to the mind’s activity: the inner world where desires are nurtured, excuses are formed, futures are imagined, and values are shaped.

The “unrighteous man” is therefore called to forsake not only his unrighteous actions but also his unrighteous thoughts. This includes:

- thoughts that justify sin;
- imaginations that dwell on forbidden pleasure;
- proud reasoning against God;
- secret plans that no one else sees;
- self-deceiving narratives;
- false views of God, justice, mercy, or oneself;
- the inner world where the heart rehearses rebellion before the hands ever act.

This is a profound implication: **the inner life is morally serious before God.** Sin is not only public wrongdoing. The mind itself can become a place of unrighteousness.

## 2. Outward conduct without inward change is not enough

The pairing of “way” and “thoughts” condemns a merely external religion. A person may alter behavior superficially while the heart remains unchanged. One may stop certain actions out of fear, shame, social pressure, or self-interest, while inwardly still loving the sin, envying the sinner, or imagining a life apart from God.

Repentance that only changes the outside is like cutting weeds without dealing with the root. The outward conduct may appear better for a time, but the inward imagination remains bent toward the old way.

This is why Scripture often connects repentance with the heart. The problem is not merely what a person does; it is what a person loves, trusts, desires, and imagines. The way of life flows from the thoughts of the heart.

So the passage implies that true repentance includes the surrender of the imagination. The repentant person does not only say, “I will stop doing that.” He also says, “Lord, I must stop thinking like that. My inner world must be judged, cleansed, and reformed.”

## 3. Inward repentance does not mean perfection of thought, but reorientation

It is important not to misunderstand “thoughts” here as if God demands that a person never have another wandering or tempted thought. The issue is not the presence of every passing thought, but the settled direction of the mind.

The “unrighteous man” is called to forsake his thoughts in the sense that he abandons his old inner allegiance. He no longer consents to the imagination that shaped his former life. He turns away from the inner world that kept him distant from God.

Repentance, then, includes the renewing of the mind. It means letting God’s thoughts challenge our thoughts. This becomes especially clear in the verses immediately following.

Isaiah 55:8–9 says:

> “For my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways, saith the Lord. For as the heavens are higher than the earth, so are my ways higher than your ways, and my thoughts than your thoughts.”

This context is crucial. The call to forsake “way” and “thoughts” prepares us for the contrast between **our ways and thoughts** and **God’s ways and thoughts**.

Repentance is therefore not only negative — forsaking something — but positive: returning to the Lord and submitting to His higher ways. The repentant person exchanges self-directed living for God-directed living. He stops trusting his own imagination as the final authority and yields to God’s wisdom.

## 4. Repentance is a return to God, not merely moral reform

The verse does not simply say, “Let the wicked improve his behavior.” It says:

> “let him return unto the Lord.”

The movement is relational. Repentance is not merely ethical self-correction. It is a return to God.

This is essential. One can forsake certain habits and still remain far from God if the heart has not turned toward Him in faith, humility, and dependence. Biblical repentance is not just turning *from* sin; it is turning *to* the Lord.

The word often translated “return” carries the idea of turning around, changing direction, coming back. It implies that sin is a departure from God, a wandering into a false way. Repentance is the reversal of that movement.

And this turning includes the thoughts because the mind is where we decide what is true, what is desirable, and whom we will trust. If our thoughts are not turned toward God, our outward reformation will eventually collapse into self-righteousness, hypocrisy, or renewed rebellion.

## 5. The possibility of mercy: God receives the returning sinner

The passage does not end with the demand to forsake way and thoughts. It moves immediately to promise:

> “and he will have mercy upon him; and to our God, for he will abundantly pardon.”

This is the heart of the mercy offered. The wicked person is not told to first make himself worthy. He is told to forsake, return, and seek the Lord — and the Lord promises mercy.

There are several important implications here.

### Mercy is possible for the wicked

The text specifically addresses “the wicked” and “the unrighteous man.” This shows that the invitation is not only for the morally respectable. It is for those whose way is evil and whose thoughts are unrighteous.

No one is excluded from mercy simply because their sin is deep. The problem is not that their sin is too great; the danger is that they will not seek the Lord while He may be found.

### Mercy is grounded in God’s character, not human worthiness

The reason given is not, “for the sinner has earned it,” but:

> “for he will abundantly pardon.”

The abundance of pardon belongs to God. He pardons abundantly because He is merciful. The word for mercy here is connected with compassion, tender mercy, and deep inward pity. God does not receive the returning sinner with cold reluctance. He receives him with compassion.

The phrase “abundantly pardon” literally carries the sense of multiplying forgiveness. God’s pardon is not grudging or minimal. It is generous.

### Mercy is offered now, while God is near

The call begins:

> “Seek ye the Lord while he may be found, call ye upon him while he is near.”

This gives the passage urgency. Mercy is real, but it must be sought. God is near and accessible, yet there is a “while.” The opportunity of grace should not be presumed upon or delayed.

The nearness of God is itself an invitation. He is not hiding from sinners or demanding that they travel an impossible distance to reach Him. He draws near, calls, and promises pardon. The sinner’s responsibility is to respond while the invitation stands.

## 6. What this implies about outward conduct, inward imagination, and mercy

Putting it together, Isaiah 55:6–7 teaches at least three major things.

### First, outward conduct matters.

Repentance must affect the way of life. There is no genuine return to God if one continues deliberately in the wicked way. Grace does not give permission to remain in sin. The mercy promised to the repentant is inseparable from a real turning away from the sinful path.

### Second, inward imagination matters.

Repentance must reach the thoughts. God is not satisfied with outward reformation that leaves the inner world untouched. The thoughts must be forsaken because they shape the way. The imagination must be surrendered because it is often the workshop of sin.

This means repentance includes honesty about the inner life: what we dwell on, what we desire, what we secretly admire, what we plan, how we justify ourselves, and how we think about God.

### Third, mercy is abundantly possible.

The passage does not drive the sinner to despair. It calls the wicked to forsake his way and thoughts, but it does so with a promise: God will have mercy. He will abundantly pardon.

This is the balance of the text: repentance is serious, but mercy is generous. The demand is radical, but the promise is gracious. God requires a real turning, but He receives the turning sinner with compassion.

## In summary

Repentance involves both **“way”** and **“thoughts”** because sin corrupts both the outward life and the inward mind. The “way” represents visible conduct, habits, and actions. The “thoughts” represent the hidden inner world of imagination, intention, and reasoning. True repentance must therefore include both the forsaking of sinful behavior and the surrender of sinful thinking.

This implies that outward morality without inward renewal is insufficient. It also implies that private thoughts and imaginations are not morally neutral; they can be unrighteous and must be brought before God.

Yet the passage is full of hope. The same God who calls the wicked to forsake his way and thoughts promises mercy and abundant pardon to all who return to Him. The mercy is possible because God is near, compassionate, and generous to forgive. But it must be sought while He may be found.
