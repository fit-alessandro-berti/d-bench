The elder brother’s deepest problem is **his inability to share his father’s joy at his brother’s return**. He seems to understand belonging chiefly in terms of faithful service and deserved reward. From that perspective, the celebration feels unfair.

His anger, envy, and wounded sense of fairness are real, but they point toward that deeper estrangement. He contrasts his own obedience with his brother’s wrongdoing, calls him “this thy son,” and cannot see why mercy should be celebrated. The father answers, “this thy brother,” reminding him that the returned son belongs to him too.

The father also takes the elder brother’s place seriously: “all that I have is thine.” He does not dismiss the years of faithfulness. But he invites him to see the feast not as a reward for wrongdoing, but as joy over someone who “was lost, and is found.”

So it is not simply discomfort with mercy in the abstract; it is discomfort with mercy **when it is given to someone who has not earned it**. The parable leaves open whether the elder brother will accept that invitation.
