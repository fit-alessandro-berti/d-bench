**A humble, restorative posture of meekness plus self-suspicion (not superiority, condemnation, or detached judgment).**

The verse addresses a fellow believer “overtaken” (caught/surprised) in a transgression. Those “who are spiritual” are told to *restore* the person—the verb evokes setting a bone or mending something broken, not punishing, exposing, or scoring points. This must be done “in a spirit of gentleness/meekness,” and the restorer must “keep watch on yourself, lest you too be tempted.” Restoration (the aim), meekness (the manner), and self-suspicion (the inner check) are deliberately joined.

They belong together because each guards the others against distortion:
- Restoration without meekness becomes harsh or triumphalist “help.”
- Meekness without the goal of restoration can slide into indifference or enabling.
- Either without self-suspicion (“considering yourself”) easily becomes pride: the corrector forgets shared frailty, treats the other’s fault as alien, and is then vulnerable to the same or a worse fall (hypocrisy, self-righteousness, or the very temptation they confront).

The required posture is therefore *spiritual* in the Pauline sense: Spirit-led people act as fellow patients, not as judges standing above the fray. It assumes the community is a hospital for sinners rather than a courtroom of the already-righteous, and it treats the restorer’s own heart as equally capable of being overtaken. This combination keeps correction from becoming either cruelty or cowardice. 

(The surrounding context in Galatians 5–6 contrasts life in the Spirit with biting, devouring, and conceit; the verse is the practical outworking of that contrast.)
