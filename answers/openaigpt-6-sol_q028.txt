It is destabilizing because Jesus does not answer the crowd’s question on its terms—*Does she deserve punishment?* He asks a question the accusers have avoided: *Who are you, to carry it out?*

That does several things at once:

- **It exposes hypocrisy.** The crowd is ready to scrutinize her guilt, not its own.
- **It interrupts a mob.** Each person must decide whether to throw a stone as an individual, rather than borrow moral confidence from the group.
- **It separates judgment from self-righteousness.** Jesus does not declare adultery harmless; the story ends with “go, and sin no more.” But recognizing a wrong does not make someone fit to punish it without restraint or mercy.

So the sentence is not a blanket ban on judging conduct, nor a claim that all sins are equal. Its deeper challenge is to the comforting idea that *being right about someone else’s wrong makes us righteous in what we do next*.
