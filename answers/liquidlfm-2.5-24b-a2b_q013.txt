**1. Why the sharp rejection?**  

Amos is not merely asking for a “more balanced” worship schedule; he is confronting a **fundamental mismatch between what the people are doing and what God actually requires**. In the eighth‑century kingdom of Israel the elite could afford lavish temple feasts, burnt‑offering sacrifices, and “peace” offerings of fat beasts, yet they were simultaneously **exploiting the poor, cheating the needy, and trampling justice** (cf. Amos 2:6‑7; 5:11‑12).  

In the prophetic worldview of the Old Testament, **ritual worship is never an end in itself**; it is a response to a covenant that includes the command to “love your neighbor as yourself” (Leviticus 19:18). When the cultic acts are separated from that ethical demand, they become **empty performances**—the people “offer me burnt offerings… but I will not accept them” (v. 22). God’s declaration, “I hate your feast days,” is therefore a **declaration that the external worship is morally bankrupt**, and that the “noise of your songs” and “melody of your viols” are nothing more than the soundtrack of oppression.  

Because the worship is **instrumentalized**—used as a ticket to divine favor while the people continue to commit injustice—the prophet cannot simply ask for a “better balance.” The only honest response to such fraud is a **total repudiation of the false worship** and a call to the only worship God truly accepts: **righteousness and justice**.

---

**2. What kind of fraud is being exposed?**  

The passage unveils a classic **moral‑spiritual fraud: hypocritical religiosity (or “religious formalism”).** Its main features are:

| Element of the text | What it reveals about the fraud |
|----------------------|---------------------------------|
| **“I hate, I despise your feast days”** | God’s personal revulsion at a cult that masks oppression with celebration. |
| **“I will not smell in your solemn assemblies”** | The rituals are not pleasing to God because they are detached from the cries of the afflicted. |
| **“Though ye offer me burnt offerings… I will not accept them”** | Sacrifices are treated as a **transaction**—a way to “buy” God’s favor while ignoring the moral cost of the people’s actions. |
| **“Take away the noise of your songs… I will not hear the melody of your viols”** | Music and ritual become **empty noise** when there is no underlying justice. |
| **“Let judgment run down as waters, and righteousness as a mighty stream”** | The promised remedy is **ethical transformation**, not more ritual. |

In short, the people are **commodifying God**: they think that by paying the price of sacrifices they can keep the covenant, while they **fraudulently present themselves as godly** despite:

* **Systemic oppression** – “the hands that rob the poor” (Amos 2:6‑7).  
* **Gluttony and excess** – “the peace offerings of your fat beasts” (v. 23) point to wasteful consumption that ignores the needs of the needy.  
* **Empty ceremonialism** – the “noise” of their music shows that the worship lacks substance.

This fraud is sometimes called **“religious consumerism”** or **“ritualistic hypocrisy.”** The people treat worship as a **means of securing personal blessings** (health, prosperity, protection) while **abandoning the moral obligations** that God originally commanded. The prophet’s harsh language (“I despise,” “I will not hear”) signals that **God’s holiness cannot be satisfied by a façade**; He demands genuine righteousness.

---

### Bottom line

- **Sharp rejection** is necessary because the worship is **morally hollow**; God rejects any ritual that is divorced from justice.  
- **The fraud** is the **hypocritical, transactional piety** of a society that celebrates before God while **exploiting the vulnerable, indulging in greed, and ignoring the cries for justice**. True worship, according to Amos, is **justice and righteousness**—“as waters” and “as a mighty stream”—not a series of empty sacrifices accompanied by empty music.
