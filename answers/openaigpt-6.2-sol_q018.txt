Paul places bitterness and forgiveness against each other because they are two ways of carrying a wrong forward. Bitterness keeps the injury at work in the relationship; forgiveness refuses to let the injury dictate how one treats the other person. Paul grounds that refusal not in the idea that the wrong was trivial, but in how God has treated believers in Christ.

The passage doesn’t neatly classify bitterness as a feeling, a habit, or a choice. It can begin as a feeling without being chosen. But here Paul treats it as something that can settle into a posture and find expression in habits. Its neighbors in the list—wrath, shouting (“clamour”), slander (“evil speaking”), and malice—show how an inward grievance can become a public, communal problem. The list need not describe a fixed sequence for us to see the connection: resentment can shape speech, and speech can draw others into the quarrel. In that sense, bitterness is contagious.

Notice, too, that Paul doesn’t simply say, “Stop being bitter.” He gives the community something to practice in its place: kindness, tenderheartedness, and “forgiving one another.” The contrast is not between *feeling hurt* and *feeling fine*. It is between letting hurt harden into ill will and responding to a real wrong in light of the mercy one has received.
