The elder brother’s deepest problem is **pride in obedience**.  

In Luke 15:28–32, the older son repeatedly emphasizes his faithful service (“these many years do I serve you, neither transgressing thy commandment”) and feels that his loyalty should be met with recognition and celebration. His complaint that his father “never gavest me a kid” reveals a sense of entitlement rooted in his belief that strict obedience earns him the full inheritance and honor. When the father instead restores the younger brother with a feast and the killing of a fatted calf, the elder brother’s reaction—anger and refusal to join the celebration—stems from his wounded self-righteousness. He cannot accept that grace and mercy are freely given to the repentant sinner, insisting instead on a transactional relationship where faithfulness must be rewarded. While anger, envy, and a sense of unfairness are evident, they are symptoms of his underlying pride: he measures his worth by his obedience and feels slighted when that is not acknowledged. Thus, the core issue is a self-righteous pride that blocks his ability to receive the father’s (and, by extension, God’s) unconditional love and mercy.
