This passage from Matthew 12:34–35 establishes a profound connection between speech and the internal moral state, suggesting that words are not merely external actions but overflow of the internal condition. Here is how the text connects these elements and addresses the nature of speech as symptoms, choices, or revelations:

### 1. The Connection: "Out of the Abundance of the Heart"
The central mechanism described in the passage is **overflow** ("abundance"). The text posits that the heart functions as a storage place or "treasure" house containing moral quality—either "good" or "evil."

*   **Natural Flow:** Speech is depicted as the inevitable output of this storage. Just as a container yields what fills it, the mouth yields what fills the heart.
*   **Moral Causality:** The passage argues that speech cannot be fundamentally disconnected from character. An "evil" heart cannot genuinely produce "good" speech because the source material (the treasure) determines the output (the things brought forth).

### 2. Symptoms, Choices, or Revelations?
The passage suggests that words are **all three**, intertwined inseparably:

*   **Symptoms (Evidence of Condition):**
    The text explicitly frames speech as a diagnostic indicator. Jesus asks, "How can ye, being evil, speak good things?" implying that the nature of the speech reveals the nature of the speaker. The words are the symptom of the underlying spiritual health or sickness ("generation of vipers"). You judge the tree by its fruit; here, you judge the heart by the mouth.

*   **Revelations (Unveiling of what is Hidden):**
    Because the speech comes from the "abundance" or "treasure" of the heart, it brings hidden moral stockpiles into the open. Words act as a revelation of what is otherwise internal and unseen. The public act of speaking unveils the private reality of the moral character.

*   **Choices (Expressions of Will and Character):**
    While the passage emphasizes the *origin* of speech (the heart's treasure) rather than the cognitive process of selecting words, it implies moral agency. A "good man" brings forth good things; an "evil man" brings forth evil things. This distinction suggests that the actions (speaking) are consistent with the identity and willful orientation of the person. The speech is the execution of the heart's store.

### Conclusion
Matthew 12:34–35 argues that speech is the **exposure of the inner self**. It suggests that words are **symptoms** of the heart's condition, **revelations** of hidden moral reality, and **choices** that flow naturally from who a person fundamentally is. The passage teaches that authentic moral transformation must begin in the "treasure of the heart," because the mouth will inevitably speak what the heart abounds in.
