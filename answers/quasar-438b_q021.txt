These lines shift pride from a vice of manner into a vice of **perception and structure**.

- **“Pride goeth before destruction”** says pride is not merely a symptom after disaster; it is a predisposition that makes disaster likely. The proud person pursues advantage, rank, or conquest without adequately seeing the fragility, hostile responses, or moral limits around him.
- **“An haughty spirit before a fall”** adds blindness: the arrogant one is already internally elevated, so he does not notice warning signs below him. He falls because his self-image is too high relative to reality.
- The force of the proverb is causal: pride creates a condition of **overconfidence**, and overconfidence creates **collapse**.

The contrast with **“the lowly”** sharpen the moral psychology:

- To be “of an humble spirit with the lowly” is to keep company with those beneath one in power or status, not flattering them falsely, but refusing to detach from them.
- It implies humility is not groveling weakness; it is **relational perception**: the humble person sees dependences, duties, dangers, and the claims of others because he remains among them.
- The proud person, by contrast, abstracts himself from the “lowly.” He thinks he can enjoy the spoils of position while ignoring the people and conditions that produce those spoils.

The proverb’s moral psychology is therefore:

1. Arrogance narrows vision: it makes the world look manageable from a height.
2. That narrowing promotes guilt, because one exploits or despises others.
3. But more deeply, it promotes **blindness and collapse**, because one acts on a map that omits the forces that can undo one.
4. Humility is safer not because it is morally pretty, but because it keeps one in contact with reality, with obligation, and with the consequences of one’s ambitions.

The line about dividing spoil with the proud means: when arrogance has brought success, the proud will demand a share, but that partnership is morally and practically unfit. They corrupt wealth from the outset.</think>The proverb turns pride into a **temporal and epistemic fault**: it comes “before” disaster and before a fall. Pride is therefore not only the guilty feeling one has *after* harming others, nor merely the snobbish manner of looking down on them. It is a condition that makes one **fail to see** the approaching collapse.

An haughty spirit raises the self above its actual footing. From that false elevation, the world looks smaller, others look less significant, and warning signs disappear beneath the horizon of one’s self-conceit. So pride produces blindness: the proud person cannot perceive the limits, resistances, and dependencies that actually support his power. He acts as if his rank or advantage is secure, and then the fall follows.

The contrast with “the lowly” clarifies the psychology. To be “of an humble spirit with the lowly” is not to groveling before everyone beneath oneself; it is to remain in moral contact with those one depends upon, benefits from, or stands above. It means:

- seeing the claims of lesser-positioned people as real,
- refusing to detach from them once one has risen,
- treating position as conditional upon relations, not as proof of sovereignty.

The proud person does the opposite. He abstracts himself from the lowly, takes the spoil as evidence of merit, and then shares it only with others equally detached and self-inflated—“the proud.” That is why it is “better” to abide with the lowly than to divide spoil with the proud: humility is not false modesty but a safeguard against moral and practical blindness; arrogance is not just greed for honor, but a kind of insulation from reality that invites ruin.
