Amos is not criticizing people who are insufficiently religious. He is criticizing people who are *very* religious — and using that religion to conceal a corrupt life.

That is why the language is so violent: “I hate, I despise your feast days.” God is not saying, “Your worship would be nicer with a little more social concern added.” He is saying that, in their present condition, their worship itself has become fraudulent.

### The situation behind Amos

Amos prophesied to the northern kingdom of Israel during a period of prosperity and national confidence. The sanctuaries were busy, festivals were celebrated, sacrifices were offered, music was elaborate.

At the same time, Amos charges that:

- the poor were being crushed and exploited;
- courts accepted bribes;
- merchants cheated with dishonest measures;
- the wealthy lived in luxury built on injustice;
- people trusted that Israel’s status as God’s chosen nation guaranteed them safety.

Earlier in the same chapter Amos says: “Hate the evil, and love the good, and establish judgment in the gate” (Amos 5:15). The “gate” was where legal cases were decided. In other words, the place where God wanted to see righteousness was not only the sanctuary, but the courtroom, the marketplace, and the treatment of the vulnerable.

### The fraud: ritual as a substitute for obedience

The people seem to have assumed a transactional view of religion:

> We offer sacrifices, attend festivals, and sing sacred songs; therefore God is pleased with us and will protect us.

Amos exposes that as a kind of spiritual bribery. They treated God the way corrupt officials treated judges — as someone whose favor could be purchased. But the God of Israel could not be bought with “burnt offerings” or “fat beasts” while the worshippers themselves were buying and selling justice.

Their worship was therefore not merely incomplete. It was being used to do something dishonest:

1. **It manufactured innocence.**  
   Festivals and sacrifices gave oppressors a clean conscience without repentance.

2. **It claimed covenant loyalty while breaking the covenant.**  
   The covenant bound love of God inseparably to justice toward neighbor. They wanted the privileges of belonging to God without reflecting His character.

3. **It turned praise into lying.**  
   They sang to a righteous God while practicing unrighteousness. So God reclassifies their sacred music: what they call worship, He calls “noise.”

4. **It used God to bless the very system God condemns.**  
   Public religion gave divine legitimacy to national prosperity built on exploitation. That is why it is offensive rather than merely inadequate — it invokes God’s name against God’s purposes.

This is why “balance” is too weak a category. A balance metaphor suggests: “You have 80% ritual and 20% ethics; adjust the proportions.” Amos says their ritual has been corrupted at its purpose. Worship that functions as a cover for injustice is not true worship needing supplementation; it is false worship needing exposure and rejection.

This is not a rejection of sacrifice or festival worship in themselves — those were commanded in Israel’s law. Other prophets make the same point: Hosea’s “I desired mercy, and not sacrifice” (Hosea 6:6), Isaiah 1, Micah 6:6–8, Psalm 50. The prophetic point is that God refuses rituals when people use them to evade the obedience those rituals were meant to express.

### “Let judgment run down as waters”

The final image is deliberate.

Their religion was periodic: feast days, assemblies, special offerings, occasional bursts of devotion. But justice — *mishpat* — and righteousness — *tsedaqah* — were to be constant.

“Righteousness as a mighty stream” likely evokes a perennial, ever-flowing stream, not a seasonal wadi that rushes briefly and then dries up. God does not want occasional religious intensity. He wants a society in which justice flows continuously — in courts that do not take bribes, in honest commerce, in protection for the poor, in ordinary daily dealings.

So the fraud Amos exposes is the attempt to separate communion with God from conformity to God’s character. The worshippers wanted intimacy without integrity, assurance without repentance, and sacred songs without social righteousness.

Amos’s shocking verdict is that God will not accept worship as a performance staged to hide injustice. Where justice is absent, the festival is not pleasing devotion. It is evidence in the case against them.
