It's realistic — realistically pessimistic, if you want — but not pessimistic for its own sake.

Jeremiah isn't just saying "people are terrible." He's diagnosing *why* we get ourselves so wrong.

### What Jeremiah is actually saying

Context matters. Right before this, in 17:5-8, Jeremiah contrasts the person who trusts in man/flesh and the person who trusts in the Lord — like a shrub in the desert vs. a tree planted by water.

Then comes verse 9:

> The heart is deceitful above all things, and desperately wicked: who can know it?

In Hebrew it's even more vivid:
* **'aqob** = crooked, twisted, slippery — same root as Jacob's name, the heel-grabber.
* **'anush** = incurable, desperately sick.

So the point isn't only that the heart does bad things. It's that it *misleads* about the bad things it does. It's an unreliable witness to itself. Who can know it? Implied answer: No one can — by just looking inward.

Then verse 10 is the relief and the warning:

> I the Lord search the heart, I try the reins [kidneys — the seat of emotion/conscience], even to give every man according to his ways, and according to the fruit of his doings.

God doesn't grade us on our press release about our motives. He searches, tests, and looks at *ways* and *fruit*. That's scary if we've been spinning ourselves, and comforting if we've been misunderstood.

This fits modern psychology surprisingly well: motivated reasoning, confirmation bias, rationalization. We are very good at making our desires sound noble to ourselves. Jeremiah named that 2,600 years ago.

And Jeremiah doesn't end in cynicism. The same book promises a new covenant where God will write his law *on the heart* (Jer. 31:33), and Ezekiel says it as a heart transplant (Ezek. 36:26). The diagnosis leads to: you can't fix this by trusting your heart more. You need to be searched and reshaped from outside.

### How to talk about motives if your heart lies

If your own heart is a biased witness, you have to change how you testify.

**1. Talk tentatively, not definitively.**

The heart wants to say: "I *know* why I did that. My intentions were pure."

Realism says: "This is the story I'm telling myself..."

Try language like:
- "Part of me wanted to help, but I wonder if I also wanted to be needed/admired/right."
- "My first read on myself is X, but I know I can deceive myself."
- "Here's what I intended, here's the impact — help me see the gap."

That's not false humility. It's 1 Cor. 4:4 in practice: "I am not aware of anything against myself, but I am not thereby acquitted. It is the Lord who judges me."

**2. Talk evidentially — look at fruit, not just feelings.**

Verse 10 is key: God judges according to "ways" and "fruit of his doings," not just professed intent.

Don't ask only: *What did I feel inside?* Ask:
- What pattern do my actions show over time?
- What fruit did this actually produce?
- Would I accept this motive-explanation from someone else?

Introspection alone will just loop. Track needs receipts.

**3. Talk invitationally — let yourself be searched.**

Jeremiah 17:10 pairs perfectly with Psalm 139:23-24: "Search me, O God, and know my heart: try me, and know my thoughts..."

Instead of using verse 9 to beat yourself up — "I'm hopelessly wicked" — or to excuse yourself — "well, my heart is deceitful, can't help it" — use it as a prayer and a community practice:

- In prayer: "Show me what I'm not seeing."
- With Scripture: Let Hebrews 4:12 do its dividing work.
- With trusted people: "Can you tell me what motive you see operating in me? I will try not to get defensive."

We need external mirrors because the internal mirror is warped.

**4. Don't play God with other people's motives — or your own final verdict.**

If only the Lord searches the heart, then:

- Be slow to declare with certainty why *someone else* did something. Describe what they did and how it affected you, and ask, don't pronounce.
- Be slow to declare your own final verdict, good or bad. Leave room for both conviction and growth.

In short: Jeremiah 17:9-10 isn't telling you to distrust *everything* you feel. It's telling you not to trust your self-assessment *alone*. Your heart is a witness, but it's not the judge.

A realistic way to live it: hold your motives with open hands — honest about the crookedness, open to being searched, and hopeful that the One who knows you fully can make you new.
