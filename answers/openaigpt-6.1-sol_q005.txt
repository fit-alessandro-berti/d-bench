Enemy-love is difficult to take seriously because it challenges something more basic than our manners: **our sense of how justice, loyalty, and self-protection work.** If someone harms us, hostility feels deserved. If we love them, it can seem that we are betraying the victim—even when the victim is ourselves.

Jesus’ command unsettles that moral arithmetic. He does not deny that enemies are enemies, or that persecution is wrong. He asks whether another person’s wrongdoing must determine the character of our response.

### Not primarily sentimental

“Love your enemies” need not mean *feel affection for them*, trust them, or pretend they are harmless. The verbs in the passage make love concrete: bless, do good, pray. These are practices one can undertake even while feeling grief, fear, or anger.

Enemy-love therefore does not require reconciliation without repentance, continued exposure to abuse, or the abandonment of justice. You can seek someone’s accountability while refusing to wish for their degradation. You can maintain a firm boundary while still wanting them to become better rather than simply to suffer.

The difficult distinction is between **opposing someone’s wrongdoing and treating that person as nothing but their wrongdoing**.

### Not merely strategic

Kindness sometimes disarms an opponent. But if enemy-love is only a technique for getting a desired response, it remains conditional: *I will love you because it might make you useful, agreeable, or safe.*

The image of sun and rain points beyond that bargain. God gives goods even to those who do not reciprocate. The command is not grounded in a prediction that every enemy will change; it is grounded in the character of the Father.

That makes it more demanding—and less naïve—than a strategy that assumes kindness always works.

### A deeper form of moral strength

Enemy-love requires the strength to resist two temptations at once: surrendering to evil and becoming its mirror.

Retaliation can feel like independence, yet it may leave the enemy governing our conduct: their cruelty supplies the pattern for ours. Enemy-love seeks a different freedom—the ability to act from a good that hostility cannot dictate.

“That ye may be the children of your Father” points to this family resemblance: acting like the Father whose generosity is not restricted to the deserving.

This is not an easy ideal, especially for someone who has been seriously harmed. It should not be used to shame victims or demand instant forgiveness. But its difficulty is part of its seriousness.

**Enemy-love is neither softness toward injustice nor a clever way to win. It is the disciplined refusal to let the struggle against evil destroy one’s commitment to the good—including the possibility of good in the enemy.**
