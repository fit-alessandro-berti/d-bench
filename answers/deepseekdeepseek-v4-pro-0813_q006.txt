The “self” Jesus calls us to deny is not our personhood, uniqueness, or legitimate needs. It is the **self that has made itself the center**—the self that says, “My will be done,” that grasps for control, self-preservation, reputation, comfort, and meaning apart from God. This is what Paul calls the “old self” or the flesh (Romans 6:6). In Mark 8, Peter embodies it: he wants a Messiah without the cross, a God who serves his own vision of power and success. Jesus says that mindset savors “the things of men, not the things of God” (Mark 8:33).

So, “deny yourself” means: **dethrone yourself**. It means surrendering the right to run your own life, to decide what your life is for, and to protect yourself at all costs. It is not self-hatred or the destruction of personality. It is a re-ordering of the self under Christ—losing the false, self-centered self so that the true self, the self made and redeemed by God, can live.

### Why does this sound liberating to some?

Because the self as God is a tyrant. It must be constantly fed with approval, achievement, control, security, and status, and it is never satisfied. Denying that self frees a person from the exhausting project of being their own savior and identity-maker.

If you have already “lost” your life for Christ, then failure, rejection, and even death lose their ultimate power over you. You are free to love, serve, and give without constantly calculating what it costs your ego. And the paradox of verse 35 is real: **when you cling to your life, you lose it; when you surrender it to Jesus, you find it.** The old self dies, but a truer self—forgiven, secure, and hidden in Christ—rises.

That is why many who are weary of themselves hear Jesus’ words as good news.

### Why does it sound offensive to others?

Because it attacks our deepest idol: autonomy. In a culture that treats self-expression, self-fulfillment, and self-rule as sacred, “deny yourself” sounds like oppression, weakness, or a threat to identity. People are told, “Be true to yourself,” “Follow your heart,” “You are enough.” Jesus says, “Lose your self for my sake and the gospel’s.”

It is also offensive because it is total. Jesus is not asking for minor improvements or a more balanced life. He is asking for death—taking up a cross. Many want a God who will bless their plans; Jesus claims the right to replace their plans with his own.

The self being denied here is the self as God. The self being saved is the self surrendered to Christ. That is why one person hears “deny yourself” as slavery, and another hears it as the doorway to real life.
