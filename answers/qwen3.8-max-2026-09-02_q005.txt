Enemy-love is hard to take seriously precisely because it is so easy to reduce.

On the surface, it can sound like a gentle religious ideal: “Love your enemies.” But in Matthew 5:44–45 Jesus does not present it as a mood, a feeling, or a vague wish for goodwill. He gives concrete commands: **love**, **bless**, **do good**, **pray**. And he grounds it in something startling: the character of God, who causes the sun to rise on the evil and the good and sends rain on the just and the unjust.

So the question is well put: is enemy-love sentimental, strategic, or a deeper form of moral strength? I think the best answer is that **it is often mistaken for sentimentality, sometimes practiced as strategy, but at its deepest it is a demanding form of moral and spiritual strength.**

---

## 1. Why enemy-love is difficult to take seriously

Enemy-love feels difficult because it cuts against almost everything in us.

### It conflicts with our sense of justice  
When someone harms us, the immediate moral instinct is: *They deserve to pay.* Enemy-love can sound like it minimizes wrongdoing, as if the victim must simply absorb the injury and pretend it did not matter. That is why it can seem morally evasive.

### It conflicts with our desire for honor  
Hatred often feels like a way of defending our dignity. If someone curses us, we want to curse back. If someone humiliates us, we want to expose them. Enemy-love asks us to refuse the economy of insult and retaliation. That can feel like weakness, especially in cultures where honor is defended through counterattack.

### It conflicts with our fear  
To love an enemy is to refuse to treat them as purely evil. That is frightening, because it makes the world more complicated. It is easier to divide people into “good” and “evil,” “us” and “them.” Enemy-love disturbs that simple map.

### It conflicts with our instinct for control  
Retaliation gives us a sense of power. Hatred gives us a narrative: *I am right, they are wrong, and the world will make sense once they suffer for it.* Enemy-love surrenders that narrative. It says, “I will not let the enemy determine my inner life.”

That is why enemy-love can seem unrealistic. But its difficulty may be part of its point.

---

## 2. Is it sentimental?

Enemy-love becomes sentimental when it is reduced to warm feelings or moral niceness.

Sentimental enemy-love says things like:

- “Just be nice.”
- “Everything will work out if we love one another.”
- “Don’t be negative.”
- “Forgive and forget.”

That kind of version is shallow because it ignores real evil, real injury, and real power. It can become a way of silencing victims or pressuring them into premature reconciliation.

But Jesus’ command in Matthew 5 is not sentimental. He does not say, “Feel affection for your enemies.” The love he commands is not primarily emotional. In the New Testament, the word often used for this kind of love is **agapē**, which points less toward spontaneous affection and more toward **willed goodwill, active mercy, and refusal to dehumanize**.

Notice the verbs:

- **Love** your enemies  
- **Bless** those who curse you  
- **Do good** to those who hate you  
- **Pray** for those who persecute you  

These are actions, not moods.

In fact, enemy-love in this sense may be most real when it costs us something. It is not the absence of anger; it is the refusal to let anger become the final authority. It is not pretending the enemy is harmless; it is refusing to let hatred define us.

So enemy-love is not sentimental unless we domesticate it. At its truest, it is clear-eyed. It sees the enemy as dangerous, wounded, capable of evil, and still beloved by God.

---

## 3. Is it strategic?

Enemy-love can be strategic, but if it is only strategic, it becomes manipulative.

There is a legitimate strategic dimension to enemy-love. Hatred usually escalates hatred. Retaliation often produces more retaliation. Violence often breeds more violence. In that sense, loving the enemy can be a way of breaking the cycle.

This is one reason enemy-love appears in nonviolent resistance movements. Martin Luther King Jr., for example, drew deeply on this teaching. He argued that love of the enemy is not passive submission but active resistance that refuses to demonize the opponent. The goal is not merely to defeat the enemy, but to create the possibility of reconciliation.

But strategy alone is unstable. If I love my enemy only because it is the most effective way to defeat them, then I am still using love as a weapon. The enemy remains an object to be managed, not a person to be loved.

The text in Matthew 5 gives a different motive:

> “That ye may be the children of your Father which is in heaven.”

The purpose is not merely social effectiveness. It is **participation in the character of God**.

God’s sun and rain fall on the evil and the good. This is often called “common grace”: God sustains and gives good things even to those who do not deserve them. That does not mean God is indifferent to injustice. It means God’s goodness is not exhausted by immediate retribution. There is patience. There is mercy. There is space for repentance.

So enemy-love may have strategic wisdom, but its deepest ground is theological: we love because we are called to reflect the Father.

---

## 4. Is it a deeper form of moral strength?

Yes — if understood rightly.

Enemy-love is not weakness disguised as virtue. It is one of the hardest moral disciplines a person can practice. It requires strength because it refuses the easiest reactions: revenge, contempt, bitterness, and dehumanization.

It is morally strong because it does several difficult things at once.

### It refuses to make evil the center of our lives  
Hatred can become a kind of idolatry. The enemy occupies our thoughts. We rehearse their offenses. We imagine their punishment. Enemy-love says: *You will not have that place in me.*

That is not weakness. That is inner freedom.

### It preserves the humanity of the enemy  
This is not the same as excusing the enemy. One can oppose someone fiercely while still refusing to reduce them to a monster. Enemy-love says: “You are capable of terrible evil, but you are not beyond the reach of God.”

That is a difficult, costly stance. It is easier to hate a symbol than a person.

### It leaves room for justice without being consumed by vengeance  
Enemy-love does not necessarily abolish punishment, self-defense, or resistance. It rejects hatred as the ruling motive. One can resist an enemy, restrain them, expose their evil, and even oppose them forcefully, while still praying for them and refusing to desire their destruction for its own sake.

This is the difference between justice and revenge. Justice can seek the good of the community and the correction of the wrongdoer. Revenge often merely seeks suffering.

### It transforms the person who practices it  
Perhaps the deepest reason enemy-love is strong is that it changes the lover. Hatred narrows the soul. Prayer for an enemy enlarges it. Doing good to someone who hates us disrupts our own self-righteousness. It forces us to ask whether we want the enemy to be restored, or whether we secretly want them to be punished.

That is a terrifying question.

---

## 5. The theological heart: “That ye may be the children of your Father”

The key phrase is this:

> “That ye may be the children of your Father which is in heaven.”

Jesus is not saying, “Love your enemies so that God will accept you.” He is saying, “This is what life in God’s family looks like.”

Children often resemble their parents. If God is merciful even to the undeserving, then those who belong to God will show mercy even to the undeserving. The sun and rain illustrate this: God gives life-sustaining gifts even to those who are evil, not because evil does not matter, but because God’s goodness is greater than our categories of deserving.

This makes enemy-love more than ethics. It is a form of witness. It says: “God is not like us. God’s mercy is wider than our grudges.”

---

## 6. What enemy-love is not

It is important to say this clearly, because this teaching has often been used to harm people.

Enemy-love does **not** mean:

- pretending abuse was not wrong  
- staying bound to an abusive person  
- trusting someone who has not shown repentance  
- suppressing righteous anger  
- refusing to seek justice  
- pretending forgiveness requires immediate reconciliation  
- approving evil in order to appear holy  

Enemy-love can include resistance, rebuke, boundaries, and even punishment. But it refuses to let hatred be the final word.

There is a difference between saying, “I hope you are destroyed,” and saying, “I hope you are stopped, judged, and changed.” The first is vengeance. The second can be love.

---

## 7. So why is it so difficult to take seriously?

Because it asks too much of us.

Sentimental morality asks little: just feel kindly. Strategic morality asks for calculation: do what works. But enemy-love asks for something deeper: the reordering of the heart.

It asks us to believe that another human being — even a hostile one — is still held within God’s world of sun and rain. It asks us to resist the pleasure of hatred. It asks us to pray for the person we would rather see punished. It asks us to do good where we would rather withhold it.

That is why it can seem impossible. But perhaps it is meant to be impossible in the ordinary economy of the self. It becomes possible only through grace.

---

## Conclusion

Enemy-love is difficult to take seriously because it offends our instincts for justice, honor, and self-protection. It can easily be trivialized into sentimentality or reduced to strategy. But in Matthew 5:44–45, it is neither mere feeling nor mere technique.

It is a **deeper form of moral strength**: the strength to refuse dehumanization, to resist the domination of resentment, to hope for the enemy’s conversion rather than destruction, and to reflect the indiscriminate mercy of God.

In the end, enemy-love is hard not because it is weak, but because it is costly. It asks us to love as God loves — not by approving evil, but by refusing to let evil have the final claim on another human soul.
