Enemy-love is difficult to take seriously because Jesus is not asking for something mild. He is asking us to refuse the most natural moral logic we have: *treat people according to what they have done to us.*

That is why the command sounds either naive or impossible. But in Matthew 5, it is neither sentimental nor merely strategic. It describes a deeper form of strength: the refusal to let an enemy determine the kind of person you become.

### What Jesus actually commands

Notice the verbs: **love, bless, do good, pray.**

Jesus does not say, “Feel warmly toward your enemies,” or “Pretend they have not harmed you.” He names the harm honestly: they curse, hate, misuse, and persecute.

The love he commands is practical:

- **Bless** those who curse — govern your speech.
- **Do good** to those who hate — govern your actions.
- **Pray** for those who persecute — govern your inner life.

This is not affection. The Greek word is *agape*: willing and seeking another person’s good, even when there is no attraction, friendship, or reciprocity. You may not like your enemy, trust your enemy, or feel safe with your enemy. Jesus is asking something else: that you refuse to wish their destruction or treat them as less than human.

### Why it feels so implausible

First, enemy-love violates our instinct for fairness. We are comfortable loving people after they apologize, change, or prove themselves worthy. Jesus reverses the order. God “maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good.” Divine generosity does not wait for moral improvement. Verse 45 gives the reason for the command: *“That ye may be the children of your Father.”* In other words, this is about family resemblance. God’s children begin to act like God.

Second, enemy-love threatens our identity. Groups often hold themselves together through a shared enemy. “We” know who we are because we are not like “them.” To bless an enemy can feel like betrayal — of our tribe, our cause, or even our own suffering.

Third, it exposes the pleasure of contempt. Hatred can feel clarifying. It gives pain a target and makes us feel morally awake. Enemy-love asks us to surrender that bitter energy without surrendering the truth about what happened.

Finally, it appears to reward evil. If I do good to someone who harms me, will I not simply enable them? That fear is legitimate. Enemy-love does not require naivety, unlimited access, or the abandonment of justice. Love can resist, expose, restrain, prosecute, and set firm boundaries. What it refuses is vengeance, dehumanization, and cruelty.

Love and reconciliation are also different. Love can be one-sided; reconciliation cannot. It requires repentance, truth, and rebuilt trust.

### Sentimental, strategic, or strong?

It can be **strategic**. Martin Luther King Jr. argued that nonviolent love exposes injustice, breaks cycles of retaliation, and aims not merely to defeat an opponent but to create a more just community. Enemy-love can accomplish what hatred cannot.

But if it were *only* strategic, we would abandon it whenever it failed to produce results. Jesus does not say, “Love your enemies because it will work.” He says, in effect, “Love them because this is what God is like.”

Nor is it **sentimental**. Sentimentality avoids hard truth in order to preserve pleasant feelings. Jesus does the opposite. He looks directly at persecution and commands costly action anyway. Prayer for an enemy is especially unsentimental: it forces us to bring the person we would rather curse into the presence of God and ask for their genuine good.

At its deepest, then, enemy-love is **moral strength**. It is the strength not to mirror evil. Anyone can return curse for curse; retaliation requires little imagination. But to absorb an injury without transmitting it — to remain truthful without becoming hateful — requires discipline, courage, and an unusually free soul.

This is why prayer is not a decorative addition to the command. It is where the transformation begins. It is very difficult to keep praying honestly for someone’s good while continuing to enjoy fantasies of their humiliation. Prayer does not excuse them. It prevents their evil from colonizing your heart.

Enemy-love does not mean calling evil good, just as sunlight falling on an unjust person does not mean God approves of injustice. It means we refuse to confine our humanity within the boundaries set by someone else’s wrongdoing.

The radical claim of Matthew 5 is that the strongest person is not the one who can dominate an enemy, but the one who can encounter an enemy without surrendering love, truth, or self-command.
