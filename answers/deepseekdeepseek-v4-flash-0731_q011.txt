The movement from “cease to do evil” to “learn to do well” is intentional and significant.

First, there is a *negative* step: repentance means turning from evil, stopping harmful actions, and removing them from God’s sight. That is necessary, but it is not enough. Purity is not the same as goodness; a clean empty vessel is still empty.

Then comes the *positive* step: “Learn to do well.” The Hebrew word for “learn” here implies habitual practice, training, and formation. Goodness is not just the absence of evil; it is something you must actively pursue and become skilled in. Just as you must learn a language or an art, you must learn justice and compassion.

The following commands make this concrete:

- “Seek judgment” / “seek justice” — pursue what is right, not merely avoid what is wrong.
- “Relieve the oppressed” — intervene on behalf of those crushed by injustice.
- “Judge the fatherless” — defend those who have no one to stand up for them.
- “Plead for the widow” — advocate for the vulnerable and marginalized.

So the text suggests that true goodness is not mere restraint; it is a **practiced form of love and justice**. It requires both a turning from evil and a turning toward active care for others. Goodness is learned in the doing, shaped by deliberate acts of mercy, and measured by how we treat the weakest among us.
