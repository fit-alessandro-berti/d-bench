To understand Jeremiah 17:9–10, it helps to know that in ancient Hebrew thought, the "heart" (*lev*) is not merely the seat of emotions. It represents the entire inner person: the mind, the will, the conscience, and the decision-making center. 

When read in its historical and literary context, this passage is **primarily a realistic critique of self-deception**, though different theological traditions debate just how pessimistic it intends to be about human nature as a whole.

Here is an exploration of the two parts of your question.

---

### 1. Pessimistic about Human Nature, or Realistic about Self-Deception?

While this verse is famously used in certain Christian traditions (particularly Reformed theology) to argue for "total depravity"—the idea that human nature is fundamentally corrupt—within the context of the Book of Jeremiah, the immediate point is **epistemological**. It is about what humans can and cannot *know* about themselves.

**The Context of Judah’s Self-Deception**
Jeremiah was speaking to the people of Judah just before the Babylonian exile. The nation was steeped in idolatry and social injustice, yet they genuinely believed they were safe and righteous because they possessed the Temple and performed the correct religious rituals. They were lying to themselves about their own spiritual health. 

**The Nuance of the Hebrew Text**
* **"Deceitful" (*aqov*):** This shares a root with the name *Jacob* (the heel-grabber or supplanter). It means crooked, uneven, or slippery. The human mind is adept at finding crooked paths to justify what it wants to do.
* **"Desperately wicked" (*anush*):** Most modern translations render this as "desperately sick," "incurable," or "beyond cure" rather than "wicked." It is a medical metaphor, not just a moral one. 

**The Verdict:**
The passage is **realistic about the mechanics of rationalization**. Jeremiah is pointing out a psychological blind spot that modern cognitive science readily confirms: humans do not usually act out of pure, conscious malice; rather, we do what we want and then our minds ("hearts") invent noble-sounding justifications for it. Because the very instrument we use to judge ourselves (our mind) is the thing that is broken, we cannot fully diagnose ourselves ("Who can know it?").

---

### 2. How Should Someone Talk About Motives?

If our own inner witness is biased and prone to rationalizing, talking about our motives requires a fundamental shift away from certainty and toward humility. Looking closely at verse 10 provides a roadmap for how to handle this.

#### A. Shift from Pure Introspection to Observable "Fruit"
Notice the irony in verse 10: God says He searches the hidden heart and mind ("the reins," literally the kidneys, seen as the seat of deep emotion), yet how does He actually render judgment? *"According to his ways, and according to the **fruit of his doings**."*

Even God ties the reality of the invisible heart to visible outcomes. If we want to talk accurately about our motives, we cannot just look *inward* at how we feel; we must look *outward* at the patterns of our behavior. 
* **Instead of saying:** "I know my heart is in the right place, even though people keep getting hurt."
* **Say:** "I thought my intentions were good, but the 'fruit' of my actions is causing harm, which means there might be something in my motives I'm not seeing."

#### B. Adopt Epistemic Humility (Hold Motives Loosely)
Because the heart is an unreliable narrator, we should abandon absolute claims about our own purity. The Apostle Paul echoes Jeremiah’s exact logic in 1 Corinthians 4:4 when he writes, *"My conscience is clear, but that does not make me innocent. It is the Lord who judges me."* 

When discussing why you did something, it is healthier and more accurate to speak in terms of **mixed motives**. Rarely do humans do anything out of 100% altruism or 100% malice.
* **Instead of saying:** "I am only telling you this because I love you and want the best for you."
* **Say:** "I think I’m sharing this to be helpful, though I admit part of me might just be frustrated and wanting to control the outcome."

#### C. Invite External Perspective
"Who can know it?" implies that a closed system cannot correct itself. In Jeremiah, the external perspective is God—prayer becomes a way of asking an objective observer to expose blind spots (as in Psalm 139: *"Search me, O God, and know my heart... see if there is any offensive way in me"*). 

On a human level, this means relying on trusted, honest community. Because we are blind to our own self-deception, other people can often read our motives through our body language, tone, and track record far better than we can read them through introspection. Talking about motives should be a **dialogue**, not a monologue.

### Summary
Jeremiah 17:9–10 does not require us to walk around in despair, assuming every human impulse is pure evil. Rather, it invites us to stop trusting our own PR department. If the heart is a master of rationalization, the most honest way to talk about our motives is with **tentativeness**, judging ourselves less by the noble stories we tell inside our heads, and more by the actual footprints we leave behind in the world.
