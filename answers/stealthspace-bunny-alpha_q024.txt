The difference is not merely **how much someone obeys**, but **what kind of person is being obeyeds**—more precisely, whether obedience expresses a heart already aligned with God.

### External compliance: right actions, unchanged heart

External compliance means following God’s commands—or a community’s version of them—without a corresponding inward renewal. A person may keep the rules to avoid punishment, gain approval, or feel morally superior. They may even make a sincere effort to do right, while their deepest desires remain self-directed.

External obedience is not necessarily dishonest or morally worthless. But it can conceal division: a person may know what is right, perform it, and still resist God’s authority at heart. Outward conformity does not by itself mean whole-person trust, love, or devotion.

In the Bible, this is why a “hard heart” is not simply an ignorant heart or a weak one. It is a heart whose settled dispositions have become resistant to God’s word.

### Receiving a “new heart”: inward transformation

In Scripture, the **heart** represents the inner person—not just emotions, but desires, convictions, moral resolve, and the fundamental orientation of life. A new heart therefore means that God renews how a person thinks, wants, and lives before Him.

The sequence in Ezekiel 36:26–27 is significant:

1. God gives a new heart.
2. He removes the stony heart.
3. He gives a heart of flesh.
4. He puts His Spirit within them.
5. He enables them to walk in His statutes and judgments.

The last point is the consequence of the preceding renewal, not its independent goal. The promise is not that a renewed person receives a better technique for rule-keeping. It is that God changes the desires and allegiance from which obedience flows. What was externally imposed becomes an expression of inward faith and loyalty.

The repeated **“I will”** also emphasizes that this renewal is God’s gracious work. A person can resolve to behave better; he cannot manufacture a new heart. Receiving God’s gift is not the same as earning it.

### Why is hardness described like a material condition?

“Stony” is figurative, not a claim that a physical heart has literally turned to stone. But the image is deliberate: **stone is hard, unresponsive, and resistant to being worked into something different.**

Hardness describes an entrenched condition, not a small defect in behavior. It suggests that a person’s inner commitments and attachments have become fixed in resistance to God. Therefore, God promises to **take away and replace** that heart rather than merely improve outward behavior. A person’s deepest resistance cannot be overcome by social pressure, education, or stronger self-control alone; restoration requires divine intervention.

A “heart of flesh” is likewise more than becoming more emotional. It means a heart made responsive to God: soft to correction, capable of sincere faith, and oriented toward His justice and mercy. The language does not promise immediate sinlessness. Rather, it promises a restored direction, Spirit-given power, and a life bearing the mark of God’s law.

### The difference is not obedience versus freedom from obedience

The new heart is **not** freedom from God’s statutes. It is freedom to pursue them from a renewed heart. Ezekiel’s point is that God does not want His people merely constrained by His commands; He wants His Spirit to write His law upon them, so that faithfulness becomes their inward desire (cf. Jeremiah 31:33; Hebrews 8:10).

In short: **external compliance restrains conduct; a new heart transforms the person whose conduct needs transforming.** God promises not only that His people will do right, but that they will have hearts and a Spirit capable of loving obedience to Him.
