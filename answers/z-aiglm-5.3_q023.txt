The pairing is deliberate and, in Hebrew, exact. Hebrew poetry rarely repeats itself; it completes itself. "The wicked … his way" and "the unrighteous man … his thoughts" are not two classes of sinner but one sinner addressed at two depths: the road he walks (*derek*) and the drafting room where the road is designed (*machashavah*). Sin has two addresses, and repentance must serve notice on both.

### "His way": conduct as trajectory

*Derek* is a traveled road—habituated course, not isolated incident. To forsake one's way is not to regret individual missteps while remaining on the same road; it is to leave the road. That is why the governing verb is *shuv*—"return," the Hebrew word for repentance itself. The image is spatial: the wicked is a traveler headed away from God, and repentance is an about-face. Note also that the verse does not stop at "forsake his way"—it aims at "return unto the Lord." The goal is not moral renovation on one's own terms but homecoming. Yet the demand is real and outward: no purely interior repentance that leaves the road untraveled is contemplated.

### "His thoughts": the imagination as engine

*Machashavah* is not idle cognition but the devising, planning, imagining mind—the same word the KJV renders "imagination" in Genesis 6:5, "every imagination of the thoughts of his heart." Sin is drafted before it is performed: "He deviseth mischief upon his bed; he setteth himself in a way that is not good" (Ps. 36:4)—bed first, then road. But the traffic runs both ways: thoughts beget ways, and traveled ways groove the mind, teaching the imagination what to want. The double demand is therefore diagnosis, not redundancy. Forsake the way but keep the thoughts, and the imagination will re-pave the road. Renounce the thoughts but keep walking the way, and the road will re-train the mind. Half-repentance is, over time, no repentance.

The hinge is the very next verse: "For my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways … as the heavens are higher than the earth" (vv. 8–9). The repentance commanded in v. 7 is answered point for point by the transcendence declared in vv. 8–9: *your* ways and thoughts exchanged for *His*. "Forsaking thoughts" is thus not mindlessness but surrender of the self-authored life-plan—the fantasy in which one is one's own god—for thoughts one could never have generated (the same insight carried by *metanoia*, "change of mind," and by "casting down imaginations," 2 Cor. 10:5).

### Mercy matching the demand

Because sin has occupied both spheres, pardon must cover both—hence the emphatic Hebrew: *ki-yarbeh lislōach*, "for he multiplies to forgive." "Abundantly pardon" catches it. Two things deepen the promise:

**First, the verb for pardon (*salach*) is, throughout the Hebrew Bible, used only with God as subject.** Pardon is a divine prerogative—and here it is exercised profusely, not doled out in measured doses. Mercy is *racham*, a compassion rooted in the Hebrew word for womb. The prodigal's father who "had compassion, and ran" (Luke 15:20) is this verse dramatized; the robe, the ring, the fatted calf are what "abundantly" looks like.

**Second, the grammar.** Man's duties appear as appeals ("let him forsake … let him return"); God's response is flat indicative: "he will have mercy … he will abundantly pardon." Once the turn is made, mercy is not probable but certain—not because of the quality of the repentance, but because of the character of the One sought. In the flow of Isaiah, this certainty is grounded in what precedes: the Servant who has already "bare the sin of many" (53:12), and the "sure mercies" freely offered (55:1–3). Repentance is the empty hand; the pardon is already prepared. Even the little word "our" matters—the returning sinner speaks as one of the people, restored not only to favor but to fellowship.

And nothing in the doubling of the demand suggests the sinner must first clean his own interior before God will receive him. Many fear their conduct can be forgiven but their inner world—who they "really" are—cannot. Verse 7 names precisely that inner world, and pardons it *abundantly*.

All of it is framed by urgency: "while he may be found … while he is near" (v. 6). Grace has a present tense; the season is not guaranteed forever. There is a quiet elegance in the chapter's close: God's word "shall not return unto me void" (v. 11)—the same verb *shuv* as the sinner's return. The word that calls the wanderer home does not come back empty; our return is carried in its return.
