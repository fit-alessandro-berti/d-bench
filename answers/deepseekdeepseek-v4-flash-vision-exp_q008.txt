The verse is not saying that loving people earns God’s love. It is saying that loving people is the visible evidence of God’s love living in you.

John’s point is simple: God is invisible. You cannot point to God and say, “I love Him” in a way that can be verified. But the person in front of you is visible. God has given you a concrete object of love. If God’s love is truly in you, it will show itself in everyday, tangible love toward the brother or sister you can see. If that love is absent, your profession of love for the invisible God is a lie.

## Why the visible neighbor is the test

In 1 John 4:12, John says:

> “No one has ever seen God; but if we love one another, God lives in us and his love is made complete in us.”

So the invisible God chooses to make His love *visible* through our love for one another. The neighbor is not a side issue. He is the arena where the love of God becomes real and testable. You can have strong feelings about God and still be self-deceived. But you cannot love the invisible God and hate the visible person He made in His image. That is like saying you love the sun but hate light.

Also, in 1 John, to love God is to obey His commands (1 John 5:3). And the command is: “Love one another” (1 John 3:23). If you disobey the command about the brother you can see, your claim to love the God you cannot see is empty.

The logic of 4:20 is not abstract. It is a diagnostic. If a person says, “I love God,” but hates his brother, the contradiction is not hidden. The person standing in front of him exposes the lie.

## What kind of self-deception is it?

It is a very common and very religious kind of self-deception: the belief that you can have a loving relationship with God while refusing to love the actual people God has put around you.

The self-deceived person may be sincere. They may pray, worship, and have deep religious feelings. They may genuinely believe they love God. But their “love for God” is actually a safe substitute for the costly, difficult, unglamorous work of loving flawed people. They are using God as an escape from their neighbor.

This self-deception usually looks like this:

- It confuses **religious feeling** with **love**. “I feel close to God” becomes “I love God,” even if the same heart is cold toward a brother.
- It confuses **orthodoxy** with **love**. “I believe the right things about God” becomes “I love God,” even if a person treats others with contempt.
- It **spiritualizes hatred**. “I don’t hate my brother; I just have nothing to do with him” is a lie, because in 1 John, love is active and commanded, not merely a nice feeling.
- It treats the invisible God as a “safe” object of devotion while the visible brother is a demanding, inconvenient, sometimes unlovely presence.

John calls this person “a liar.” The lie is not only toward others; it is self-deception. The heart can convince itself that it loves God while actually being set against His children.

## The test is not to earn salvation

The point is not to make you doubt whether you try hard enough. John’s test is diagnostic: it helps us know whether the love of God has truly taken root in us. If we love one another, God’s invisible love becomes visible. If we do not love, the claim of love for God is hollow.

The answer to this deception is not to try to love God harder. It is to come back to the source of love. We love because He first loved us (1 John 4:19). When we are filled with that love, it cannot stay bottled up; it flows out to the real, visible, imperfect people God has placed in front of us.
