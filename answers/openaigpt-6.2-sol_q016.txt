The elder brother’s deepest problem is that **he sees his obedience as a claim to reward, so his father’s mercy toward his brother feels like a wrong done to him.** Anger and envy are what we see; wounded fairness is how he explains it; pride in his obedience gives that grievance its force. His refusal to join the feast shows where it all leads: he cannot rejoice that his brother has come home.

Listen to how he describes his life with his father: “These many years do I serve thee … and yet thou never gavest me a kid.” There is real hurt in that complaint: he feels that his faithfulness has gone unnoticed. But the father doesn’t belittle his obedience or dismiss the hurt. He comes out to him and says, “Son, thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine.” The younger son’s welcome has not diminished the elder son’s place in the family.

The sharpest contrast is between **“this thy son”** and **“this thy brother.”** The elder brother wants the father to reckon with what the younger son did; the father wants the elder brother to reckon with who has come home. The feast is not a prize for bad behavior. It is the joy of having someone back who was as good as dead.

So yes, it is discomfort with mercy itself—but in a particular form: **he experiences mercy shown to another as a judgment on the value of his own faithfulness.** The father invites him to see that his faithfulness need not be worth less for his brother’s return to be worth celebrating.
