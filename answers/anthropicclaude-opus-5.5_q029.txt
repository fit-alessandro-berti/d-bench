# Galatians 6:1: The Posture of Correction

## The moral posture required

Paul describes someone who takes another's sin seriously enough to act, but who stands beside the fallen person rather than above them. Several features of the verse build this posture.

**1. "Ye which are spiritual" is a description, not a rank.**
In context (5:16–26), the "spiritual" (*pneumatikoi*) are those who "walk in the Spirit" and bear its fruit. Paul is not appointing an elite class of correctors. The phrase is almost a test: *if* you are spiritual, this is how it will show. The fruit of the Spirit listed just before includes "meekness" (5:23), the very quality now required. Spirituality is proven not by detecting another's fault but by how one handles it.

**2. "Overtaken" suggests a person caught, not a person defiant.**
The verb (*prolambanō*) implies being surprised or caught off guard, whether by the sin itself or by discovery. The "fault" (*paraptōma*) is a false step, a stumble. Paul pictures someone who has fallen, not someone brazenly entrenched (contrast 1 Corinthians 5). The response is shaped accordingly: this person needs help up, not a verdict.

**3. "Restore" is a word of repair, not punishment.**
*Katartizō* was used for mending torn nets (Mark 1:19) and, in medical usage, for setting a dislocated bone. The goal of correction is the person's return to wholeness and usefulness. It is not exposure, humiliation, or the corrector's vindication. Bone-setting is also a good image of the manner: it must be done, it may hurt, but it requires a careful and gentle hand.

**4. "In the spirit of meekness"**
Meekness (*praütēs*) is not weakness or timidity. It is strength under control: firmness without harshness, and the absence of self-assertion. The meek corrector has nothing to prove and no ego invested in the other's failure.

**5. "Considering thyself, lest thou also be tempted"**
Paul shifts from the plural ("ye") to the singular ("thyself"). The community acts together, but each individual must examine himself. "Considering" (*skopōn*) means to keep a watchful eye on, continually. The corrector is also under scrutiny.

## Why the three are kept together

Each element guards the others. Remove any one and correction degrades into something else:

| If you keep... | but lose... | you get... |
|---|---|---|
| Correction | Restoration as the goal | Punishment, exposure, or expulsion |
| Restoration | Meekness | Harsh, coercive "fixing" that wounds further |
| Meekness | Restoration | Indulgence and avoidance, leaving the fallen alone |
| Restoration and meekness | Self-suspicion | Condescension: gentle, but from above |
| Self-suspicion | Restoration | Paralysis ("who am I to say anything?") |

The triad therefore holds together **responsibility** (you must restore), **manner** (gently), and **self-knowledge** (as one equally vulnerable).

## What "lest thou also be tempted" guards against

The warning cuts two ways, and both are likely in view.

- **Temptation to the same sin.** Close involvement with another's fall can expose one's own weakness. No one is beyond the reach of what overtook the brother.
- **Temptation to pride.** Correcting others is itself a spiritual danger. The immediate context supports this reading. Just before, Paul warns against being "desirous of vain glory, provoking one another, envying one another" (5:26). Just after, he adds: "if a man think himself to be something, when he is nothing, he deceiveth himself" (6:3), and "let every man prove his own work" (6:4). The characteristic sin of the corrector is self-exaltation, the Pharisee's prayer: "I thank thee, that I am not as other men" (Luke 18:11).

Self-suspicion is therefore not a reluctant limit on correction but the very condition that makes it safe and fruitful. It echoes Jesus' teaching about removing the beam from your own eye *so that* you can see clearly to remove the speck from your brother's (Matthew 7:5). Self-examination does not cancel the duty to help; it qualifies you for it.

## The larger frame

Verse 2 follows directly: "Bear ye one another's burdens, and so fulfil the law of Christ." Restoration is one form of burden-bearing. The fallen brother's failure becomes, in a sense, the community's shared load rather than a private scandal or an occasion for someone's superiority. This fits Galatians as a whole. A community that has abandoned law-keeping as a ground of status cannot turn moral correction into a new ladder of self-righteousness. Those who live by grace correct as people who themselves stand only by grace.

**In short:** the posture Paul requires is humble responsibility. Take the fall seriously, aim at healing, act gently, and remember throughout that you are made of the same material as the one you are helping up.
