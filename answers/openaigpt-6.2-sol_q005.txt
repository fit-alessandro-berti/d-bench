Enemy-love is difficult to take seriously because anger often feels like a way of staying loyal to what was harmed. If I wish my enemy well, am I saying the wrong didn’t matter? Am I betraying the people they hurt? And if I give up retaliation, what will stop them from doing it again? Those are real moral questions, not just excuses for resentment.

But Jesus doesn’t ask his hearers to feel affection for their enemies. He names things they can do even while they are angry or afraid: bless, do good, pray. Nor does he promise that love will win the enemy over. His reason is that God gives sun and rain to the just and the unjust alike. The distinction between just and unjust still matters; it simply doesn’t determine who gets the rain.

That makes enemy-love a deeper form of moral strength, not sentimentality or a strategy for getting results. You can tell the truth about a wrong, protect yourself and others, and insist on accountability without making the wrongdoer’s suffering your goal. You can hope they change without trusting them to change. The hard thing Jesus asks is not to call evil good, but to keep doing good even when someone has given you a reason not to.
