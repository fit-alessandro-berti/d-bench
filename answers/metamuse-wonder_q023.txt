Isaiah puts "way" and "thoughts" together because repentance, in Scripture, is never only behavior and never only feelings. It is a turning of the whole person.

> Let the wicked forsake his **way**, and the unrighteous man his **thoughts**: and let him return unto the Lord...

The next two verses explain why both words are needed:

> For my **thoughts** are not your thoughts, neither are your **ways** my ways, saith the Lord. — Isaiah 55:8

Man's way and thoughts must be forsaken because they are not God's way and thoughts.

### 1. "His way" — outward conduct

*Way* is the Hebrew *derek*, a road or path. It means a chosen course of life: habits, practices, alliances, actions repeated until they become a direction.

To forsake your way is concrete. Stop doing it. Leave the road. 

In Isaiah's context that meant forsaking idolatry, injustice, self-reliant political schemes, and the whole way of life formed in exile. Repentance that never changes what we do with our hands, money, speech, and body is not yet repentance — it is only sentiment.

### 2. "His thoughts" — inward imagination

*Thoughts* is *machashavot* — not just fleeting mental impressions, but plans, designs, intentions, and inner reasonings. The same word is used for a craftsman's designs. It's what a person is plotting, justifying, and cherishing inside.

A man can temporarily pause an outward act while still loving it, planning to return to it, and building a worldview that excuses it. Isaiah says that inner world must also be abandoned:

- the imagination that rehearses sin before committing it
- the self-justifying story that makes sin seem necessary or harmless
- the ambitions and calculations formed apart from God
- even the way we imagine God himself

That last point is crucial. One of the unrighteous thoughts to forsake is the thought that God thinks like we do — reluctant to forgive, keeping score, pardoning narrowly. God says: He does *not*.

Outward change without inward change is hypocrisy and will relapse, because the way always flows from the thoughts. Inward sorrow without outward change is fantasy, because true thoughts always eventually produce a new way.

Isaiah demands both because sin creates a cycle: thoughts produce a way, and walking that way trains the thoughts to think that way again. Repentance breaks the cycle at both ends.

### 3. What this implies about mercy

Notice the disproportion.

Man's part is to *forsake* and *return* — *shuv*, turn back to the Lord he left. God's part is to "have mercy" and "abundantly pardon."

The phrase is literally "he will multiply to pardon." God's pardon is not measured to the size of our repentance. We forsake in part, haltingly, and still struggling with our thoughts — He pardons abundantly.

And He can pardon even the thoughts because mercy reaches deeper than conduct. A merely outward righteousness could perhaps hide sin from people, but not from God. The promise here is that the hidden imagination — the plans no one else saw, the inner life we are most ashamed of — is not beyond mercy when it is forsaken and brought to Him.

Verses 6 and 7 also hold urgency and assurance together: "Seek ye the Lord *while he may be found*." Don't presume on tomorrow, and don't despair today. While He is near — in His word of invitation — return is possible, because His thoughts toward returning sinners are higher, freer, and more merciful than our thoughts about Him or about ourselves.
