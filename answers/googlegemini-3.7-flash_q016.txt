While the elder brother displays all of these traits, his **deepest problem is a fundamentally transactional relationship with his father, which produces an underlying discomfort with grace and mercy.**

To understand the depth of his problem, it helps to peel back the layers from symptom to root:

---

### 1. The Surface Symptoms: Anger, Envy, and Wounded Fairness
* **The Reaction:** He is angry, refuses to join the feast, and complains that he was never given even a young goat to celebrate with his friends (vv. 28–29).
* **The Flaw:** He measures the father’s love strictly in terms of comparative compensation. To him, the celebration for the younger brother feels like an insult to his own record of service.

### 2. The Intermediate Root: Pride in Legalistic Obedience
* **The Reaction:** *"Lo, these many years do I serve thee, neither transgressed I at any time thy commandment"* (v. 29). 
* **The Flaw:** The Greek word he uses for "serve" is *douleuō*—literally, **"I have slaved for you."** He does not view himself as a beloved heir enjoying his father’s presence; he views himself as an underpaid, dutiful employee. He believes his standing is earned through flawless performance rather than received through relationship.

### 3. The Deepest Core: Blindness to Sonship and Discomfort with Mercy
The father’s reply exposes the true tragedy of the elder brother’s heart:
> *“Son, thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine. It was meet that we should make merry, and be glad: for this thy brother was dead, and is alive again...”* (vv. 31–32)

His ultimate problem consists of two deeply interconnected realities:

1. **He is spiritually alienated from the father while living in his house:**
   He never realized that everything the father owned was already his to enjoy through communion with him. He wanted the father’s *goods* (the goat/party), but had no care for the father’s *presence* or his *heart*.
2. **He cannot accept unmerited grace:**
   Because his entire identity rests on his own merit, the father’s free, lavish forgiveness of the prodigal destroys his moral framework. If the prodigal gets the fatted calf for free, then the elder brother's years of "slaving" lose their currency. Mercy offends him because grace makes his self-righteousness obsolete.

---

### Contextual Significance
In the broader context of Luke 15, Jesus directs this parable at the Pharisees and scribes who were muttering, *"This man receives sinners and eats with them"* (Luke 15:2). 

The elder brother represents the religious person who is **lost inside the house**. His deepest failure is that he shares the father's roof and rules, but does not share the father's heart. He disowns his brother (*"this thy son,"* v. 30) because he has fundamentally misunderstood what it means to be a son himself.
