This passage from the Sermon on the Mount is one of the most memorable moral metaphors in Western literature. Its enduring power lies in its striking visual imagery and its nuanced, practical approach to moral discernment.

---

### 1. What Makes the Image So Sharp?

The sharpness of the metaphor relies on several rhetorical and psychological devices:

* **Grotesque Hyperbole:** 
  The contrast is intentionally absurd. A *mote* (Greek: *karphos*) is a tiny speck of dust, chaff, or sawdust. A *beam* (Greek: *dokos*) is a massive structural timber or rafter used in building construction. The image of a person walking around with a heavy log protruding from their eye socket is comically surreal and visually arresting.
* **The Irony of the Eye:**
  The eye is both the most delicate organ of the human body and the primary metaphor for perception, judgment, and discernment. The sharpness of the irony lies in a person who is practically blind attempting to perform delicate, precision "ocular surgery" on someone else. 
* **Psychological Precision (Projection):**
  The image captures a profound truth of human psychology: people are often hyper-vigilant about small faults in others precisely to distract from—or compensate for—massive, unexamined moral failures in themselves. The external focus serves as a shield against uncomfortable self-examination.
* **Direct, Indicting Tone:**
  The sudden shift in verse 5 to direct address—*“Thou hypocrite”*—breaks the hypothetical frame and lands as a direct challenge to the listener's conscience.

---

### 2. How the Passage Criticizes Hypocrisy Without Forbidding Correction

A common misconception is that this passage teaches a blanket prohibition against ever correcting someone else (often summarized casually as "don't judge"). However, the text carefully distinguishes between **hypocritical judgment** and **restorative moral correction**.

#### A. The "First / Then" Sequence (V. 5)
Jesus does not say, *"Leave your brother’s eye alone forever."* Instead, he establishes a mandatory sequence:
> *“**First** cast out the beam out of thine own eye; and **then** shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote out of thy brother’s eye.”*

The goal is not to abandon the brother to his fault, but to **qualify the corrector** to be of actual help. Moral correction is not forbidden; it is *conditioned upon self-awareness and repentance*.

#### B. The Speck Still Needs to Be Removed
The passage acknowledges that the speck in the brother’s eye is real. A mote in the eye causes pain, impairs vision, and needs to be taken out. By stating that the corrected person will eventually *"see clearly to cast out the mote,"* the passage affirms that helping another person overcome a fault is a good and necessary act of love.

#### C. Transforming Judgment into Empathy
By demanding that self-correction precede fraternal correction, the passage fundamentally changes the *spirit* in which correction is given:
* **The Hypocrite’s Approach:** Motivated by superiority, pride, and blindness to their own flaws; treats the brother's speck as an opportunity for condemnation.
* **The Cleared-Eyed Approach:** Motivated by empathy and humility; having experienced the painful and difficult process of removing their own "beam," they approach the brother’s "speck" with the gentleness and precision of a healed patient helping another heal.

### Summary
The passage does not call for moral relativism or indifference to wrong. Rather, it demands that **self-examination must precede external criticism**, ensuring that moral correction is done with clear vision, humility, and the genuine intent to heal rather than condemn.
