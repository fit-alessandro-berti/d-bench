This portrait is mostly negative because Paul is not describing love as a feeling to be added to life, but as a self-assertion to be removed from it.

In Corinth, the problem was not a lack of religious intensity. It was competition — over spiritual gifts, status, knowledge, and who was most impressive. So Paul defines *agape* (KJV: "charity") largely by what it refuses to do when the ego feels threatened.

The negatives fall into groups:

**Envy, boasting, being "puffed up"** — these are love corrupted by comparison. Envy needs someone else to lose; boasting needs someone else to admire; pride needs to be inflated. Love, Paul says, is secure enough not to keep score of rank.

**Behaving "unseemly," seeking "her own"** — these are love corrupted by use. Other people become an audience, a tool, or an obstacle. Love does not insist that every relationship serve its own interests or protect its own dignity at others' expense.

**Being "easily provoked," "think[ing] no evil"** — these are love corrupted by defensiveness. Irritability is often fragility disguised as strength: the self is so easily wounded that it must react at once. "Thinketh no evil" is literally *does not keep an account of evil* — it does not maintain a ledger of slights, or rehearse suspicions until they become certainties.

In other words, pride wants to rise, envy wants to pull down, irritability wants to retaliate, and suspicion wants to control. Love refuses all four.

### The strength being proposed

The passage is framed by positives. It begins:

> "suffereth long, and is kind"

and ends with four sweeping verbs:

> "Beareth all things, believeth all things, hopeth all things, endureth all things."

That is Paul's vision of strength: not the power to dominate, impress, win an argument, or never be hurt, but the power to *remain* — patient under provocation, kind under pressure, truthful without rejoicing in another's failure, hopeful without denying evil.

"Beareth" suggests bearing a weight, like a roof bearing a load. "Endureth" is military language: holding one's ground rather than fleeing or striking back. "Believeth" and "hopeth" are not naivety; they are a refusal to make a final, cynical judgment about a person. Love leaves room for truth, repentance, and a future it does not control.

So the strength here is cruciform, not heroic in the worldly sense. The weak self must boast, envy, react, and keep accounts because it feels scarce and unsafe. The strong self — as Paul portrays it — is unthreatened enough to be gentle, slow enough not to retaliate, and durable enough to bear all things without becoming bitter.

Love is powerful, in this passage, not because it never suffers, but because suffering does not turn it into pride, envy, or suspicion.
