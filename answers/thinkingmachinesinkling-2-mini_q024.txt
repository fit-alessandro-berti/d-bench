External compliance is obedience to rules without the inner orientation that makes obedience want-to-be or freely chosen. A person can keep commands, attend rites, preserve outward order, and still experience the law as a burden or as a bargaining tool rather than as expression of covenant loyalty. Ezekiel’s contrast is not merely “good behavior” versus “bad behavior,” but exterior conformity versus internal disposition.

A “new heart” is a new interior substance: compassion, fear of God, willing desire to walk in His statutes. It makes obedience possible not by mechanical force but because the center of desire and decision has changed.

The stony-heart image makes hardness feel like a material defect rather than just a failing attitude. Stone is thick, inert, rigid, and unresponsive; it does not love, sorrow, or obey. By describing “hardness” as a material condition, the text portrays resistance as intrinsic to the inner person. It is not enough to fine-tune outward conduct; what must be removed is the inward unresponsiveness that keeps obedience from flowing.

That is why the replacement is portrayed almost mechanically: God “takes away” and “gives.” The metaphor emphasizes that this transformation is a divine gift, not merely a skill or self-improvement. The old material cannot be reformed enough to obey life-giving commands; it must be exchanged for living flesh. In that sense, new heart is a renewal of the center of life, not a polished outer morality.

There is a further implication: external compliance can look like obedience while remaining passive, fear-driven, or inconsistent. The new heart makes obedience possible as a sustained orientation, so “walk in my statutes” and “keep my judgments” become more than a list of rules to be satisfied; they become expressions of an inwardly renewed life. This is similar to the broader Second Testament teaching that a heart made new can produce genuine, “from within” obedience.

So the contrast is: outward rule-following versus inward covenant renewal. Hardness is described as a material condition because that image makes the inner defect unmistakable: dead, impenetrable, unmoved—something that must be removed and replaced, not merely decorated or disciplined.
