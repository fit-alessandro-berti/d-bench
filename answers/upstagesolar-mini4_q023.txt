In Isaiah 55:6–7, repentance is presented as a twofold turning: away from **“his way”** and **“his thoughts.”** These two phrases are not redundant; they describe two interlocking dimensions of human rebellion and restoration.

### 1. Why repentance involves both “way” and “thoughts”

**“Way” (Hebrew: *derek*)** refers to the concrete path of conduct—the habitual actions, choices, and behaviors that shape a person’s life. In biblical thought, “way” often carries the sense of a pattern of living, sometimes described as a “hard way” (Deut. 21:21) or a “straight way” (Isa. 30:21). Repentance here means turning off the road of sinful action and stepping onto the path of obedience.

**“Thoughts” (Hebrew: *ma’aseh libe* or *machashavot lev*)** refers to the inner landscape of intention, imagination, desire, and reasoning. In the Old Testament, the “heart” or “mind” is the seat of direction, and whatever a person imagines and delights in eventually flows into action. So “thoughts” represents the internal root that produces the external “way.” A person may stop doing a sin outwardly, but if the inner longing, rationalization, or fantasy remains unchanged, the old pattern will likely re-emerge.

By pairing them, the text insists that genuine repentance is **whole-person conversion**: a change of behavior *and* a change of inner orientation. It is not merely behavioral modification; it is a reorientation of the mind and will. The prophet Isaiah frequently uses this “way/thoughts” pairing (e.g., Isa. 57:17; 65:2) to stress that God deals with both what people *do* and what they *love, imagine, and plan*.

### 2. What this implies about outward conduct

Outward conduct is the visible fruit of repentance. If a person’s “way” is changed, their daily life, relationships, and moral choices are transformed. This does not mean that behavior is independent of the inner life; rather, behavior is the expression of what the heart has chosen. In Isaiah’s context, Israel’s idolatry and injustice were reflected in their social and religious practices. Therefore, changing the “way” means leaving behind harmful patterns of living that injure others and dishonor God. This implies that true repentance is not private or purely internal—it becomes measurable in how a person actually lives. But it also implies that behavior change without inner transformation is fragile, because the underlying desires remain.

### 3. What this implies about inward imagination

The emphasis on “thoughts” shows that what a person **imagines, desires, and accepts as adequate** lies at the root of sin. Jesus later echoes this in the Sermon on the Mount, where lustful thoughts are treated as part of the same moral reality as adultery (Matt. 5:28), and Paul speaks of the need to “take every thought captive” (2 Cor. 10:5). In this view, sin is not only a matter of actions but also of the heart’s longing, the mind’s assumptions, and the imagination’s projection of what is good, beautiful, and meaningful. Repentance therefore involves a deep rethinking: abandoning false idols, unexamined desires, and sinful rationalizations. It means letting God’s Word rewrite the inner script so that imagination, desire, and decision are aligned with His character.

### 4. What this implies about the possibility of mercy

The closing promise is strikingly hopeful: “let him return unto the Lord, and he will have mercy upon him; and to our God, for he will abundantly pardon.” This verse deliberately emphasizes **God’s generous response** to genuine repentance. The grammar is personal and promising: the wicked, the unrighteous, the one who has left both the wrong path and the wrong inner desires, is invited to return. The mercy is not based on human perfection but on God’s willingness to forgive and restore.

This implies that:
- **Mercy is available to all**, including the most morally broken, because the invitation is to “forsake” and “return,” not to first achieve moral purity.
- **Forgiveness is abundant**, not scanty or conditional in a way that requires flawless restoration.
- **Outward and inward change both matter**, but they do not make God’s mercy conditional on human effort. Rather, they are the natural response that opens the door to grace. God’s mercy meets the returning person, and it is the turning of the will and heart that allows that mercy to take root.
- The very fact that the text says “our God” (perhaps including the returning outcast, not just the established community) suggests that repentance is not a closed-door ritual but an open invitation into covenant fellowship.

### Summary

In Isaiah 55:6–7, the “way” and “thoughts” are two dimensions of the same human problem: we sin in what we do and in what we desire, imagine, and justify. Repentance is therefore total—outer conduct must realign with God’s will, and inner imagination must be transformed. Yet for all that is required of us, the passage is equally clear about mercy: the God who calls us to return is the God who “abundantly pardons.” Repentance does not earn mercy; it receives it, and it prepares the heart to walk in mercy.
