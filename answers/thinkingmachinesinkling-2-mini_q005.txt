Enemy-love is difficult because it violates several automatic systems at once: self-protection, retaliation, group loyalty, and the need to make sense of harm. We naturally categorize enemies as “unsafe,” “bad,” or “illegitimate,” and love toward them feels like humiliation or naiveté. So in its basic sense, it is not sentimental: it does not require emotional warmth toward someone who has injured you. It is also not simply strategic: it is not a clever way to manipulate others, win influence, or get undeserved favor.

At a deeper level, it is a form of moral strength—or religious discipline—that reorders what love means. Jesus is not saying “pretend to like them” or “tolerate abuse.” He is arguing that genuine life comes from aligning oneself with God’s mercy rather than with a worldview built on fairness-as-balance, reputation, and deterrence.

The difficulty lies in the cost. Love of enemies asks a person to:

- hold an enemy as both human and guilty of harm;
- respond before revenge hardens into habit;
- maintain boundaries without turning rage into a permanent identity;
- act differently from the powerful impulse to protect and honor self;
- remember that the own-self is not the center of meaning.

So it can have a strategic effect—at the level of identity, community, and politics—but not as calculation. It strategically weakens cycles of revenge, but mainly by remaking the actor from the inside. Strategically it also inverts the power dynamic: a person no longer defines strength as domination, but as restraint, forgiveness where possible, and hope when return feels unfair.

The deepest point is this: enemy-love is hard because it is both mercy and risk. It asks the one who suffers to become, in some way, like the Father who “makes his sun to rise on the evil and on the good.” The hard part is not intellectual agreement—many can recite the verse—but the embodied discipline of continuing to act as if the enemy remains human, exposed, and answerable to a higher moral and religious order.

It is also not blanket license: one can love an enemy while refusing to surrender justice, safety, or truth. Enemy-love can coexist with refusal, lawful resistance, honest confrontation, and even refusal to be exploited. But it changes the moral posture from “how do I win?” to “how do I remain human under threat?”

So: sentimental? No. Strategic? Only derivatively. Primarily, it is a deeper form of strength—costly moral courage that becomes a way of life rather than a tactic.
