**Short answer:** the last one — but only if we say why. The anger, envy, fairness-claim, and pride are all really there in the text; they are layers, not competitors. What lies under them is that he cannot live from gift, and therefore cannot share a joy he did not earn.

**Sorting the layers**

Anger is the symptom that shows (v. 28, *he was angry and would not go in*). Envy is the comparison that feeds it (a kid versus the fatted calf). Wounded fairness is the argument he makes out loud. Pride in obedience is the ground he stands on while making it (*these many years… neither transgressed I at any time*). Each explains the one above it, and none explains the last.

**The tell is in his vocabulary**

He does not say *I have been your son* but *these many years do I serve thee* — the Greek is δουλεύω, *I have been slaving for you*. He does not say *I loved you* but *I never broke your commandment*: the relationship has been experienced as command, and command kept. He does not say *my brother* but *this thy son* — twice disowning the kinship, and the father quietly hands the word back: *this thy brother*. He wants a goat *that I might make merry with my friends* — a separate table, away from the father. And he calls the squandered inheritance *thy living*, though by v. 12 it had already been divided and the wasted third was, in his accounting, his own.

Put those together and you get a man who has been living in his father's house as a hireling, keeping a ledger, and who has quietly assumed the father was withholding from him. He never asked for the kid. He never asked. He assumed the answer was no. That is the same portrait as the servant with one talent: *I knew thee that thou art an hard man.*

**Which is why the father's reply is a diagnosis, not a defense**

Notice what the father does *not* do: he does not dispute the facts, or explain that the calf was a special case, or promise the elder son something equivalent later. He says, *all that I have is thine.* Concede the ledger entirely — nothing was ever withheld; you were never a servant; the whole estate has been yours every day you were calling yourself a slave. And then, *thou art ever with me* — naming the thing the son has had all along and never enjoyed.

So the wound is not actually inequity. It is that the father's love turns out not to be apportioned by merit at all. And if merit does not determine love, then those blameless years purchased nothing distinctive. The self he built — *the deserving one* — has no purchase on reality. Mercy toward his brother is not merely unfair to him; it dissolves the only identity he has. That is why it registers as theft rather than as joy. In a ledger world, love is a finite quantity, and every gram given to the undeserving is a gram taken from him.

**So: discomfort with mercy itself — yes, but locate it precisely**

It is not that he finds forgiveness theologically objectionable. It is that he cannot *eat at a table constituted by forgiveness*. And that is exactly where the parable was aimed: Luke 15:1–2, the Pharisees and scribes murmuring, *This man receiveth sinners, and eateth with them.* The elder brother stands outside a feast, refusing to go in, while his father comes out and begs him. The father goes out to both sons — one in the far country, one in the dooryard. Both are lost; only one of them knows it. The refusal to enter is the whole complaint enacted: he would rather have a small party he earned than a great party he was given.

**Two honest caveats**

First, his fairness objection is not refuted on its own terms, and the parable does not pretend it is. Grace really is asymmetric. The father answers a different question than the one asked — not *is this fair?* but *what has actually happened?* — and answers it with resurrection language: *was dead, and is alive.* Fairness is a real good, but it is not the category that governs a return from the dead. The text says *it was meet*, ἔδει: this joy was necessary. He is being told that his accounting, though accurate, has missed the event.

Second, we never learn whether he goes in. The story stops mid-sentence, with the father still pleading. That is not an oversight. The parable's last word is an unanswered invitation, because the ones telling the story about themselves are the audience, and they have to write the ending.

Which suggests the deepest problem may be best named not as a flaw but as a refusal: he will not be a son, because being a son means receiving, and receiving means he was never the one who earned his place — and he cannot bear that. The father's answer to that is not an argument. It is *Son.*
