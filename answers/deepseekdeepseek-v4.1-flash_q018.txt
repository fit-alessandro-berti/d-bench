In Ephesians 4:31–32, bitterness and forgiveness are set against each other because they are two rival ways of responding to being wronged. Bitterness nurses the wound and keeps the debt alive; forgiveness releases the debt and refuses to let the wound become malice.

The Greek word for “bitterness” is *pikria*. It can describe literal bitterness of taste, but in the New Testament it is used metaphorically for a sharp, resentful, embittered spirit. It is not merely a fleeting emotion like “I feel hurt.” It is what hurt becomes when it is cultivated. Hebrews 12:15 calls it a “root of bitterness” that “springs up” and “defiles many.” That means the text treats bitterness as something that begins inwardly, becomes a settled habit or posture, and then spreads outwardly through speech and relationships.

Notice the list in verse 31: “bitterness, wrath, anger, clamour, evil speaking, malice.” It moves from inner resentment to explosive anger, to shouting, to slander, to malicious intent. Bitterness is not just one item on the list; it is often the root that feeds the others. So yes, it can be a feeling, but the text is primarily treating it as a chosen, habituated posture of the heart—and one that is contagious in a community.

Forgiveness is placed directly opposite it because forgiveness is the Spirit-empowered replacement for bitterness. The Greek word behind “forgiving” in verse 32 is *charizomai*, a grace-word: to grant forgiveness graciously, to cancel a debt. Bitterness says, “You owe me. I will keep score.” Forgiveness says, “I release you. I will not exact payment.” They cannot both rule the heart at the same time.

Ephesians also grounds forgiveness in the gospel: “even as God for Christ’s sake hath forgiven you.” We do not forgive in order to be forgiven; we forgive because we have been forgiven. Bitterness contradicts that gospel logic. It is the unforgiving servant refusing to release a small debt after being forgiven a massive one (Matt. 18:21–35).

So the text does not treat bitterness as merely a private emotion to manage. It treats it as a moral and relational condition to put away. It begins as feeling, hardens into habit, becomes a chosen posture, and spreads like a root or infection. Forgiveness is its opposite: not a denial that wrong happened, not a feeling that instantly replaces pain, but a grace-given posture that releases vengeance to God and refuses to let resentment define the heart.
