This passage is best described as **realistic—even painfully honest—about self-deception**, not simply pessimistic in a cynical sense.

### 1. The “heart” in Scripture is deeper than feelings
In Hebrew thought, the heart (לֵב, *lev*) is not just the emotional center; it is the inner control room of a person—thoughts, will, desires, conscience, and identity. So when Jeremiah says the heart is “deceitful above all things” (Jer. 17:9), he means that **our deepest inner self is capable of deceiving even us**.

The phrase “desperately wicked” (KJV) is more literally rendered by many modern translations as “desperately sick” or “beyond cure” (ESV: “desperately sick”; NIV: “beyond cure”). So the image is of a corrupted or diseased inner life that warps the way we see ourselves, our motives, and our choices.

### 2. Is it pessimistic or realistic?
It is pessimistic **only if you think the story should end with the human heart.** But the passage doesn’t end there. Verse 10 says:

> “I the Lord search the heart, I try the reins, even to give every man according to his ways…”

God alone knows what the heart is really doing. So Jeremiah is not saying, “People are hopeless.” He is saying, **“People are unreliable witnesses to their own hearts—and God is the only trustworthy judge.”**

This is realism, not despair. It matches what we observe: people can genuinely believe they are acting for noble reasons while being driven by pride, fear, or selfishness. Even when we feel sincere, the sincerity itself can be part of the self-deception.

### 3. How should we talk about our motives?
If the heart is an unreliable witness, the right response is not to stop reflecting, but to reflect with humility and with the right source of truth.

- **Don’t claim certainty about your own motives.** Say things like, “I think I did this out of love—but God knows my heart better than I do.” This is honesty, not false modesty.
- **Use Scripture as a mirror.** Psalm 119:105 says God’s word is a lamp for the feet. It exposes what we would not see on our own.
- **Invite critique and accountability.** Other people can sometimes see our blind spots more clearly than we can. “Plans fail for lack of counsel, but with many advisers they succeed” (Prov. 15:22).
- **Look at fruit, not just feelings.** Jesus said, “By their fruit you will recognize them” (Matt. 7:16). Motives are revealed by patterns of behavior over time more than by our own emotional honesty in the moment.
- **Let God do the searching.** Instead of obsessively interrogating yourself, pray like David: “Search me, God, and know my heart; test me and know my anxious thoughts” (Ps. 139:23). This is the biblical alternative to trusting your self-diagnosis.

So the practical answer is: **Talk about motives with confession, not certainty.** The heart is too deceitful for us to become the final judge of ourselves. That role belongs to God—and that is good news, because He is both true and merciful.
