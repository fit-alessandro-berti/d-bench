Luke 6:37 does not say that moral distinctions disappear. It warns against assuming the posture of a judge whose chief aim is to pronounce people guilty.

**Discernment** asks, “What is true? What is harmful? What would love and justice require here?” It can recognize wrongdoing, set boundaries, protect vulnerable people, and call someone to account. Without discernment, forgiveness could be confused with pretending nothing happened.

The **appetite to condemn** is different. It takes satisfaction in fixing a person’s identity as “the bad one.” It may rush past context, ignore one’s own faults, or seek punishment and superiority rather than truth and restoration.

The surrounding passage makes the distinction vivid. Jesus asks how someone can attend to the speck in another’s eye while ignoring the plank in their own (Luke 6:41–42). The point is not to leave the speck there, but to deal honestly with oneself *before* trying to help another see.

For example, saying, “What you did was dishonest, and I cannot entrust you with this responsibility until trust is rebuilt,” is a moral judgment. Saying, “You are worthless, and I want everyone to know it,” is condemnation.

Forgiveness, too, need not mean denying the harm, restoring trust immediately, or abandoning appropriate consequences. It means refusing to make retaliation or the offender’s permanent disgrace one’s governing aim. Jesus’ call is not to stop judging actions, but to exercise judgment with humility and mercy.
