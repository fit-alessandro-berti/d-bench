Isaiah 55:6–7 calls for a whole-person turning to God: the “way” and the “thoughts” together represent the outer life and the inner life.

**Why both “way” and “thoughts”?**

- **“Way”** is the course of life—conduct, habits, actions, the path a person walks. It is the visible, practiced direction of life.
- **“Thoughts”** is the inner life—plans, intentions, reasonings, imaginations, the counsel of the heart. It is the hidden source from which the “way” comes.

So the verse is not saying, “Change your behavior only,” nor “Have better thoughts only.” It says repentance must reach both the fruit and the root. If a person forsakes wrong actions but keeps cherishing wrong thoughts, the repentance is superficial. If a person has vague inner remorse but keeps walking in the same sinful path, the repentance is incomplete. God calls the wicked to abandon both his path and his mental agenda, and to “return unto the Lord.”

This also fits the immediate context: Isaiah 55:8–9 says, “My thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways.” The repentant person exchanges his own ways and thoughts for God’s.

**What this implies about outward conduct**

Repentance is concrete and ethical. It means turning from actual sins—dishonesty, injustice, impurity, cruelty, idolatry, neglect of God—and turning toward the Lord. It is not merely feeling sorry or having a religious experience. It produces a changed walk. Outward reform matters because God cares about what we do.

**What this implies about inward imagination**

The inner life is not a neutral private zone. The imagination, fantasies, ambitions, resentments, lusts, prideful plans, and mental narratives are all part of the “thoughts” that must be forsaken. Sin often begins in the mind before it appears in the life. So repentance includes renouncing the inner pattern of rebellion and bringing the mind under God’s lordship. This does not mean every involuntary temptation is already the sin of the heart, but it does mean we must not entertain, cherish, and cultivate what God forbids.

**What this implies about the possibility of mercy**

The command to forsake and return is followed by a promise: “and he will have mercy upon him; and to our God, for he will abundantly pardon.” Mercy is not earned by perfect repentance. It is found by returning to the Lord. The ground of pardon is God’s own abundant mercy, not the sinner’s completeness.

That means:

- No one should despair over outward sins: God can pardon.
- No one should despair over inward thoughts: God can pardon even the deep corruption of the mind.
- No one should separate outward change from inward renewal.
- No one should presume upon mercy forever: the call is “while he may be found,” “while he is near.”

So Isaiah 55:6–7 presents repentance as a Godward conversion of both path and mind, met by a God who is “abundantly” ready to pardon. It is not behavior modification alone, nor inner sentiment alone, but a return of the whole person to the Lord.
