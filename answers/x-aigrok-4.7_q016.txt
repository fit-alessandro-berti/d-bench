The elder brother’s anger is real, but it is a symptom. What the speech discloses is a son who has lived beside the father without sharing the father’s heart.

He frames the relationship as long service and unbroken compliance, then treats the missing kid as evidence that obedience was not properly paid. That is pride in his record, and it produces the wounded sense of fairness: if standing is earned, then killing the calf for someone who “devoured thy living with harlots” is an injustice. Envy follows from the same ledger. He will not even say “my brother”; it is “this thy son.”

The father’s answer names what that ledger cannot bear. “Son, thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine” means the elder son was not waiting on a wage. He already possessed the inheritance as a son, not as a hired hand. The feast is not a reward seized by the prodigal; it is joy that the dead is alive and the lost is found. The elder son’s deepest problem is that this mercy offends him. He wants a father who settles accounts, not a father who runs to the undeserving and calls the household to rejoice. Until he can enter that joy, he remains outside the house even though he never left the property.
