This passage is **realistic about self-deception**—and it sounds pessimistic only because most of us prefer to believe we are more transparent to ourselves than we actually are.

The Hebrew word for “deceitful” is *ʿāqōb*, linked to Jacob—the heel-grabber, the supplanter. The heart, Jeremiah says, is a Jacob-like deceiver. It twists, manipulates, and hides. And the word translated “desperately wicked” or “desperately sick” is *ʾānûš*, meaning chronically ill, incurable. So the problem is not just that we sometimes make mistakes about our motives. The problem is that **our inner self is not a reliable witness in its own case**.

The question “who can know it?” is a rhetorical question. The implied answer: no human being can, not even yourself.

But verse 10 gives the key:

> “I the Lord search the heart, I try the reins.”

“Reins” means the kidneys—the hidden, inner depths of a person. God sees what we cannot see. He tests, weighs, and knows. So the passage is not a dark dead end. It turns us away from trusting our own hearts and toward the only One who truly knows us.

That is how we should talk about motives:

### 1. Don’t say “I know my own heart” as if that settles it.

Instead, pray like David:

> “Search me, O God, and know my heart; try me, and know my thoughts.”  
> — Psalm 139:23

The heart is not the court of appeal. God is.

### 2. Speak about your own motives with humility, not certainty.

Instead of “My motives were pure,” say something like:

> “I intended to help, but I know my motives can be mixed. I may have wanted approval too. God knows; I’m asking Him to search me.”

That is not false modesty; it is biblical honesty.

### 3. Be very slow to judge someone else’s motives.

You cannot see another person’s heart any better than you can see your own. So don’t say, “You did that because you don’t care.” Say:

> “What I saw hurt me. I don’t know what was in your heart, but I want to understand.”

We can assess actions, patterns, and fruit—Jesus said we know trees by their fruit—but the hidden spring of motives is God’s department.

### 4. Let God’s Word be the witness, not your feelings.

The heart is a false witness. Scripture is a true one. When you are unsure why you did something, measure yourself against the Bible’s teaching on love, humility, obedience, and faith—and bring it to God in prayer.

### 5. Remember the gospel.

If the heart is “desperately sick,” the answer is not simply more introspection. The answer is a **new heart**. God promised:

> “I will give you a new heart, and a new spirit I will put within you.”  
> — Ezekiel 36:26

So we don’t need to perfectly understand our motives before God can accept us. Christ covers what we cannot see. We can be honest about our mixed motives, ask God to purify them, and trust that because God knows everything, we are known far more deeply than we know ourselves.

In short: **This passage is realistic, and that realism is meant to drive us to God, not despair.** Talk about motives with humility, under the searchlight of God’s Word, and with confidence in the One who searches the heart.
