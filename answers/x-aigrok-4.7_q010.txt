The power praised here is not the power to dominate, but the power to descend without losing itself. Christ is pictured as already “in the form of God,” equal with God, and therefore not in need of status. What is honored is the refusal to treat that equality as something to be seized, displayed, or defended. Greatness shows itself by emptying, serving, and going all the way to a shameful death. The hymn calls that mind—not conquest—the pattern to be shared.

Open domination is unsettling, but it is legible. The strong crush the weak; everyone knows the currency. You can resist it, bargain with it, or submit and still keep your map of the world. Humility joined to real greatness scrambles that map. If the one who needs nothing still becomes a servant, then rank, reputation, and force are no longer the proof of worth. The gesture judges the observer: clinging to status starts to look like fear, and ordinary ambition is exposed as small. That is harder to answer than a tyrant, because the usual weapons—shame, rivalry, superior force—do not land on someone who has already given them up.

There is a further unease. Self-lowering can itself become a kind of authority. A power that will not compete on your terms, and that turns even death into obedience rather than defeat, cannot be beaten inside the game of domination. It claims a different order of greatness, and in doing so it quietly dethrones the old one. That can feel like liberation, or like a moral claim you did not agree to, or like both at once. The passage praises the first reading: power secure enough to serve. The unsettling part is that such power does not merely oppose domination. It makes domination look unnecessary.
