1 John 4:20 — Why love of the visible brother tests love of the invisible God

1. The shape of the argument

The verse has two parts. The first is a verdict: the person who claims to love God while hating his brother "is a liar." The second is the reason (gar, "for"): one who does not love the brother he has seen cannot love the God he has not seen. John is not making a sentimental point ("be nice to people because God likes that"); he is making a claim about how love works and how it can be known. The visible case is the easier, nearer, more concrete case. If love fails there, the claim that it succeeds in the harder, remoter, less checkable case is not credible.

The logic is a fortiori (from the lesser to the greater). Loving a brother is, in one sense, the lower rung: he is present, has a face, makes concrete demands, can be fed or forgiven or visited. Loving God is the higher rung: he is unseen, and "love" for him cannot be handed to him directly. If a man cannot climb the first step, he has not climbed the second. To say "I have skipped the low step and stand on the high one" is not a paradox; it is a lie.

2. Why the seen/unseen contrast matters

John presses the contrast between "seen" and "not seen" for a specific reason. The invisibility of God is exactly what makes a claim to love him unfalsifiable if it stands alone. No one can watch you love God. There is no observable transaction. You can feel warmly at prayer, use the right words, be moved by worship, and all of this is compatible with loving God — but it is equally compatible with loving your own religious feelings, your own image of yourself as devout, or an idea of God that you have made in your own likeness. Because God cannot be seen, "love of God" is the perfect place for counterfeit love to hide, since nothing external ever contradicts it.

The brother is different. He is seen. He is there, with his irritating habits, his needs, his opinions, his claim on your time and goods. Love toward him has to become an act or it is nothing. It costs something. It can be observed, and it can be observed to be absent. So the brother functions as the point at which an inward, invisible claim becomes visible and testable. John is saying: the only place where your love of God leaves a footprint you can actually inspect is in your dealings with the people in front of you. If the footprint is not there, the claim is empty.

This fits John's whole epistle. He repeatedly insists that knowing and loving God must become manifest: "hereby we know that we know him, if we keep his commandments" (2:3); "whoso hath this world's good, and seeth his brother have need, and shutteth up his bowels of compassion from him, how dwelleth the love of God in him?" (3:17); "let us not love in word, neither in tongue; but in deed and in truth" (3:18). The seen brother is where "word" becomes "deed."

3. Why the two loves are one love, not two

There is a deeper point than mere evidence. John does not think of love for God and love for neighbor as two separate loves, one of which happens to be good evidence for the other. He thinks of them as a single love with a single source. A few verses earlier: "love is of God; and every one that loveth is born of God, and knoweth God" (4:7); "God is love" (4:8); "we love him, because he first loved us" (4:19). Love, in John's sense, is not a natural human affection that one may choose to direct at God or at neighbor. It is God's own life communicated to the believer. And that life, when it is present, goes out to whatever God loves. God loves the brother. Therefore, if the love of God is in me, it will reach the brother. If it does not reach the brother, then whatever is in me is not the love of God.

So hating one's brother is not merely a failure in a parallel department that "casts doubt" on one's religion. It is direct evidence that the specific thing one claims to have — God's love dwelling in oneself — is absent, because that love by its nature cannot coexist with hatred of those God loves. It is like claiming to have a fire in the room while the room is cold. The cold is not a separate problem from the fire; it is the proof that there is no fire.

There is also the fact that the brother bears God's image and, in the Christian community John addresses, is a child of the same Father (5:1–2). To love God is to love what belongs to him. To hate his child while professing to love him is like professing devotion to a father while despising his son to his face — the profession is refuted by the act, because the father's love for the son is part of who the father is.

4. What kind of self-deception is exposed

John calls the man a liar, but the lie in view is mainly a lie one tells oneself and believes. Several layers of self-deception can be distinguished.

(a) Mistaking religious feeling for love. The most basic deception is to take the warm sensations, elevated thoughts, or devotional intensity that arise in worship and to label them "love of God." Because God is unseen, these feelings never meet resistance, never have to bend to another will, never have to give anything up. They are frictionless. Love that is frictionless is easy to confuse with love that is real. The brother introduces friction. When the "lover of God" meets a person who is difficult, he discovers what his love is actually made of. If it evaporates, it was feeling, not love.

(b) Loving an idol instead of God. A man who loves "God" but hates his brother is, in practice, loving a God who does not command love of the brother — that is, a God of his own construction. The real God, on John's account, is love and has commanded that "he who loveth God love his brother also" (4:21). To love a God who permits your hatred is to love a projection. The invisibility of God makes this substitution nearly undetectable from the inside, because the false God and the true one look identical in the privacy of one's own mind. The brother is the reality check: the true God has attached himself to the neighbor, and the false God has not. Which God you love is revealed by how you treat the neighbor.

(c) Using the higher duty to escape the lower. There is a specifically religious form of evasion in which devotion to God becomes the excuse for neglecting or despising people. The man who "loves God" can tell himself that his quarrel with his brother is zeal for truth, that his contempt is discernment, that his coldness is holy separation. The greater the invisible object of devotion, the more it can be used to license failures toward the visible ones, because who can argue with God? John cuts this off: you do not get to appeal to your love for the unseen as a cover for your failure toward the seen. The order runs the other way. The seen is the test of the unseen, not the unseen the excuse for the seen.

(d) Preferring the abstract because it is cheap. Loving humanity in general, or God in the abstract, costs nothing; loving this particular person costs everything, because he is concrete and makes concrete claims. The self-deceived man loves the general and the invisible precisely because they ask nothing of him, and then credits himself with a great love. John's argument exposes the inversion: the easier love is the one he cannot do, so the harder love he claims is a fiction.

(e) Divorcing inner and outer. Underneath all these lies is the assumption that one's "real self" is an inner life of intentions and sentiments, and that outward conduct is a separate, lesser matter. John rejects this. In his theology, what is inside is what comes out. "Whosoever hateth his brother is a murderer" (3:15). A love that stays inside and never becomes deed is not a hidden treasure; it is a non-existent thing. The self-deceived man believes he is one thing inwardly while being another outwardly, and John insists that the outward is the truth about the inward.

5. Why "liar" rather than "mistaken"

John's language is harsh because the situation is not innocent confusion. A person who hates his brother knows he hates his brother; hatred is not subtle to the one who feels it. What he refuses to know is what that hatred means about his relation to God. The lie consists in holding the two facts — "I hate this man" and "I love God" — side by side and refusing to let the first correct the second. That refusal is chosen, even if it is habitual and half-conscious. It is the will not to see. This is why John says "liar" and not "error": the truth is available, right there in the visible brother, and the man is looking away from it.

6. The positive teaching implied

The verse is not only a negative test. It also tells the believer where to look for God. If God cannot be seen, and if his love becomes visible only in love among his people, then the brother is the place where the invisible God becomes tangible. John says as much in 4:12: "No man hath seen God at any time. If we love one another, God dwelleth in us, and his love is perfected in us." The seen brother is not a rival to the unseen God, nor a detour on the way to him; he is the appointed place of meeting. To love the neighbor is not a substitute for loving God, and not merely evidence of it — it is the concrete form that loving God takes in this world.

Summary

The verse makes love of the visible brother a test of love of the invisible God because (1) love of the unseen is unfalsifiable on its own and therefore the ideal hiding place for counterfeit love, while love of the seen must become costly, observable action; (2) the two loves are in reality one love, God's own love dwelling in the believer, which by its nature goes out to those God loves, so its absence toward the brother proves its absence altogether; and (3) the brother, as God's image and child, is the point at which God has made himself concretely present. The self-deception exposed is the confusion of religious feeling with love, the substitution of a self-made God who permits one's hatreds for the real God who forbids them, the use of devotion to the unseen as an excuse for failure toward the seen, the preference for a cheap abstract love over a costly particular one, and, underlying all of these, the belief that one's true self is an inner sentiment untouched by one's outward conduct. John's word for all of this is "liar," because the truth is standing in front of the man in the form of his brother, and he is choosing not to look.
