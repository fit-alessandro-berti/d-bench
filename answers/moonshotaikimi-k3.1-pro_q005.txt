Enemy-love is difficult to take seriously because it seems to fail two tests we commonly use for morality: **desert** and **effectiveness**. It appears to give goodness to those who do not deserve it, without guaranteeing that goodness will change them. It can therefore sound either unjust or naïve—especially when it is reduced to being nice, suppressing anger, or asking victims to remain vulnerable.

Matthew 5, however, does not present a sentimental ethic. God still distinguishes “the evil” from “the good,” and “the just” from “the unjust.” Sunshine and rain do not pretend that these differences do not exist. God’s generosity is therefore **not moral approval**: he sustains people without endorsing everything they do. Enemy-love likewise does not require calling cruelty good, trusting someone untrustworthy, or abandoning the pursuit of justice.

Nor is Jesus primarily commanding a feeling. He does not say, “Feel affection for your enemies.” In the actions gathered around the command—blessing, doing good, praying—love appears as a settled orientation: **refusing to desire another person’s destruction and seeking their genuine good instead**. That good cannot mean helping their wrongdoing succeed. It may include their repentance, their being stopped from harming others, and their coming to live truthfully. Prayer for an enemy is not prayer that their cruelty prosper.

Enemy-love can certainly have strategic effects. It may interrupt retaliation, expose the emptiness of hatred, preserve one’s own humanity, or create an opening for reconciliation. But if it is practiced only because it works, it remains a technique: love becomes another way of managing the enemy. Jesus grounds it somewhere deeper—“that ye may be the children of your Father.” The point is not merely, **“This is the most effective way to live,”** but, **“This is what God’s goodness is like.”** God’s gifts do not wait for deserving recipients.

That makes enemy-love a form of moral strength, though not necessarily strength as the world usually imagines it. It is the strength to name wrong without becoming possessed by it; to resist someone without needing to dehumanize them; to refuse to let the enemy determine the whole shape of one’s character. Hatred can keep a person inwardly governed by the very injury they hate. Enemy-love seeks freedom from that government. It says, in effect: *You have done wrong, and I may need to oppose you. But your wrongdoing will not dictate what kind of person I become.*

This must not be turned into a command for victims to stay in danger, grant continued access to an abuser, or remain silent. These verses do not by themselves settle every question about self-defense, punishment, or public justice. Love is compatible with boundaries and accountability, and it is not identical with restored trust or reconciliation.

Its real difficulty is that it offers no guarantee. It surrenders the satisfaction of vengeance and may cost something without producing visible change. That is why prayer is so fitting: the disciple does not have to become the enemy’s savior or final judge.

So enemy-love has sentimental counterfeits, and it may have strategic fruits. But in Matthew its deepest rationale is neither optimism about enemies nor confidence in a technique. It is confidence about God: **one can take evil utterly seriously without allowing evil to set the limits of one’s love.**
