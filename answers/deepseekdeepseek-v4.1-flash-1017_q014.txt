Strictly speaking, Matthew 6:21 says neither “treasure reveals the heart” nor “treasure trains the heart” in modern psychological language. It says:

> “Where your treasure is, there will your heart be also.”

The movement is **from treasure to heart**: the heart is located by what one treasures. Because this saying is embedded in the command “lay up for yourselves treasures,” Jesus treats treasuring as a practice that **forms the heart**. So the verse is primarily **formative/locative**—treasure pulls the heart—and the “reveals” idea is a true corollary: what you actually treasure exposes where your heart already is and where it is going.

So if forced to choose: the text teaches more directly that **treasure trains/locates the heart**, but it also functions diagnostically as a revealer of the heart.

How possession, attention, and love tie together:

1. **Possession**  
“Treasure” is stored value, security, and worth. What we possess—or anxiously try to possess—claims our care. We protect it, maintain it, calculate for it, and worry about losing it. Earthly treasure is vulnerable to moth, rust, and thieves, so it creates anxious attention.

2. **Attention**  
The heart follows what attention returns to. We mentally visit, guard, and imagine what we value. Attention is the bridge between possession and love: what we own or want to own commands attention, and repeated attention matures into affection.

3. **Love**  
In Scripture the “heart” is the center of affection, will, and loyalty. Treasure is what we love and serve. Jesus makes this explicit in the next verse: “You cannot serve God and money” (Matt 6:24). Love chooses a treasure; treasure captures the heart; the heart then serves that treasure.

So the logic is a feedback loop: **what you possess commands attention; attention becomes love; love stores up more of that treasure.** Jesus calls for a deliberate reordering: “Lay up treasures in heaven,” where the treasure is secure and the heart can be rightly oriented toward God. The verse both reveals what currently rules the heart and calls for practices—generosity, prayer, mercy, kingdom-seeking—that retrain the heart’s love.
