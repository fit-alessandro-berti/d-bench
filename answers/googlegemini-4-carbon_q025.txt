In Matthew 6:1, the problem is definitively **the craving to be seen**, not visibility itself. 

We know visibility is not the issue because just fifteen verses earlier, in the exact same sermon, Jesus commands public visibility: *“Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father which is in heaven”* (Matthew 5:16). 

The apparent contradiction between chapter 5 and chapter 6 disappears when we look at the **motive**—the ultimate destination of the praise. In Matthew 5:16, good works are visible so that observers look *past* the doer to glorify God. In Matthew 6:1, good works are performed so that the gaze of the observers stops *at* the doer. 

In the original Greek, the phrase “to be seen” is *pros to theathēnai*, which shares the root of our English word **theater**. Jesus is warning against turning virtue into a stage performance. 

According to this verse (and the verses immediately following it), public goodness driven by the craving to be seen carries several profound moral dangers:

### 1. It instrumentalizes other people
Genuine charity (alms) is an act of love directed toward relieving a neighbor's suffering, done out of reverence for God. Performative goodness quietly hijacks this relationship. When you give *in order to be seen*, both the needy recipient and the watching crowd are reduced to mere props in a drama about your own virtue. You are no longer serving the poor; you are using the poor to purchase the admiration of the public.

### 2. It hollows out the soul (Hypocrisy)
In the verses that follow (6:2, 5, 16), Jesus calls people who do this “hypocrites” (*hypokritai*—literally, stage actors wearing masks). The moral danger of acting is that you confuse the mask with your actual face. Performative goodness breeds deep self-deception: because the *external action* is genuinely helpful (the poor person still gets the money), the giver is blinded to their *internal rot* (vanity, pride, and self-worship). As T.S. Eliot famously put it, the greatest treason is "to do the right deed for the wrong reason," because virtue itself becomes the hiding place for pride.

### 3. It substitutes a cheap, temporary currency for an eternal one
Jesus warns that if you act to be seen, “ye have no reward of your Father which is in heaven.” In the following verse, He adds of the hypocrites, “they have their reward.” The Greek word used there (*apechousin*) was a commercial term used on ancient receipts meaning **"paid in full."** 

This is less an arbitrary divine punishment and more a spiritual law of natural consequences. Every action aims at a specific wage. If you do good deeds to earn the applause of society, and society applauds you, the transaction is complete—paid in full. God owes you nothing, because you weren't working for Him; you were working for an audience. The moral danger is trading the weighty, transformative approval of the Creator for the fickle, fleeting validation of the crowd.

### 4. It enslaves you to the audience
Once goodness is motivated by the craving to be seen, your moral life is no longer anchored in truth or compassion; it is anchored in public opinion. A person who only does good when watched will inevitably stop doing good when no one is looking—or worse, will redefine "goodness" to match whatever the crowd happens to applaud at any given moment. 

### Summary
 Jesus begins the verse with **“Take heed”** (beware, stay on guard) precisely because this danger is so subtle. Bad deeds announce their own ugliness, so they are easy to spot. Good deeds, however, naturally attract praise, making it terribly easy for the human heart to shift from *doing good* to *loving the praise that goodness brings*. 

The 19th-century theologian A.B. Bruce summarized the harmony between Matthew 5:16 and Matthew 6:1 best: we are meant to **"show when tempted to hide, and hide when tempted to show."** Visibility is right when cowardice makes us want to blend in, and secrecy is vital when vanity makes us want to stand out.
