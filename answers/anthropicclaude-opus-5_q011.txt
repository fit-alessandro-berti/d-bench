## The grammar of the turn

The two clauses are built as a matched pair — both use the same construction, an imperative followed by an infinitive: *ḥidlû hārēaʿ* / *limdû hêṭēḇ*, "cease doing-evil" / "learn doing-good." The symmetry of form makes the asymmetry of content louder.

*Ḥadal* — cease, desist, leave off — describes something that can happen in an instant. The hand that is raised comes down. It is punctiliar; it has a moment.

*Lamad* — learn — has no moment. It is the root behind *talmid*, disciple, and *talmud*, instruction. To learn something is to be an apprentice at it: to be bad at it first, to practice, to be corrected, to fail in ways that are permitted because you are still learning. The prophet could have said "do good." He says *learn* to do good. That single word concedes that the people addressed do not yet know how, and grants them the time and the clumsiness that acquiring a craft requires.

So the two halves of the sentence run at different speeds. Evil is something you stop. Good is something you take up as a discipline.

## Why restraint alone would be a trap

Read the verse in its setting and the point sharpens. Isaiah 1 is a covenant lawsuit — heaven and earth summoned as witnesses (v. 2), the indictment read out. And the charge is not irreligion. The charge is *too much* religion: new moons, sabbaths, solemn assemblies, a multitude of sacrifices, hands spread out in prayer. The verdict on all of it is that God will hide his eyes, "for your hands are full of blood" (v. 15).

Notice what happens to the imagery. "Wash you, make you clean" is ritual vocabulary — the language of ablution before approaching the altar. Isaiah seizes it and points it somewhere else entirely: the washing he wants is *put away the evil of your doings*. And then he keeps going, because he knows exactly what a purification rite is capable of becoming. It becomes a project of self-regard. It becomes a people intensely, expensively occupied with the state of their own hands.

If the passage stopped at "cease to do evil," it would recreate the very sin it is prosecuting: a fastidiousness about one's own standing that never once looks outward. A man can be perfectly innocent while asleep. The negative commandments can, in principle, be satisfied by doing nothing at all — and a life organized around not-doing has a way of curving back on itself, becoming a private inventory of clean hands. Isaiah's God is not interested in an inventory. He wants the washed hands *used*.

That is the real function of the pivot. Cleanness is not the destination; it is the precondition. You wash before you work.

## What "good" turns out to mean

And then the prophet refuses to leave "good" abstract. Four imperatives follow, and they are startlingly concrete — in fact, they are almost all courtroom verbs:

- *dirshû mishpāṭ* — **seek justice**. Not receive it, not approve of it; go looking, investigate, inquire after it as one seeks a lost thing.
- *ʾashsherû ḥāmôṣ* — the KJV's "relieve the oppressed"; the Hebrew is disputed and may mean "set right the wronged" or "rebuke the oppressor." Either way it requires intervening in someone else's situation.
- *shifṭû yātôm* — **judge the orphan**, i.e., adjudicate his case, give him his day.
- *rîbû ʾalmānâ* — **plead the widow's cause**. Argue for her. Take her side in the dispute.

The orphan and the widow are not chosen sentimentally. In that society they are precisely the people who have no one to represent them — no father, no husband, no one whose standing makes their claim audible in the gate. They are the structurally unrepresented. And God's instruction to a people whose worship he has just refused is: *become their advocate.*

There is a fine irony in the last verb. *Rîb* is the same root as the lawsuit God has just brought against Israel. He litigates against them; he tells them to go litigate for someone else. Learn to stand on the right side of a case.

## The answer to the question

Goodness here is plainly not restraint. Restraint is the floor beneath goodness — necessary, since an oppressor cannot credibly plead the oppressed's cause, and bloody hands cannot be raised in prayer or in advocacy. But the floor is not the house.

Two things follow from Isaiah's word choice. First, goodness is a **practice** — something learned by repetition, in a community, from teachers, badly at first. It is a skill more than a state. Aristotle's observation that we become just by doing just acts is already assumed by the Hebrew verb: you do not acquire justice by resolving to have it.

Second, goodness is **directional**. Every one of the four imperatives sends you toward a specific person who is losing. Prohibitions are bounded — you can complete them. "Plead for the widow" has no completion; there is always another widow. The negative command has an end; the positive one opens a horizon you walk toward for the rest of your life.

Which is why the sentence has to move. Stopping is where repentance begins. It is not where righteousness lives.
