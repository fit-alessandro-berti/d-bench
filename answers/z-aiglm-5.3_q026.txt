## The setting: a scene that is already about speech

The passage doesn't arrive in a vacuum. Jesus has just delivered a man whom a demon had rendered blind and mute (12:22) — and the Pharisees, faced with an unambiguous good, can only produce one sentence: "This fellow doth not cast out devils, but by Beelzebub" (12:24). So when Jesus says "out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaketh," he is reading their words back to them as an audit of their interior. The imagery then stacks in three layers: *venom* ("generation of vipers" — offspring whose speech carries the parent's poison, cf. Psalm 140:3), *fruit* (v. 33, the tree known by its yield), and *treasure* (v. 35, the stockpile). Each layer contributes something distinct to the connection between mouth and heart.

## How the stockpile mechanism works

The key word is *thēsauros* — treasury, storehouse, accumulated stock (the word that gives us "thesaurus," fittingly, a treasury of words). Three features of a treasury matter here:

1. **It is stocked over time.** No one fills a treasury in an afternoon. The word spoken today draws on deposits made across a lifetime of attention, rehearsal, and love. This is why the evil man "treasures" evil — the verb implies curation, not accident. What's in the vault got there because someone valued it enough to keep it.
2. **It is hidden until spent.** The heart's contents are invisible to every observer — until the mouth issues them. The Greek verb is *ekballō*, a strong word — "casts out," even the same verb used for expelling demons. Speech is not a gentle sampling from the vault; it is a discharge.
3. **The mouth is the overflow point, not the factory.** *Perisseuma* ("abundance") means surplus — what the vessel can no longer contain. Speech is not manufactured at the lips; it is what the heart spills.

So the connection is hydraulic and economic at once: character accumulates in the vault, and the mouth is where the surplus surfaces.

## Words as symptoms

The overflow logic is essentially diagnostic. "How can ye, being evil, speak good things?" poses an impossibility of the contrary — at least in the deep sense. An evil man can counterfeit formally good sentences (hypocrites say "Lord, Lord"), but on this accounting the counterfeit is itself a withdrawal from the evil stock; deception is inventory too. What he *cannot* do is genuinely bless the manifest work of God — which is exactly what the Pharisees, staring at a healed man, failed to do. Their treasury had one output available.

The purest symptom is the "idle word" of v. 36 — *rhēma argon*, literally a "workless" word, one that did nothing, that no one bothered to curate. It is precisely the unguarded word that best diagnoses the stock, the way what spills when a cup is bumped shows what the cup was holding. We moderns tend to use "it just came out" as an excuse; Jesus uses it as evidence.

## Words as choices

Yet vv. 36–37 refuse to let symptom-logic collapse into determinism: every idle word will be audited, and "by thy words thou shalt be justified, and by thy words thou shalt be condemned." Judgment presupposes imputable agency. So choice operates at two levels:

- **The utterance itself** — the Pharisees' verdict on the healing was not a slip but a chosen interpretation of reality.
- **The stockpiling** — the deeper agency. Someone stocked that vault. Matthew 6:21 states the inverse principle: "where your treasure is, there will your heart be also." The present word is the yield of past treasuring.

The elegant part is that the idle word holds both levels together: it is the *least* chosen of words and still *fully* imputed — because even an unguarded withdrawal is charged to an account the account-holder built.

## Words as revelations

The tree/fruit logic in v. 33 is explicitly epistemic: "the tree is known by his fruit" (cf. 7:16–20). Words disclose the otherwise invisible heart — to observers, to the speaker (we often discover our own stockpiles by what escapes us: "I didn't know I felt that until I heard myself say it"), and finally to the divine court. That is the forensic force of v. 37: at the judgment no external prosecutor is needed, because the mouth has been transcribing the heart all along. Your words are the deposition your heart gives — for you or against you. Speech is the heart made audible and therefore judgeable.

## Why it must be all three

Each category alone breaks down against the text:

- **Symptom alone** yields fatalism — judgment on words would be like punishing a sneeze. Verses 36–37 forbid this.
- **Choice alone** yields voluntarism — words as free-floating acts unconditioned by character, with hypocrisy unexplainable. Verses 34–35 forbid this.
- **Revelation alone** makes words mere signs without moral stakes.

What the passage actually offers is an integrated anthropology: **every word is at once a willed act, an overflow of a stocked treasury, and the unveiling of that treasury in public.** The three are not rival theories but three faces of a single event — the moment the hidden stockpile goes on record.

## The turn: v. 33's imperative

It matters that Jesus commands "make the tree good" — the passage is not fatalistic, and the remedy is not mouth-management. You cannot purify the overflow by plugging the spillway; the treasury itself must be restocked. Psalm 119:11 describes exactly that strategy ("Thy word have I hid in mine heart"), and Colossians 3:16 gives it a positively hydraulic grammar: let the word of Christ dwell in you *richly* — abundance again — and psalms and teaching are what come out. For the Christian reader, that restocking is grace's work before it is discipline (Ezekiel 36:26), since no mouth can refill its own vault.

In short: the passage connects speech to the heart as overflow to reservoir, and it treats words as chosen revelations of a symptomatic condition — acts you answer for, signs by which you are known, and the surplus of what you have spent your life treasuring.
