Enemy-love is difficult to take seriously because it asks for more than good feelings. It asks us to do good toward people who may not return it—and to resist the satisfying impulse to repay injury with injury. That can look naïve in a world where retaliation seems protective and power seems to mean making others afraid.

But Jesus’ command is neither sentimentality nor a tactic for winning enemies over. Sentiment can fade, and strategy still treats the other person as a means to an end. In Matthew, the reason given is imitation: God gives sun and rain to the good and the bad alike. Enemy-love reflects a generosity that is not limited to people who deserve it or can repay it.

That makes it a form of moral strength: the strength to oppose wrongdoing without letting hatred dictate one’s character. It does not mean calling evil good, abandoning justice, or remaining in danger. Love may require distance, firm boundaries, protection of victims, and truthful accountability. Forgiveness or reconciliation cannot simply be demanded of someone who has been harmed.

To take enemy-love seriously, then, is to refuse dehumanization and revenge while still naming harm and resisting it. It is not weakness before an enemy; it is refusing to become governed by the enemy’s way of acting.
