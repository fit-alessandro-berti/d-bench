Luke 6:37 is often interpreted in modern culture as an endorsement of moral relativism—a command to suspend all critical thinking and never evaluate human behavior. However, within its historical, linguistic, and philosophical context, the verse does not abolish moral distinctions; rather, it targets the **spirit of vindictive superiority** and the usurpation of ultimate judgment.

To understand why this differs from saying moral judgments never matter, we must explore the boundary between **discernment** and the **appetite to condemn**.

---

### 1. Discernment vs. Condemnation: A Functional Difference

* **Discernment (Moral Reality):** Discernment is the ability to see things as they truly are. It distinguishes between truth and falsehood, justice and oppression, virtue and vice. Without moral discernment, one cannot protect the vulnerable, practice justice, or even forgive (because forgiveness requires first recognizing that a genuine wrong was committed). 
* **Condemnation (Judicial Retribution):** Condemnation goes beyond recognizing an evil act; it passes an ultimate, irrevocable verdict on the *person*. It writes an individual off as irredeemable, assigns motive, and seeks punishment rather than restoration.

The Greek word for "judge" in this passage is *krinō* (to separate, pick out, or pass sentence), but it is immediately clarified by the next clause: *“condemn not”* (*katadikazō*—to pronounce final guilt/sentence against someone). Jesus is not commanding moral blindness; He is forbidding the human ego from acting as the ultimate court of cosmic justice.

---

### 2. The Nature of the "Appetite to Condemn"

The appetite to condemn is a psychological and spiritual pathology. It is characterized by:

* **Moral Deflection:** Projecting one’s own unacknowledged brokenness onto others. (As Jesus notes just four verses later in Luke 6:41: focusing on the speck in another's eye while ignoring the log in one's own.)
* **Self-Righteous Elevation:** Using another person’s moral failure as a pedestal to establish one's own righteousness. The Pharisaical appetite thrives on comparison.
* **Finality:** The condemner closes the door on the possibility of the other person's transformation.

When Jesus says *"Judge not,"* He is warning against this hungry, vindictive impulse—the desire to see others exposed, disgraced, and punished to validate ourselves.

---

### 3. How the Context Proves Moral Judgments Still Matter

If Luke 6:37 meant that moral judgments never matter, the rest of Jesus's teachings would be incoherent:
* A few verses later (Luke 6:43–45), Jesus tells His listeners to **evaluate fruit**—to recognize that "a good tree bringeth not forth corrupt fruit." You must make a moral judgment to know what is good fruit versus bad fruit.
* In John 7:24, Jesus explicitly commands: *“Judge not according to the appearance, but judge righteous judgment.”*
* To obey the final clause of Luke 6:37—*“forgive, and ye shall be forgiven”*—you must first judge that an offense has occurred. Forgiveness without moral judgment is not mercy; it is merely pretending the harm did not exist.

---

### 4. A Helpful Analogy: The Physician vs. The Executioner

The difference between discernment and condemnation can be understood through the roles of a doctor and an executioner:

* **The Discernment of the Physician:** A doctor must evaluate the patient accurately. They identify cancer, call it what it is without sugarcoating, and recognize it as deadly. The diagnosis is clear, precise, and uncompromising. Yet the goal is **healing, preservation, and life**.
* **The Condemnation of the Executioner:** The executioner also examines the crime, but the goal is **destruction, termination, and retribution**. 

Luke 6:37 forbids believers from adopting the posture of the executioner, while calling them to exercise the discernment of the physician.

---

### Summary

Moral judgments **matter deeply** because truth, goodness, and protection of the innocent depend on them. What Luke 6:37 condemns is not the act of moral evaluation, but the **arrogance of final condemnation**. 

True discernment diagnoses sin in order to apply grace and pursue restoration; the appetite to condemn diagnoses sin in order to exert power and destroy the sinner.
