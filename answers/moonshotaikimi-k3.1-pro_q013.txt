Amos is not diagnosing an imbalance within sincere piety—**too much ceremony and too little ethics**. He is exposing ceremony being used to sustain a lie. The problem is not merely that justice has been neglected alongside worship; worship itself has become a means of avoiding repentance while retaining the assurance of God’s favor.

The surrounding chapter makes the charge concrete. Amos condemns those who trample the poor, take bribes, and deny the poor justice “in the gate” (Amos 5:10–12). Against that background, the festivals and offerings function in several fraudulent ways:

- **Substitution:** ritual performance is offered in place of changed conduct. Costly sacrifices and impressive music are supposed to suffice without ending exploitation or correcting the courts.
- **Transaction:** worship is treated almost as leverage. The worshipers give God gifts and expect acceptance, protection, and continued prosperity in return, while reserving the right to live as they please.
- **False witness:** the festivals publicly declare, “We are God’s faithful people,” while their common life denies it. God is asked to lend his name and favor to that contradiction.

That helps explain the totality of the rejection: “I will not smell … I will not accept … neither will I regard … I will not hear.” Accepting the offerings would symbolically confirm the worshipers’ claim to covenant peace. God refuses to provide that confirmation. “The noise of thy songs” is therefore not an aesthetic criticism of music, nor necessarily a blanket condemnation of sacrifice and festival. It is the exposure of worship that has become religious cover for injustice—an attempt to use God rather than seek him.

There is also something close to idolatry here. The deity being invoked is no longer truly the Lord who demands justice and defends the wronged, but a imagined patron who can be ceremonially placated while remaining indifferent to oppression. The correct divine name and traditional forms do not make that worship truthful.

Verse 24 consequently demands more than an ethical supplement to the liturgy. “Judgment” (*mishpat*) concerns just decisions and the vindication of the wronged; “righteousness” (*tsedaqah*) concerns conduct and relationships ordered according to what is right. These are to flow like abundant, continuing water—not appear occasionally, like a festival observance or an act of compensatory charity. Courts, economic dealings, and public life must be changed.

This does **not** mean that worshipers must be morally perfect before approaching God. The issue is unrepentant people using worship specifically to avoid the repentance God requires. Nor does Amos simply replace religion with social activism. He denies that devotion to God can be genuine when it is deliberately detached from God’s own moral will.

In short: **they are not merely doing one good thing too much and another good thing too little; they are using sacred things to legitimize an unjust life. God’s severe refusal is his refusal to be bribed or to let his worship become an alibi for oppression.**
