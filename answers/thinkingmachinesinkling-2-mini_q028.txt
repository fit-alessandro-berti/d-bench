It is more than “don’t judge” and more than a scolding about hypocrisy. It exposes the machinery by which people justify punishing another while claiming moral standing.

A few layers:

- **Hypocrisy is partly what is broken.** The religiously righteous expect their “sinless” stance to be verified by an external action: lifting a stone, issuing a verdict, enacting the law spectacularly. Jesus makes that performance impossible by asking for the inability to sin—an identity no one honestly possesses.
- **But the point is not mere embarrassment.** The sentence undoes the whole logic of moral superiority. If judgment is meant to express innocence, then judgment becomes impossible for the very person trying the hardest to exercise it.
- **It interrupts mob-righteousness.** Several present together for righteous action. The threat is not simply “be compassionate” but “notice that righteousness organized through collective moral self-regard functions as a mechanism of exclusion.”
- **It redirects judgment toward self-recognition.** Justice without self-examination is aestheticized cruelty; mercy toward the vulnerable can be performed as status rather than as real love. The sentence forces the judge to ask: what does it mean to judge a human being as though I were outside sin?

Yet it does **not** forbid all judgment. It forbids a certain kind of judgment: the kind that is used to crown one group as pure and legitimate out of some supposedly innocent posture. The deeper effect is posture-shifting. Jesus does not say “never distinguish.” He says: do not turn human accountability into a badge of holiness.

So: not simply “forbid judgment,” nor simply “expose hypocrisy.” It interrupts sacralized righteousness by revealing that an upright identity cannot be built by looking at others more honestly. The morally destabilizing move is that mercy becomes the precondition for judgment rather than merely the opposite of it.
