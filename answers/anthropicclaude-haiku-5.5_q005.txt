The difficulty comes from a few places. We usually hear "love" as a feeling, and no one can produce warmth on command. We also tend to think moral seriousness means keeping score: wrongs should be answered, and forgiveness or generosity toward a wrongdoer can look like betraying the people he harmed. And the sun-and-rain image sounds like a poetic blur, since God does discriminate in judgment elsewhere in Scripture.

The text itself resists the sentimental reading. The commands are verbs of action: bless, do good, pray. Agapē is about the orientation of the will toward another's real good, not about how you feel. Jesus doesn't say "feel fondly toward your enemies." He describes conduct that continues whether or not affection is present. Sentimentality would mean the love depends on emotion, and the passage asks for something that holds when emotion fails.

The strategic reading has some textual support. Romans 12:20 ("heap coals of fire on his head") and the general pattern of disarming hostility suggest that kindness often works. But Matthew undercuts it. Verse 46 asks, "If ye love them which love you, what reward have ye?" and the stated aim is "that ye may be the children of your Father," not that enemies will be won over. If the love is justified only by its payoff, it is just a more refined form of reciprocity, the very thing Jesus is criticizing. Love that is conditional on success is still a transaction, and it will stop when the enemy refuses to yield.

The strongest reading is that enemy-love is a form of moral strength, and it is hard precisely because it is strength. Consider what it refuses to do:

- **It refuses to let the enemy author your character.** Hatred makes the wrongdoer the shaper of your inner life. Enemy-love keeps your conduct answerable to your own commitments rather than to his.
- **It separates love from approval.** You can oppose someone, resist his injustice, and even seek his punishment through legitimate institutions, while still wishing him well and refusing to dehumanize him. Martin Luther King Jr. drew this distinction in his sermon "Loving Your Enemies," arguing that agapē can coexist with resistance to evil.
- **It acknowledges shared dependence.** The sun-and-rain line points to God's common gifts, which are given to all. The point is not that God doesn't judge, but that the disciple's generosity should not be narrower than the Creator's.

Jesus's own practice on the cross ("Father, forgive them") is the clearest model. That is not sentimentality. It is an act performed under maximal provocation.

So my view is that enemy-love is not sentimental and not primarily strategic. It is a demanding discipline of the will that refuses to let retaliation define you. Its difficulty is that it asks you to keep wanting the other person's good when every incentive, emotional and social, points the other way. The skeptic's worry is legitimate in one respect: the command doesn't by itself settle how justice should be pursued, and enemy-love can be abused to pressure victims into silence. But that is a misuse of the command, which, read carefully, leaves room for protecting the vulnerable and holding wrongdoers accountable while refusing to make hatred the rule of your heart.
