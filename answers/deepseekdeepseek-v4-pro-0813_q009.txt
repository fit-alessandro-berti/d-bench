Romans 12:20–21 defines victory over evil not as defeating an enemy, but as defeating **evil itself** by doing good.

### The passage’s definition of victory

Paul writes:

> “Be not overcome of evil, but overcome evil with good.”

The first part warns that if you return evil for evil, evil has **conquered you**. The enemy’s real victory is not that he hurt you, but that he made you become like him—bitter, vengeful, hateful.

The second part redefines victory: good is the weapon. Evil is overcome not by superior force or revenge, but by active, concrete goodness—feeding a hungry enemy, giving drink to a thirsty enemy.

So victory over evil means:

- refusing to let someone else’s evil control your character,
- responding to hostility with love,
- breaking the cycle of evil by replacing it with good,
- leaving vengeance to God rather than taking it into your own hands.

### What about “heaping coals of fire on his head”?

This phrase comes from Proverbs 25:21–22. It is often misunderstood as a disguised form of revenge—“be nice to your enemy so he will feel terrible.” But in context, Paul has just said:

> “Avenge not yourselves, but rather give place unto wrath: for it is written, Vengeance is mine; I will repay, saith the Lord.” (Romans 12:19)

So Paul cannot be commanding kindness as a sneaky way to harm someone. The “coals of fire” likely refer to the burning shame or conviction that comes when an enemy realizes he has received good in return for evil. It is meant to lead to repentance and reconciliation, not to gloating or secret revenge.

### Is the command weak, manipulative, or genuinely strong?

**Genuinely strong.**

- It is not weak, because it takes far more strength to master your anger and serve an enemy than to strike back.
- It is not manipulative, because the goal is the enemy’s good and the defeat of evil, not hidden control or payback.
- It is active, not passive. Paul does not say “ignore evil.” He says conquer it—but with good.

The passage teaches that evil is ultimately defeated when it is met with the opposite of itself. That is the strength of love, and Paul presents it as the Christian’s true victory.
