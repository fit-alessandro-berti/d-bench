Here is a clear, theologically grounded, and biblically faithful explanation—focused directly on the question, avoiding overstatement or speculation. It uses plain language and addresses both parts of the query step by step:

1. **Difference between external compliance ("keeping statutes, judgments, doing them") and receiving a "new heart":**  
    *External compliance* refers to *acting* according to God's laws, commands, or ethical standards (e.g., "I obey the rule," "I perform the ritual," "I avoid the sinful act"). It can be consistent, sporadic, or motivated by reasons like fear, social pressure, habit, external reward, or even superficial piety. Someone can *comply externally* without genuine inner change—e.g., obeying rules out of duty, avoiding sin to avoid punishment, or because it fits their identity ("I'm a good person who follows these rules"). External compliance is behavioral and observable.

    *Receiving a "new heart" (and new spirit)* refers to an **internal transformation at the level of identity, desire, motivation, and capacity**. It is described as God *giving* a "heart of flesh" (replacing the "stony heart") *and* putting His Spirit *within* the person. Key implications:
    *   The heart (which in Scripture consistently represents the inner self—thoughts, emotions, will, desires, understanding—cf. Prov. 4:23; Ps. 51:10) is no longer stony/hardened but "flesh"—implying softness, sensitivity, responsiveness, relational capacity, and ability to feel, love, perceive God's goodness and holiness.
    *   The Spirit of God dwells *within*, actively guiding, empowering, and shaping the inner life.
    *   As a result, walking in God's statutes ("cause you to walk in my statutes") and keeping judgments naturally follows *from the inner state*: it is no longer a struggle imposed from outside but flows from the new heart's desires, understanding, and Spirit-empowered ability. The phrase "ye shall keep my judgments, and do them" (v.27) is included *after* the heart/spirit description precisely because the new heart *enables* consistent, internalized obedience—not as an additional separate task apart from the heart change.

    > **In short: External compliance is *doing* the right things; receiving a new heart is *being* changed from within so that doing the right things flows naturally from one's new desires, understanding, and Spirit-empowered capacity. Compliance without the new heart can be consistent but disconnected from inner conviction, motivation, or vulnerability; the new heart makes consistent, relational obedience possible and characteristic.**

2. **Why is hardness ("the stony heart") described almost like a material condition that must be replaced?**  
    *   The description ("take away the stony heart... give you an heart of flesh") uses concrete, physical imagery deliberately. While "heart" in Scripture often symbolizes the inner person (mind, emotions, will), calling it "stony" and "hard" is meant to be vividly *descriptive* of the *functional reality* of hardened rebellion/apathy toward God. It is not merely a vague metaphor; it accurately describes how sin and spiritual indifference operate:
        *   **Resistance:** A stony heart *resists* God's voice, conviction, compassion, and invitation (cf. Jer. 17:9-10; Rom. 1:21; Ps. 95:7-11). It cannot be persuaded by reason alone or gentle appeal; it is "hard" like rock.
        *   **Emotional Insensitivity:** It lacks responsiveness to God's goodness, mercy, grace, or the pain of sin/consequence (e.g., no sorrow for sin, no desire for relationship). "Stone" naturally feels little.
        *   **Moral Rigidity/Inflexibility:** It defaults to self-centeredness, rebellion, or indifference; it cannot easily perceive or value God's standards or be softened by grace. It lacks the "flesh" quality of sensitivity, tenderness, relational openness.
        *   **Functional Reality:** From God's perspective, the person *is* living out of this stony condition (e.g., persisting in sin despite awareness, lacking genuine desire for holiness, being spiritually insensitive). Calling it "the stony heart" accurately names the *root cause* of the observed behavior (external compliance *or* continued hardness). It's not just "they choose to sin"; it's that their inner core is functionally stony.

    *   Therefore, saying God "takes away the stony heart" and "gives a heart of flesh" uses material language (*stone*, *flesh*) appropriately because:
        1.  It conveys the **functional truth**: The person *is* operating out of a hard, insensitive, resistant inner state (the "stone").
        2.  It signals **God's active intervention**: God does not merely *encourage* softening; He *replaces* the stony condition with a new heart capable of feeling, loving, and responding. This reflects God's sovereign power to change the inner person (cf. regeneration = new birth; Titus 3:5).
        3.  It avoids vague abstraction: Saying "God makes them less hard" is less precise. Saying "the stony heart is taken away and a heart of flesh is given" clearly states the *change in the inner constitution* that enables the promised obedience. "Flesh" (as opposed to "stone") implies permeability, responsiveness, relational capacity—the opposite of hardness.

    > **In short: "Stony heart" is descriptive, functional language (like calling a lid "tight" or a wall "high") that accurately captures how rebellion/apathy operates internally—resisting conviction, lacking sensitivity, being inflexible. God describes replacing it because He actively changes the *inner capacity*: the stony condition *is* the root cause of continued hardness or ineffective compliance; the new "heart of flesh" with God's Spirit *is* the necessary internal condition that allows genuine, relational, Spirit-empowered obedience to flow naturally. It is "material" in the sense of naming the concrete inner state functioning in the person, not in claiming the heart is literally stone (a physiological claim).

**Why this matters practically (briefly, for clarity):**  
Someone can consistently *do* what God asks (external compliance) for many reasons: fear of punishment, desire for approval, habit, or temporary conviction. But if the inner heart remains stony (resistant, insensitive, self-centered), that compliance is fragile, prone to lapse under pressure, lacks genuine joy or love for God, and is not sustained by inner motivation. God's promise is not just "do better," but "I will replace your hard inner condition with a soft, responsive heart, put my Spirit within you, and then your walking in my statutes will naturally follow from who you now are." True obedience rooted in the new heart flows from delight, understanding, and Spirit-empowered desire—not merely external rule-following.

> **Key takeaway in one sentence:** Receiving a "new heart" means God fundamentally changes the inner person's capacity—replacing the resistant, insensitive "stony" condition with a responsive "heart of flesh" inhabited by His Spirit—so that walking in God's statutes flows naturally from the new inner desires and motivations; external compliance alone does not guarantee this internal transformation and can be inconsistent or merely rule-based without the heart change. God describes the change as replacing the "stony heart" because that hardness accurately names the functional inner reality causing resistance and poor response, and His promise is active, sovereign internal renewal.
