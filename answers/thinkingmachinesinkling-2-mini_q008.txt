1 John 4:20 is not a syllogism that lets us prove God’s existence from someone’s behavior. It is a moral diagnostic: love is a disposition in a person, and a disposition cannot be fully “invisible” without some shape in visible life. God is unseen, but the “neighbor” is seen, so the visible relationship is the easiest place to discover whether love is real or only spoken. In John’s theology, “love of God” is not merely intellectual apprehension of an abstract object; it is union in a way of life. So if someone claims love for the invisible God, that love is already supposed to show itself somewhere—and, since the person has actually seen a brother or sister, the clearest locus is the neighbor.

The verse also exposes an evasion created by the hiddenness of God. Because God is not bodily perceived, people can make a “spiritual” profession without being answerable in any concrete way. John says: do not do exactly that. If what you say about God is to mean anything, it must land on real human need, injury, closeness, and command. Love of God is therefore tested by whether it produces mercy, steadiness, and willingness toward the seen person.

The self-deception being exposed is the split between profession and posture: a person can speak fluently about God while privately withholding tenderness, refusing to help, judging a brother harshly, or using religious language to avoid human life. It is also a prideful self-deception—the fantasy that one can be “spiritual” while remaining distant, or that hiddenness of God excuses inaction toward the visible. John is not saying that the neighbor can replace divine faith; he is saying that when the neighbor is ignored, the claim “I love God” collapses into an untested slogan rather than a real disposition. In that sense, the passage denies a spiritualized ego in which love is treated as feeling or identity without consequence. The test is not technical but existential: love of God remains humanly credible only if it does not disappear the moment it enters the ordinary visible world.
