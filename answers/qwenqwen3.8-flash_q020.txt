Bitterness is called a **root** rather than an act because the author of Hebrews is not warning primarily about an occasional angry moment, but about a **hidden inner condition** that develops over time. An act is visible and temporary; a root is concealed, persistent, and productive.

The image suggests several things:

1. **Hidden moral growth**  
   Bitterness usually begins beneath the surface. It may start as an unprocessed hurt, a sense of offense, pride, unforgiveness, or a claim on one’s own pain. At first it may not look like a serious sin. But like a root, it can spread quietly in the heart, feeding on memories, narratives of injury, and resentment. The problem is not merely that someone does something bitter, but that bitterness becomes a settled disposition within them.

2. **Delayed damage**  
   A root can grow unseen for a long time before producing obvious fruit. A person may carry bitterness for weeks, months, or years without it fully revealing itself. By the time it erupts into harsh speech, conflict, revenge, slander, or withdrawal, much damage has already occurred in the heart. The root imagery shows that sin is not always immediate in its worst effects. It can mature slowly, becoming more entrenched and more destructive over time.

3. **One person’s resentment can spread to others**  
   The text says that the root of bitterness can “trouble you” and “thereby many be defiled.” This is communal as well as personal. Bitterness is not private poison; it tends to affect relationships. One resentful person can draw others into their grievance, normalize complaint, stir suspicion, and create division. Gossip, factions, anger, and cynicism can grow from one root. What began in one heart may spread through a household, church, workplace, or community.

So the metaphor of a **root** emphasizes that bitterness is more than a single wrong action. It is a source of further wrongdoing. It grows, feeds, produces fruit, and spreads. Hebrews therefore urges vigilance not only over visible conduct but over hidden attitudes. “Looking diligently” means examining the heart before bitterness has taken root, because when bitterness is only a sprout it may be uprooted; when it becomes a root, it threatens peace, holiness, and the whole community.
