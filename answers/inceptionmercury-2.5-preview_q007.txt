

This passage is often misinterpreted as a call for moral relativism, suggesting that right and wrong hold no weight. However, the text does not abolish ethical evaluation; rather, it reorients the posture behind it. The core distinction lies between **discernment**, which seeks truth and restoration, and **condemnation**, which seeks finality and exclusion.

**1. Moral Judgments Do Matter**
If moral judgments never mattered, the command to "forgive" would be unnecessary. Forgiveness presupposes that an offense occurred and that justice or righteousness is a valid concern. The verse assumes a framework of right and wrong but warns against how humans wield that framework against one another. It challenges the authority to issue a final verdict on another’s character or eternal standing, a role reserved for the divine in Christian theology.

**2. Discernment vs. The Appetite to Condemn**
*   **Discernment** is the capacity to distinguish between good and bad, true and false. It is essential for safety, growth, and community health. A leader might discern a behavior as harmful to correct it; a friend might discern a choice as unwise to offer advice. This form of judgment is constructive and often internal or restorative.
*   **The Appetite to Condemn** is driven by pride and superiority. It transforms evaluation into a weapon, focusing on punishment rather than repair. This attitude assumes a position of ultimate authority over another’s worth and often ignores one’s own flaws (as highlighted in the preceding verses about removing the plank from one’s own eye).

**Conclusion**
Luke 6:37 critiques the *heart* behind the judgment, not the *act* of moral reasoning. It invites humility—recognizing that our assessments are limited and that mercy should prevail over superiority. We are called to evaluate actions to uphold truth, but to avoid condemning persons, leaving ultimate judgment to God and prioritizing forgiveness in our own conduct.
