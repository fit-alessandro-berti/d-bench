

Isaiah 55:7 does not limit repentance to “stop doing bad things.” The pairing of **“way”** and **“thoughts”**—depending on translation, *way, thoughts, desires, purposes, plans*—shows that repentance must reach the whole person.

### 1. “Way”: outward conduct as a life-pattern

The “way” is not merely a single sinful act. It is the habitual direction of life: choices, practices, relationships, loyalties, and repeated behavior. To forsake one’s way is to break out of a pattern.

So repentance implies real external change. It is not only a feeling of regret; it is a turning away from a course of life.

### 2. “Thoughts”: the inner life that generates conduct

The “thoughts” are not random mental images. In this context they point to the inner intentions, desires, imaginations, and mental orientations from which conduct flows.

This is important: outward behavior can be reformed while the heart still clings to its own plans, ambitions, resentments, fantasies, or self-justifications. That would be incomplete repentance. Conversely, inward change must produce outward change; otherwise it remains vague self-deception.

So “way” and “thoughts” together express the unity of sin:  
**what one does** is shaped by **what one wants, imagines, and aims at**.

Repentance, then, is not only behavioral correction. It is a reorientation of the moral imagination: one’s desires, hopes, and purposes are turned back to God.

### 3. Mercy: possible because the turn is toward a near God

The verse moves from forsaking to returning:

> “and let him return unto the Lord, and he will have mercy upon him… for he will abundantly pardon.”

The possibility of mercy rests not on our ability to purify ourselves completely, but on God’s nearness and his abundant pardon. The invitation is urgent: “while he may be found,” “while he is near.”

Mercy is therefore both:

- **conditional**: there is a required turning away from way and thoughts and a turning toward the Lord;
- **abundant**: God’s forgiveness is not small or grudging. He “abundantly pardons.”

### In short

The verse teaches that true repentance changes both **what we do** and **what we desire and imagine**. Outward conduct is important, but it is not the deepest root of sin; the inner life of thought, desire, and intention is also brought into judgment and transformation. And mercy remains possible because God invites the whole person—body, mind, will, and imagination—to return to him.
