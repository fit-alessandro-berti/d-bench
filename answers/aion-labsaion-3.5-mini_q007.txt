## The Verse Refutes the Relativist Reading From Within

Notice first what the verse itself does. It is structured as a parallelism: "judge not" is immediately glossed by "condemn not" — and this is the verse interpreting itself. The judging prohibited is the condemning kind. Then comes the positive command: "forgive, and ye shall be forgiven." And forgiveness is impossible without moral judgment — you can only forgive what you have first judged to be a genuine wrong. Forgiveness is not the suspension of moral evaluation; it is its completion in mercy. If nothing were truly wrong, there would be nothing to forgive, and the command would be incoherent.

So Luke 6:37 is not an epistemological claim ("you cannot know right from wrong") but a relational one ("the way you judge will become the way you are judged"). Moral relativism says judgments are meaningless. This verse says they are dangerously *meaningful* — so meaningful that they return to the one who utters them.

## Discernment: Vision in Service of Repair

The parallel passage in Matthew 7 makes the distinction explicit through the image of the mote and the beam. The man with a beam in his own eye is not told to stop noticing his brother's speck. He is told to remove the beam first, "and then shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote out of thy brother's eye." Clear sight is the goal; self-blindedness is the obstacle. Discernment is vision aimed at the other's healing.

A few verses later in the same sermon, Jesus says, "of thine own self... the evil man out of the evil treasure of his heart bringeth forth that which is evil" — and "by their fruits ye shall know them" is not far off (Luke 6:43–45). The Sermon on the Plain assumes moral evaluation constantly. A community without discernment could not correct, could not protect, could not forgive. Discernment says "this is wrong" and opens a path toward repair. It is a faculty, like sight or taste — it can be refined, but it does not consume. It is satisfied by truth, not by someone's downfall.

## The Appetite to Condemn: A Different Thing in Kind

Condemnation is not discernment carried too far; it is a different act with a different aim. And the word "appetite" in your question is exactly right, because condemnation has the signature of appetite:

- **It is pleasurable.** Righteous indignation feels good — there is a reason public condemnation is the most addictive currency of every age, ours included.
- **It grows with feeding.** Each condemnation makes the next easier and coarser.
- **It displaces other hungers.** The more one craves condemnation, the less one craves justice, mercy, or truth.
- **It aims at finality.** Where discernment says "this is wrong, and there is a way back," condemnation says "you are wrong" — a verdict on the *person*, not the act. It closes the case, seals the sentence, places the other beyond reach. It is judgment as foreclosure.

It is also, typically, self-exalting and evasive. Condemnation is a cheap way to stand tall: we lower others to raise ourselves. And the appetite to condemn is strongest precisely where self-examination is most needed — which is why the verse is paired with the beam. The condemner is usually not judging too sharply but too safely, aiming at targets that cost him nothing.

## The Measure That Returns

The verse's own explanation follows immediately: "with the same measure that ye mete withal it shall be measured to you again" (v. 38). This is why judgment is dangerous — not because judgments are meaningless, but because they are *generative*. They build the world the judge inhabits. The condemner lives in a condemned world: suspicious, defensive, unforgiven — partly because he has taught everyone around him that verdicts are how things work here, and partly because a soul that feeds on condemnation becomes incapable of receiving anything else. The punishment is intrinsic to the act.

Relativism says judgments don't matter. This verse says they matter so much they *transform the one who makes them*.

## Jurisdiction, Not Epistemology

The deepest issue may not be whether we *can* know right from wrong but *who gets to issue final verdicts*. Humans can and must judge acts — that is discernment, the basis of justice, parenting, self-correction, and forgiveness itself. But the final verdict on a person — condemnation, the sealing of a soul — requires a knowledge no human has: the whole heart, the whole history, the whole circumstance. Condemnation usurps a seat that belongs to God; discernment serves as a witness within a process that remains open. The prohibition is against claiming the final word, not against using your eyes.

## Why Relativism Actually Destroys the Distinction

Here is the closing irony: moral relativism dissolves the very difference the verse depends on. If no act is truly wrong, then discernment and condemnation are both just noise — preferences dressed as perceptions — and forgiveness is pointless, since there is nothing real to release anyone from. The verse, by contrast, is fiercely anti-relativist. It presumes a moral order so real that our posture toward it — condemning or forgiving — determines our own standing within it.

Far from saying that moral judgments never matter, it says they matter with a weight we rarely imagine: they return to us, pressed down and running over.
