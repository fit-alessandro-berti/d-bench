The verse is often misread as a charter for moral relativism—as though Jesus were saying, "Nothing is really wrong, so stop pretending you can tell." But that reading collapses almost immediately. The Sermon on the Plain, of which this verse is part, is saturated with moral demand: love your enemies, do good to those who hate you, turn the other cheek, be merciful as your Father is merciful. You cannot command mercy in a world where nothing is wrong. Mercy only makes sense if there is something real to forgive.

So the prohibition is not against moral perception. It is against something else—something that *uses* moral perception for a different purpose than truth.

**Discernment is perception; condemnation is appetite.**

Discernment is the capacity to see moral reality accurately: this action is cruel, this arrangement is unjust, this pattern is destructive. It is a truth-function, like a physician diagnosing illness. It is necessary for action—you cannot protect the vulnerable, correct a wrong, or even forgive meaningfully without first perceiving that a wrong occurred. Forgiveness without discernment is not forgiveness; it is blindness. Jesus himself discerned constantly and out loud: he called the Pharisees whitewashed tombs, named hypocrisy where he saw it, drove the money-changers out. He was not a relativist. He saw very clearly, and said so.

What he warned against was not seeing, but *sentencing*—the movement of the heart in which perceiving a fault in another becomes pleasurable, and in which that pleasure becomes a substitute for one's own transformation. The appetite to condemn has a recognizable signature:

- It is *eager*. It scans for faults rather than grieving them. It is quicker to find guilt than to hope for repentance.
- It is *self-exculpating*. This is the beam and the mote from the parallel passage (Matthew 7:3–5). Condemning others is one of the most efficient ways of evacuating one's own guilt—if I am busy prosecuting you, I am not on the stand.
- It *writes people off*. Condemnation, in the sense warned against here, is not "you did wrong" but "you *are* your wrong, and there is an end of you." It forecloses the future. The Greek word for "condemn" here (*katadikazete*) is a legal term for passing a final sentence.
- It *positions the judge above the judged*. It is a technology of superiority. The one who condemns is always, in the act of condemning, quietly asserting their own cleanliness.

**The verse itself contains the argument against the relativist reading.**

Look at the logic: "Judge not, and ye shall not be judged... forgive, and ye shall be forgiven." This is a reciprocity structure. The measure you use will be the measure used on you. But reciprocity only works if moral judgment is *real*. If judgment never mattered, there would be nothing to be measured by. The verse presupposes a moral universe in which standards are live and binding—it simply warns that the standard you wield against others is the standard that will be applied to you. That is a warning against hypocrisy and mercilessness, not against moral truth. It says: *judgment is real, and you are not exempt from it, so wield it with the trembling you would want from your own judge.*

**The difference, stated plainly:**

To say "moral judgments never matter" is to abolish the distinction between good and evil. To say "judge not" is to relocate the sinner from the judge's bench. The first dissolves morality; the second deepens it. The relativist says: *there is nothing to see.* Jesus says: *there is plenty to see, including in yourself, and the seeing is meant to make you merciful, not triumphant.*

Discernment aims at truth and, ultimately, at restoration. The appetite to condemn aims at the self—at the pleasure of righteousness without the cost of it. This is why the same act of perception can be two completely different things depending on the heart doing the perceiving. Two people can say "that was wrong." One says it the way a surgeon says "there is a tumor"—with clarity and grief and intent to heal. The other says it the way a crowd says "crucify"—with relish, and with no interest in what happens afterward except the satisfaction of the sentence.

The command is not *stop seeing*. It is *stop feeding on what you see*. And the proof that you have stopped feeding is whether you can see the fault clearly and still forgive—which is the third clause of the verse, and its real destination.
