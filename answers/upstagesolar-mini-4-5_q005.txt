This is a rich question, and I think the honest answer is that enemy-love is difficult to take seriously because we tend to evaluate it with the wrong criteria. It gets dismissed as either sentimentality (a nice feeling for soft people) or strategy (a means to peace or social harmony), and both readings miss what the text is actually doing.

**The sentimental reading is the easiest to dismiss, and that's part of the problem.** "Love" in this context isn't the warm emotion of affection; the Greek *agapē* names a disciplined orientation toward another's good, and the command is paired with concrete actions—blessing, doing good, praying. The second half of the passage strips away any sentimentality: God's sun rises on the evil and the good, rain falls on the just and unjust. That's not a metaphor for nice feelings; it's a picture of *active, unearned benevolence* that doesn't wait for gratitude or reciprocity. What makes it hard to take seriously is precisely that it asks for something without a reward attached.

**The strategic reading is more plausible and more dangerous.** Enemy-love is genuinely useful—it de-escalates, it builds networks, it's often good for institutions. But when we make it strategic, we've already defeated it: the command becomes a tactic contingent on payoff, and the moment it stops paying, it evaporates. The verse's own logic resists this, because the model of love is God's indiscriminate sun and rain, which doesn't select for deservingness.

**I'd argue the deeper category is moral strength—but with a caveat.** Enemy-love is hard because it requires several costly capacities at once: the will to act against one's own interest (which looks like weakness to a culture that conflates strength with retribution), the capacity to imagine and wish well someone who has wounded you, and the inner freedom not to be defined by the enemy's actions. It's a *form* of strength—what some call a strength of the heart—but it's unusual strength, the kind that looks like vulnerability from outside. The reason it's hard to take seriously is that we've built a moral vocabulary where "strength" means standing firm against an enemy, so love-as-strength sounds like a contradiction. It isn't; it's just a different axis of strength.

**And there's a further difficulty that goes to the text's own honesty.** The passage grounds this in something almost startling: the love we're commanded to extend mirrors a divine generosity that is, by design, not tied to desert. That's either the most demanding moral claim in the tradition or the most dangerous—because it can become an infinite obligation with no boundary, or it can be romanticized into passivity before abusers. The tradition has always had to work at that boundary, and the fact that it keeps having to means the command isn't sentimental; it's a demand that keeps exposing the difference between a nice feeling and a costly practice.

So: not sentiment, not strategy—costly practice. It's hard to take seriously because it asks us to measure ourselves by a standard of unearned good that no reciprocity can meet, and to call that strength rather than foolishness.
