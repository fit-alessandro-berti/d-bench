The passage does not ask for a “better mix” because the worship it describes is **not merely insufficient—it is fraudulent by design**. Amos is not complaining that the Israelites have too much singing and too little charity; he is saying that their religious life is a **deliberate substitute for covenant justice**. God “hates” and “despises” it because it is being used to purchase divine tolerance while the community systematically crushes the poor.

Here is the fraud exposed, and why the rejection is absolute:

### 1. Ritual as camouflage for oppression
In the context of Amos 5, the people are still “seeking the Lord” (v. 6), holding festivals, offering burnt and grain offerings, and bringing “fat beasts” (the best livestock) for peace offerings. Yet just verses earlier Amos says they “turn justice to wormwood” (v. 7), “hate the one who rebukes at the gate” (v. 10), “take bribes,” and “trample on the poor” (v. 11). Their worship is **parallel to, and therefore a cover for, economic violence**. They do not need a balance; they need an **exposure**: God refuses the smell of their offerings because they are offered by hands covered in the injustice of the covenant.

### 2. Worship turned into a transactional commodity
The sharp language—“I will not smell,” “I will not accept,” “I will not hear”—shows that God is not a consumer of religious goods who can be pacified by quality. The Israelites have treated the cult as **a mechanism of appeasement**: “We bring fat animals and beautiful music, so God must be pleased and protect us.” This is spiritual fraud because it severs the link between ritual and moral covenant fidelity. The prophets consistently say that sacrifice without justice is **abomination** (Isaiah 1:11–17; Micah 6:6–8). Amos makes it visceral: God does not want the aroma; he wants the righteousness.

### 3. Aesthetic excess masking ethical emptiness
The “noise of your songs” and “melody of your viols” are not condemned because music is bad, but because they are used to **manufacture emotional satisfaction** that replaces ethical action. The worship is loud, beautiful, and expensive (fat beasts, elaborate assemblies), which makes the fraud more dangerous: it creates the **illusion of piety**. It allows the wealthy to feel holy while they exploit the poor. God’s refusal to hear the music means that **religious feeling cannot substitute for justice**.

### 4. The covenant context: Justice is not optional
Amos 5 is framed by a call to “seek the Lord and live” (v. 6) and ends with the famous demand: “Let judgment run down as waters, and righteousness as a mighty stream” (v. 24). Justice is not a secondary pillar to be balanced with worship; in the covenant, it is **the very substance of the relationship**. When Israel divorces the two, worship becomes idolatrous—an idol of self-justification. That is why Amos does not say, “Worship less”; he says, **remove this worship from my sight** until justice flows as a flood.

### The moral/spiritual fraud summarized
The fraud is **hypocritical covenantalism**: using religious observance to legitimize a society of oppression, to buy God’s protection for wealth and power, and to soothe the conscience of the exploiters. It is the belief that **ritual performance equals moral standing**, that God cares about the quality of the sacrifice more than the quality of the society. Amos exposes it by reversing the logic: **worship is only acceptable when it flows from a people who have made justice “roll down” as an unstoppable force.** Until then, God will not smell it—not because he is harsh, but because to accept it would be to endorse the fraud.
