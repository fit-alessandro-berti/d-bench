### What makes the image so sharp?

Jesus uses a **deliberately disproportionate contrast**: a tiny speck in another person’s eye is glaringly visible, while a large beam in one’s own eye goes unnoticed. The issue is not literally eyesight but **moral self-deception**—we readily magnify someone else’s fault while minimizing our own.

The word “hypocrite” reinforces this attack. Its Greek meaning is “actor,” someone who plays a role while concealing the truth. Religious words and outward gestures become especially hollow when they conceal an unexamined life. The beam makes that contradiction impossible to overlook: before offering to remove your brother’s speck, you must acknowledge the enormous obstruction in your own vision.

### Why does it not forbid moral correction?

The passage ends by explicitly imagining correction: “then shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote out of thy brother’s eye.” Jesus does **not** withdraw the offer to help. He challenges the self-blindness and inconsistency that make that help hypocritical.

“The first” establishes an order of **moral humility**. Before confidently correcting someone else, you must examine yourself. This does not require becoming sinless first; it means that your own failures cannot be used as grounds for treating someone else’s failures as trivial. Correction offered from humility is fundamentally different from condemnation offered from a claim of moral superiority.

The target, then, is **censorious, self-righteous judgment—not moral discernment or correction itself**. Jesus calls people to heal their own moral vision so that they may help others clearly and constructively. The standard is not “never point out a fault,” but “do not condemn from a blindness you refuse to recognize in yourself.”
