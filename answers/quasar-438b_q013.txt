The passage rejects worship sharply because it is attacking the very thing that worship, in this form, tries to do: **substitute outward ceremony for inward justice**.

A “balanced” answer would say: worship is sometimes good, but it can become hollow if overused. That is not this passage’s point. The speaker does not want better rituals, finer feasts, more solemn assemblies, richer offerings, sweeter songs, or more moderate ceremonies. He refuses the entire economy of ritualized piety:

- **Feast days** — public celebration of moral truth.
- **Solemn assemblies** — gatherings meant to look like real judgment.
- **Burnt offerings and meat offerings** — offerings given to a person as if devotion could be consumed like food.
- **Peace offerings of fat beasts** — the offerings of those who are prosperous, complacent, and materially rich, as if wealth pacifies moral demand.
- **Songs and viols** — music, chant, ritual chant’s aesthetic appeal.
- **Noise** — the noise of praise and ceremony.

What is being exposed is the fraud of **conversion by ceremony**: the belief that righteousness can be produced, bought, sung, eaten, offered, or performed into existence. The rejected worship is not worship as sincere inward obedience, but worship as theatrical religion — religion made of spectacle, consumption, gifts, songs, festivals, and assemblies. The danger is that such worship lets righteousness appear to be present when only its external signs are present.

That is why the final image matters:

> “But let judgment run down as waters, and righteousness as a mighty stream.”

Judgment is not something handed over at a feast. It must flow down, like water, into the low places, into houses, streets, lives, institutions, ordinary days. Righteousness is not the feast; it is the stream that must keep running. If worship becomes a feast day, righteousness becomes occasional and decorative. If judgment must “run down,” then it cannot remain aloft with priests, magistrates, officials, or rich patrons passing outward offerings. It must descend into ordinary conduct.

So the moral fraud is: **ceremonial righteousness without flowing righteousness**. The rich, the celebratory, and the solemn can appear magnificent while leaving the world morally dry beneath them. They bring offerings, not judgment; songs, not obedience; fat beasts, not purified hearts. The passage refuses to be nourished by such offerings because accepting them would legitimate the false belief that righteousness has already arrived.

In short: worship is rejected because it is being used as payment for a righteousness that has not actually run down into the world. The exposed fraud is the worshiper’s self-serving illusion that ceremony, wealth, and aesthetic reverence can discharge the obligation of real justice.
