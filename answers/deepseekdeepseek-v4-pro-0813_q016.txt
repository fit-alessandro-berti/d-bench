The elder brother’s deepest problem is best described as **discomfort with mercy itself**—or more fully, a self-righteousness that cannot bear grace.

His anger, envy, wounded sense of fairness, and pride in obedience are all real, but they are symptoms of this deeper root. Notice how he speaks:

> “Lo, these many years do I serve thee, neither transgressed I at any time thy commandment: and yet thou never gavest me a kid…”

He sees his relationship with the father as **transactional**: obedience should earn reward. He says “I serve thee,” like a servant or slave, not “I have been with thee,” like a son. He also says “this thy son,” not “my brother,” revealing how detached he is from the relationship.

The father’s reply exposes the real issue:

> “Son, thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine.”

The elder brother already had everything. He had full access to the father’s love and possessions. But he did not live as a son enjoying the father’s presence; he lived as a worker keeping score. Therefore, when the father shows mercy to the returning prodigal, it offends the elder brother’s entire system of deserving and earning.

His deepest problem is not that he is too obedient, but that he is **offended by grace**. He would rather see his brother punished than welcomed. He cannot rejoice with the father because he does not share the father’s heart of mercy.

In context, Jesus is addressing the Pharisees and scribes who complained that He received sinners (Luke 15:1–2). The elder brother represents that same spirit: a religious correctness that is more disturbed by mercy than by sin. So the deepest problem is **discomfort with mercy itself**, rooted in pride and a failure to know the father’s generous, gracious heart.
