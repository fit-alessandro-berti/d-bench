Micah 6 is a courtroom. God summons the mountains as jury, indicts Israel, and recites His case: I brought you up out of Egypt, redeemed you, sent Moses, Aaron, and Miriam. Israel's imagined reply is revealing—it goes shopping. Burnt offerings? Calves a year old? Thousands of rams? Ten thousand rivers of oil? My firstborn for my transgression? The logic of the questions is arithmetic: guilt is a debt, somewhere there is a price, and the only question is how high. Verse 8 refuses the arithmetic. What the Lord requires has no price, no quantity—and this is the beginning of its severity—no substitute.

## Why brevity is the severity

Long codes are comfortable. Complexity offers hiding places: under six hundred commandments you can specialize, find loopholes, be scrupulous here and asleep there. The rabbinic tradition itself noticed what distillation does. In Makkot 24a, R. Simlai traces the reduction: Moses gave 613 commandments; David reduced them to eleven; Isaiah to six; Micah to three. Each reduction is a judgment. You can hide behind 613; you cannot hide behind three verbs.

And the verbs are chosen to close every exit. **Do** justice—an outward act, verifiable in courts and marketplaces. **Love** mercy—an affection, not directly controllable; you can perform mercy grudgingly, but you cannot pretend to love it for long. **Walk** humbly—a habit, continuous; a burnt offering can be finished, a walk cannot. Action, affection, disposition: nothing about a person is left out, and nothing in the list can be delegated. There is no professional class of the just; you cannot pay a priest to love mercy on your behalf. The sacrificial question—*how much?*—assumed atonement could be outsourced. The answer names a self, not a transaction.

Two more closures. "He hath shewed thee" removes the excuse of obscurity—Micah is echoing Deuteronomy 10, where the identical question ("what doth the Lord thy God require of thee?") is answered with walking and loving, followed immediately by the God who "executeth the judgment of the fatherless and widow, and loveth the stranger." The knowledge was never the problem. And the address is singular—"O man." The trial of a nation lands on one face. You cannot blame the kings, the priests, or the system.

## Justice and mercy: each other's truth-test

The three demands are not a heap; they are engineered to check each other.

Justice without mercy hardens into severity: persons become ledgers, deserts without remainder, procedure with a closed heart—cruelty wearing the robes of principle. Mercy corrects this not by contradicting justice but by insisting that no one is the sum of their record; that the end of justice is restoration, not mere distribution; that the judge who forgets he might have been the accused will eventually enjoy sentencing. Mercy keeps justice from eating the very people it was meant to feed.

Mercy without justice dissolves into sentimentality: forgiveness of what was never ours to forgive, mercy for the strong that leaves the weak exactly where they were—what Bonhoeffer called cheap grace. Micah's own context sharpens this. This is a chapter about "wicked balances" and "the bag of deceitful weights" (6:11), about rich men "full of violence" (6:12), about women cast out of their pleasant houses (2:9). Here mercy is not a feeling but restitution—honest scales, returned fields, protected widows. Justice is mercy's truth-test: mercy that repairs nothing is only a mood. Martin Luther King Jr. compressed the mutual correction into one sentence: "Power at its best is love implementing the demands of justice, and justice at its best is power correcting everything that stands against love." Justice without love is abuse; love without justice is anemia.

## Humility: the correction of the corrections

But the sharpest demand is the third word, because pride is the corruption that ruins both other virtues precisely at the moment of their apparent success.

Justice is proudest when it is most right: the elder brother, the Pharisee thanking God he is not as other men are. Mercy is proudest when it is most generous: conspicuous compassion, pity that performs itself, forgiveness that keeps a lien on the forgiven. Humility corrects both by attacking the self-congratulation inside them. It makes justice *teachable*—my account of what is owed is partial and often self-interested, so hold the sword loosely. Lincoln's second inaugural is the whole triad in statecraft: "with firmness in the right, *as God gives us to see the right*... with malice toward none, with charity for all"—justice under explicit epistemic humility, joined to charity. Humility makes mercy *reciprocal*: the one who shows mercy is also a debtor; you forgive as one who has been forgiven, or your mercy condescends.

But humility has its own failure mode, and here justice and mercy return the favor. Humility without them curdles into abjection, false modesty, the "who am I to judge?" that functions as an alibi for doing nothing while the evicted woman stands in the street. The verse says *walk* humbly—and walking is movement, direction, engagement. Nor is it kneeling *before* God but walking *with* Him: the posture of Enoch, companionship rather than groveling. Paradoxically, the humble can act more boldly than the proud, because they do not need to be the hero of the justice they do or the patron of the mercy they show. Humility frees action from self-display; action keeps humility from silence.

Take any two without the third and a recognizable vice appears. Justice and mercy without humility: the moral vanity of the reformer who builds a better world with a statue to himself at the center. Justice and humility without mercy: the severe judge—rigorous, incorruptible, and loveless. Mercy and humility without justice: the sentimentalist who deplores suffering and returns no one's house. The three are not a list but a stool; each leg bears the others. This is perhaps the deepest source of the demand: the verse corners you at your own strength. The naturally just person is ambushed by "love mercy." The naturally tender person is ambushed by "do justly." Both are ambushed by "walk humbly."

## A portrait, not a price

One final reason the summary is inexhaustible: it is not a human norm. Micah's book closes by returning to the divine character: "Who is a God like unto thee, that pardoneth iniquity... he retaineth not his anger for ever, *because he delighteth in mercy*" (7:18). The word translated "mercy" there—*chesed*—is the same word we are commanded to love. What is required of us is a portrait of what God is. That is why the standard can be neither lowered nor completed. Jesus kept the triad and called the petty exactitudes of religion—mint, anise, cumin—"the weightier matters of the law: judgment, mercy, and faith" (Matthew 23:23): the small disciplines are not wrong, but they weigh nothing against the three verbs.

So the question that opens the passage—how much will it cost?—receives its terrible, kind answer: everything, and not as a payment but as a self. The verse is short enough to memorize and long enough to spend a life on. It sounds like a summary. It is a mirror.
