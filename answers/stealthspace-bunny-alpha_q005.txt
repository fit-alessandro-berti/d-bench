**Enemy-love is not merely sentimentality or strategy. It is a costly form of moral strength—concrete goodness pursued without the usual guarantees of safety, reciprocity, or reward.** But taking it seriously means recognizing just how demanding it is.

### Why it is difficult to take seriously

It is easy to endorse love in principle—until it touches our anger, fear, or dignity. We readily say, “Love your neighbor,” while quietly exempting the person who has betrayed us, threatened us, or spoken against us.

Calling someone an *enemy* can also function as permission to stop seeing them as human. Jesus’ command rejects that exemption. It does **not** deny that they have done serious evil, or that we may need protection and justice. It refuses to let their wrongdoing determine the limits of our goodness.

### Is it sentimental?

Not if “love” means a warm feeling we can summon whenever we choose.

Here, love involves the will: refusing revenge, blessing rather than cursing, doing good where possible, and praying for someone we cannot presently love. Feelings may follow those actions—or they may not. The command is not satisfied by saying kind words while preserving hatred underneath.

The test is not, *Do I feel affectionate toward this person?* It is: *Will I seek their good, and will I refuse to make their evil my governing purpose?*

### Is it strategic?

There is certainly a **formative** dimension: repeated practices shape our character. But enemy-love is not primarily a technique for gaining favor, improving relationships, or earning God’s approval.

The passage gives a deeper reason: “That ye may be the children of your Father which is in heaven.” The standard is not merely human niceness, or even getting along. It is becoming like a God whose goodness is not limited to those who deserve it.

A strategic reading would ask, *How can I benefit from this?* Jesus’ teaching asks, *What kind of person am I becoming?* Enemy-love is meant to interrupt the cycle in which one’s enemies determine one’s conduct.

### Is it a deeper form of moral strength?

Yes—but “strength” here does not mean the absence of struggle. It means **the willingness to act rightly when resentment offers an easier and more satisfying course**.

It may require more moral strength to bless someone than to condemn them. Condemnation can cooperate with our anger and reassure us of our own goodness. Blessing says, “I will not allow what this person did to me to turn me into the thing I despise.”

For Christians, this is not simply heroic self-control. It is grace-enabled resemblance to God: a dependence on his goodness rather than confidence in one’s own ability to overcome hatred.

### How do we take it seriously?

Start with its concrete demands:

- Refuse retaliation and degrading speech.
- Do not exploit, harm, or abandon someone to “win.”
- Where safely possible, offer practical good.
- Pray for their deliverance from evil and transformation—not necessarily for their current harmful behavior.
- Pursue protection and justice when love does not require pretending danger is harmless.

We can begin with an honest prayer: **“God, I do not know how to love this person. Stop me from being governed by hatred, and show me what good I can do.”**

Enemy-love is therefore neither a sentimental ideal nor a religious transaction. It is a claim upon the whole life: goodness must extend beyond the boundaries we would naturally draw. Its seriousness lies not in how noble the words sound, but in whether we will live them—even when love costs us, and even when the enemy never repays.
