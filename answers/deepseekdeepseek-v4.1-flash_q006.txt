Jesus is not commanding the erasure of the created self. The “self” he tells us to deny is the self as lord—the fallen, self-enclosed ego that lives by self-preservation, self-justification, self-rule, and self-glorification. In Mark 8:35 he defines it: the self that wants to “save its life” at any cost. The Greek word for “deny” (*aparneomai*) is the same word used for Peter denying Jesus—to disown, repudiate, say “I do not belong to you.” So to deny yourself is to say to the old master, “I am no longer yours; I belong to Christ.”

So the self denied is:
- not the self as God’s good creation,
- not the body, personality, gifts, or legitimate needs,
- not human dignity or God-given identity,
- but the “old self” (Rom. 6:6; Eph. 4:22; Col. 3:9): the self curved in on itself, enslaved to sin, pretending to be autonomous, trying to save itself, and resisting the cross.

“Take up his cross” does not mean seeking abuse, glorifying suffering, or hating yourself. In the Roman world, the cross was an instrument of execution. Jesus means a voluntary readiness to identify with him, to die to self-rule, and if necessary to suffer for the gospel. The command is not “destroy your humanity” but “dethrone your ego and follow me.”

**Why it sounds liberating to some:**  
It frees from the impossible burden of being your own savior. It releases from the tyranny of self-image, comparison, approval-seeking, and the fear of death. It says your identity is not something you must manufacture; it is received in Christ. You can stop using God and others to secure yourself. You can lose your life for something worth dying for. The paradox is that the self is not ultimately lost but found in a new way: “I have been crucified with Christ; it is no longer I who live, but Christ lives in me” (Gal. 2:20).

**Why it sounds offensive to others:**  
It contradicts the modern creed of autonomy, self-expression, and self-fulfillment. “Deny yourself” sounds like self-hatred or cultic control; “take up your cross” sounds morbid or oppressive. Jesus also says “for my sake and the gospel’s”—an exclusive claim to ultimate allegiance. That offends pluralism and the sovereign self. It tells us we cannot save ourselves; we must surrender. And because abusive people have sometimes misused this language to keep victims passive, it can be especially offensive—rightly so when misused. But the gospel’s self-denial is not submitting to abuse; it is voluntarily following the crucified Lord in love and truth. It denies the sinful self, not human dignity.

The self denied is the self that wants to be God. The self saved is the self restored under God. To some it sounds like death because it is death—death to the old master. To others it sounds like life because it is the way to the only life that cannot be lost.
