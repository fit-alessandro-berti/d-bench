**In this passage, victory is not making an enemy lose; it is refusing to let an enemy’s wrongdoing determine how you act.** Paul makes that refusal concrete: if the person who harmed you is hungry, feed him. You neither deny the harm nor repay it. You do something genuinely good for the person who did it.

The phrase “heap coals of fire on his head” can sound like a promise of revenge by subtler means. One traditional reading is that unexpected kindness may bring the enemy to a painful recognition of what he has done. But that is a possible *effect* of kindness, not its *purpose*. Paul has just told his readers not to avenge themselves. Feeding someone in order to humiliate him would preserve the very motive Paul asks them to relinquish.

The command is **genuinely strong**. It does not require calling evil good or allowing harm to continue. It requires enough self-command to see an enemy’s hunger as a real need rather than a deserved punishment. Whether or not the enemy repents, his cruelty has not made you cruel. That is the victory Paul describes.
