The posture required is **gentle, humble, restorative authority**, not the authority of a judge. “Overtaken” points to a person caught in a weakness or offense, not a verdict against a character. Correction is therefore meant to **mend and reintegrate** the erring person—“restore” them—rather than to humiliate, expose, or use them as a warning against the rest. The one called “spiritual” is not a superior who has escaped temptation; the phrase is directed toward mature believers who are to exercise speech and correction the way Christ and the Spirit do: with humility rather than domination.

Meekness and self-suspicion are kept together with restoration because they control the same failure modes. Restoration supplies the **purpose**—recovery—of correction. Meekness supplies the **manner**—graceful, low-burdened speech that does not turn correction into shame, domination, or self-exaltation. Self-suspicion supplies the **epistemic and ethical guard**: “considering thyself, lest thou also be tempted” means the corrector must not stand outside the problem, must not mistake maturity for moral exemption, and must not use spirituality as a sword. The command is therefore not: “be firm in judgment.” It is: “be careful not to become the person who enjoys exposing another’s fault.”

The point is not weakness, evasion, or sentimentality. It is a form of trust in the same vulnerability that applies to everyone. Correction is real and can be firm, but it must be non-triumphal: truth without cruelty, a man convicted toward repentance rather than wounded toward shame. In that way restoration, meekness, and self-suspicion are one ethic: **truth toward another, humility in the manner, and honesty about oneself**, so that the spiritual person does not become a new kind of judge over the fallen.
