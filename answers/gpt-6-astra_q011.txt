The movement from **“cease to do evil”** to **“learn to do well”** makes repentance more than stopping wrongdoing. To cease doing evil is necessary, but it is not the whole of goodness: hands that no longer harm must learn how to help.

The word **“learn”** matters. It presents goodness as something cultivated—through instruction, correction, discernment, and repeated action. Turning away from evil does not automatically teach a person how to recognize another’s need or act justly. Those capacities must be formed.

Isaiah then makes “doing well” concrete: **seek justice, relieve the oppressed, defend the fatherless, plead for the widow.** These are not merely private feelings of kindness. They involve intervention and advocacy, especially for people who lack protection or social power. Here, “judge the fatherless” means securing justice for them, not condemning them.

In the surrounding passage, God rejects worship that coexists with bloodshed and injustice. The call to “wash” therefore demands more than ritual cleanliness: it calls for a changed way of living with others.

So goodness is **not mere restraint but a practiced form of love expressed through justice**. Restraint asks, “What harm must I stop doing?” Isaiah presses further: “Whose cause must I take up, and what good must I learn to do?”
