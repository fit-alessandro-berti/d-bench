Romans 12:20–21 is the climax of a larger call to non-retaliation: “Repay no one evil for evil” (v. 17), “never avenge yourselves” (v. 19), and “do not be overcome by evil, but overcome evil with good” (v. 21). So the passage defines victory over evil not as defeating or humiliating the enemy, but as refusing to let evil set the terms of your response. Evil is “overcome” when its cycle is broken by concrete, active goodness.

The command is therefore not fundamentally **weak** or **manipulative**—though it can be twisted into either.

- **Not weak:** “Feed him… give him drink” is active, practical love. It is not mere passivity or approval of evil. It names the enemy as enemy, yet calls for costly goodness rather than revenge. It requires self-control, courage, and trust in God’s justice.
- **Not manipulative in itself:** The motive is not to control or punish the enemy through fake kindness. The passage entrusts judgment to God (“Vengeance is mine,” v. 19) and calls for genuine good. “Coals of fire” likely suggests the enemy’s conscience being awakened, shame leading to repentance, or leaving the outcome to God’s judgment. But kindness is not to become a covert weapon of humiliation.
- **Genuinely strong:** It overcomes evil by refusing to be conquered by it. The person who returns evil for evil is still being shaped by the enemy’s evil. The person who does good instead demonstrates a stronger loyalty—to God, to love, and to the enemy’s true good.

So Romans 12:21 defines victory as **moral and spiritual victory over evil itself**, not personal triumph over the enemy. It is strong because it is costly, non-retaliatory, active in doing good, and trusts God with the outcome. It is weak only if “good” is reduced to passivity, avoidance of truth, or allowing abuse without boundaries; but that is not what this passage commands. It commands real goodness in the face of real evil—good that refuses to become evil in return.
