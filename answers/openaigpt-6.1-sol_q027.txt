**The central fault is self-righteousness: the Pharisee trusts in his own righteousness, and that trust expresses itself in comparison and contempt.** His virtues become evidence for a favorable verdict he has effectively already pronounced on himself.

Luke gives the interpretive key before the prayer begins: Jesus addresses those who **“trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and despised others.”** Both halves matter.

### What is wrong with the Pharisee’s prayer?

- **Not necessarily false doctrine.** Jesus does not here accuse him of holding an incorrect creed. Nor does the story say that his fasting and tithing are fraudulent. His moral claims may be substantially true.
- **Not virtue itself.** Fasting, generosity, and avoiding injustice are not condemned. The problem is what he does with those goods: he makes them grounds for confidence in himself and superiority over another person.
- **Pride, enacted through comparison.** “I am not as other men are” establishes his standing by contrast. The tax collector becomes a convenient example of what he believes himself to be above.
- **Contempt.** “Even as this publican” is especially revealing. He does not merely acknowledge differences in conduct; he reduces the other worshipper to a despised category.
- **Self-display, but not necessarily a performance for an audience.** The text does not require us to imagine that he is speaking loudly to impress bystanders. The deeper problem is that even his address to God becomes a presentation of his own excellence.

His prayer begins with thanksgiving, which could be genuine and appropriate. But its substance is a moral résumé. **He asks for nothing because he appears to recognize no need for mercy.** God is addressed, yet the Pharisee’s attention remains fixed on himself and the inferior people against whom he measures himself.

### Why is the tax collector’s posture spiritually truer?

Because it corresponds to his actual position before God: **he needs mercy and cannot supply his own justification.**

Standing at a distance, lowering his eyes, and beating his breast express that recognition. But those gestures are not a technique that earns acceptance. The decisive element is his appeal:

> “God be merciful to me a sinner.”

He offers neither an excuse nor a comparison. He does not say, “At least I am better than someone else,” or even, “At least I am humbler than that Pharisee.” He entrusts his case to God’s mercy.

The word translated “be merciful” can carry the sense of making atonement or dealing mercifully with sin; in the temple setting, that resonance is significant. His request concerns not merely feeling better about himself, but God dealing with his guilt.

### The decisive reversal

Jesus says the tax collector goes home **“justified”**: accepted as righteous by God. That is not the same as saying his previous conduct was righteous, or that tax collectors were morally superior to Pharisees.

Nor does the passage explicitly pronounce the Pharisee’s final, eternal fate. It contrasts their standing before God in this encounter.

**The Pharisee brings his righteousness as a claim; the tax collector brings his sin as a plea.** The latter is spiritually truer because he acknowledges dependence on mercy. The danger is not having virtues, but turning them into a reason that one no longer needs mercy—and that others no longer deserve regard.
