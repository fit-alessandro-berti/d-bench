Paul defines victory over evil as **the refusal to let evil set the terms of your response**.

In Romans 12:20–21, evil is not overcome primarily by destroying the enemy, humiliating them, or matching their wrongdoing. It is overcome by **interrupting the cycle of retaliation**: feeding the hungry enemy, giving drink to the thirsty enemy, and doing good where vengeance would feel justified. The “victory” is that evil no longer gets to reproduce itself through another act of hatred.

So the passage is not saying, “Avoid conflict and hope evil goes away.” It commands costly, active goodwill.

### How should we read “coals of fire”?

The phrase “heap coals of fire on his head” can sound manipulative or vindictive, as if kindness were a weapon. But most serious readings take it as an image of **shame, conviction, or remorse**—the kind that can lead the enemy to recognize the evil of their conduct. The good deed exposes the ugliness of their hostility. That is not the same as deliberately trying to burn or shame them into submission.

The logic of the passage is closer to this:

> Your enemy wants you to become like them—angry, vengeful, cruel.  
> You refuse. You feed them instead.  
> In doing so, evil loses its power over you and may also lose its hold on them.

### Is the command weak?

It can look weak by the world’s standards, because it renounces retaliation and refuses to use force. But it is not passive or cowardly.

Feeding an enemy is often harder than striking them. Releasing vengeance to God requires more discipline than nursing resentment. This is a strength that comes from moral confidence and trust in God’s justice, not from fear.

In that sense, the command is **genuinely strong**. It takes strength to absorb wrong without becoming wrong. It takes courage to break the natural human instinct to repay evil with evil.

### Is it manipulative?

It becomes manipulative if someone does good merely to make another person feel guilty, to gain leverage, or to enjoy their shame. That would not be the love Paul describes.

Biblical enemy-love is not a technique for controlling people. It is a way of acting that commits the outcome to God. The enemy may repent, but the command stands whether they do or not.

### Summary

Paul’s definition of victory is this:  
**Evil is defeated when it fails to turn you into an evildoer and when it is met with good rather than repaid with evil.**

It is not weakness. It is not manipulation when rightly practiced. It is a costly, freeing, and morally powerful form of strength—one that leaves final judgment to God while refusing to let evil have the last word in your own heart.
