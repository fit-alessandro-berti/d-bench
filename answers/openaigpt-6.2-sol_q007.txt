Jesus is not saying that nothing is right or wrong. He is asking what we do once we see that something is wrong. **Discernment names the wrong so we can respond to it; the appetite to condemn uses the wrong as a reason to write someone off.**

The image of the speck and the plank later in Luke 6 makes the distinction concrete. Jesus does not say the speck in your neighbor’s eye is imaginary. He says to take the plank out of your own eye *first*, so that you can see clearly enough to help. The aim is not to stop seeing faults but to see them without the distortion of self-righteousness. There is a difference between wanting your neighbor to see better and wanting everyone to see how much better *you* are.

That distinction matters when real harm has been done. You can say, “What you did was wrong, and I won’t let it continue,” without saying, “What you did is all you will ever be.” Forgiveness does not mean denying the harm, dropping every consequence, or restoring trust before it is warranted.

“Forgive, and ye shall be forgiven” reminds the person making the judgment that they, too, stand in need of mercy. A useful question is: *Am I naming this wrong to protect someone, tell the truth, or make repair possible—or because I enjoy having someone to look down on?*
