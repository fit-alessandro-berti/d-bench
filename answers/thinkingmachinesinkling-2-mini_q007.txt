Luke 6:37 is easy to flatten into “don’t ever judge.” That reading misses the verse’s texture. In context, Jesus is speaking to disciples about posture, disposition, and practice—not about the elimination of moral assessment.

A helpful distinction is:

**Discernment** = knowing what is true and good versus false and harmful, in order to act wisely and faithfully.  
**Appetite to condemn** = the desire to declare someone guilty, expose their failure, or make them feel inferior—often to protect self-pride, in-group identity, or moral superiority.

The first can be a gift. The second can become a habit, and often a form of retaliation, control, or avoidance of hard truths about oneself.

The verse is not saying that truth is irrelevant. Christians can and should discern: whether a teaching is false, whether an action is harmful, whether a person is being treated with cruelty, whether one’s own words are feeding gossip or mercy. Scripture itself presents discernment as part of faith. To “prove all things” and to test spirits are commands, not license for relativism. So moral judgment matters: in choosing what to trust, what to reject, how to love, how to counsel, how to hold others accountable, and how to care for victims and the vulnerable.

But the kind of judgment Jesus warns against is not merely epistemic. It is a *lordly* and *therapeutically barren* way of knowing. It likes being right. It enjoys the gap between “us” and “them.” It turns moral insight into status: “I can see clearly; you are blind. I am righteous; you are not.” It narrows others into categories rather than seeing their needs, their wounds, their possibilities. It is less interested in repentance than in verdict.

That is why Jesus ties judging to condemnation: “judge not, and ye shall not be judged: condemn not, and ye shall not be condemned.” The issue is not only whether you can judge, but what happens to you when you do. Harsh judgment becomes a self-sealing structure: it hardens the judger, excuses the unexamined self, and tempts the justified into pride. Jesus’ point is that mercy and proportionality are the conditions under which moral life becomes livable.

A practical question is:

> Is this judgment aimed at truth, or mainly at status?

Some clues:
- **Discernment** is based on evidence, is proportionate, seeks truth, leaves doors open, and is willing to be corrected.
- **Appetite to condemn** is pleasure in exposure, is driven by humiliation or superiority, punishes rather than restores, and rarely risks judging itself.

This does not mean every “that’s wrong” must be softened into nothing. A false teaching must be named. A harmful action must be addressed. Truth is not measured by comfort. But even when truth is spoken, its aim is not contempt. The gospel-shaped judgment says: “I see that this is wrong; I also see that you are capable of change, and I am not allowed to treat you as beneath care.”

So the difference is not **judgment vs. no judgment**. It is **discernment vs. self-consuming condemnation**. Luke 6:37 is not an anti-truth verse. It is a call for discernment to be held inside love, humility, and mercy rather than detached from them—especially when judgment threatens to become a mask for pride.
