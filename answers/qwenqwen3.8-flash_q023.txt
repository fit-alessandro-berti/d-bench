In Isaiah 55:6–7, repentance involves both **“way”** and **“thoughts”** because biblical repentance is not merely a change of behavior; it is a change of the whole person—**outer action and inner intention**.

The verse says:

> “Let the wicked forsake his way,  
> and the unrighteous man his thoughts.”

In Hebrew, “way” (*derek*) refers to conduct, lifestyle, habit, direction—what a person does. “Thoughts” (*machashavot*) refers not simply to fleeting ideas, but to plans, purposes, imaginations, intentions, and the inner disposition that shapes one’s life. In other words, God calls for repentance of both the **external path** and the **internal heart**.

### 1. Outward conduct: forsake the “way”

The “way” represents visible behavior: practices, relationships, habits, decisions, and patterns of living. The wicked are not merely people who have bad thoughts; they are people who walk in a wrong direction. Repentance requires turning away from actual sin. One cannot claim devotion to God while continuing knowingly in injustice, idolatry, violence, exploitation, or impurity without change.

So outward conduct matters. Ethics cannot be separated from faith.

But Isaiah does not stop there.

### 2. Inward imagination: forsake the “thoughts”

The “thoughts” represent the inner world from which the outer life flows. A person may temporarily change behavior while the heart still desires, schemes, fantasizes, rationalizes, or plans sin. Biblical repentance goes deeper than visible reform; it confronts the imagination.

The “thoughts” can include:

- selfish ambitions,
- resentful imaginations,
- lustful fantasies,
- prideful reasoning,
- plans to manipulate or harm others,
- inner rebellion disguised as outward compliance.

This implies that God sees not only what we do but what we intend. Human judgment often looks at actions; divine mercy confronts the heart.

### 3. True repentance unifies the outer and inner life

The pairing of “way” and “thoughts” forms a kind of complete picture of human life:

- outward walk,
- inward purpose.

If repentance only changes the way, it can become hypocrisy: external morality without inner transformation.

If repentance only claims to change thoughts, it can become sentimentality: inner excitement or self-approval without ethical fruit.

Isaiah demands that both be forsaken. The wicked must turn in behavior and in heart.

### 4. God’s mercy is deeper than human sinfulness

The second half of the verse gives the hope:

> “and let him return unto the Lord, and he will have mercy upon him;  
> and to our God, for he will abundantly pardon.”

The fact that repentance includes thoughts shows the seriousness of sin: even our imaginations can be twisted. But it also shows the generosity of mercy. God does not require sinners to clean up their outer behavior before approaching Him. He calls them to return now, while He is near, and promises that He will have mercy.

“Abundantly pardon” in Hebrew can mean “to pardon abundantly” or “to multiply pardon.” It suggests that God’s forgiveness is not reluctant, not stingy, not merely sufficient. It overflows.

So the implication is both convicting and hopeful:

- Sin is not only outward; it reaches into thought and desire.
- Therefore repentance cannot be merely external.
- But God is not only judging; He is abundantly merciful.

### Summary

Repentance in Isaiah 55:7 involves both **way** and **thoughts** because God demands the repentance of the whole person. Outward conduct must change, but inward imagination and intention must also be surrendered to Him. This shows that sin is comprehensive, yet mercy is more comprehensive still. The wicked may turn from their deeds and their thoughts, and when they return to the Lord, He does not merely tolerate them; He shows mercy and pardons abundantly.
