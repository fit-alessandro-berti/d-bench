The “self” being denied is not one’s personality, humanity, or worth. In context, it is the self that insists on **preserving and governing its own life above all else**—even when following Jesus requires relinquishing security, status, or control.

“Take up his cross” gives this a sharp edge. The cross was an instrument of execution, so Jesus is describing a willingness to face profound loss, even death, rather than abandon him and the gospel. His paradox is that a life grasped as an absolute possession is lost, while a life entrusted to him is saved.

This can sound **liberating** because it releases a person from the exhausting task of making self-protection or self-advancement the highest good. It offers belonging and purpose beyond one’s own success or survival.

It can sound **offensive** because it challenges the conviction that the individual should have final authority over their own life. It also asks for allegiance to Jesus at potentially great cost. And some hear it against a history in which “self-denial” has been used to demand submission to abuse or injustice—a serious concern. The passage calls for following Jesus, not for denying one’s dignity or accepting harm as inherently good.
