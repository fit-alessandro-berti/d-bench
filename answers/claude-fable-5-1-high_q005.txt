Why enemy-love is hard to take seriously

The first thing to notice is that Jesus does not soften the enemy. The people in view curse, hate, "despitefully use," and persecute. There is no suggestion that they are misunderstood, or that they would be friends if we only saw them rightly. The command assumes a real enemy doing real harm, and it is precisely this that makes the command hard, because every instinct we have about fairness, safety, and moral seriousness pulls the other way.

Several distinct difficulties are stacked on top of one another.

1. It breaks the grammar of reciprocity. Nearly all human morality runs on some version of returning like for like: gratitude for kindness, resentment for injury, trust for trustworthiness. Reciprocity is not a vice; it is how societies hold people accountable. Enemy-love asks us to give something that has not been earned and will probably not be returned. It feels less like a higher morality than like a category error, as if someone told us to pay for goods we never received.

2. Hatred of the enemy feels like love of justice. This is the deepest difficulty. When someone has wronged us or those we love, our hostility does not feel like a moral failure; it feels like moral clarity. To relax it seems like a betrayal of the victim, a shrug at evil. So enemy-love is suspected not merely of being difficult but of being wrong: of blurring a distinction between good and evil that decent people ought to defend.

3. It looks like it rewards the wicked. If evil meets kindness, what deters it? The fear is that enemy-love is naive about power and will simply be exploited, that it turns the good into a resource for the bad.

4. It seems to command a feeling we cannot produce. We can no more make ourselves feel warmth toward a persecutor than we can make ourselves fall in love. If "love" means affection, the command is psychologically impossible and therefore easy to dismiss as pious exaggeration.

5. It has been abused. The command has often been preached by the comfortable to the oppressed: forgive your master, submit to your abuser, do not make trouble. People who have heard it used that way are right to be wary, and their wariness is part of why the saying is hard to take seriously as anything but a tool of pacification.

Because of all this, the common response is not to reject the command but to domesticate it: reduce it to "don't hold grudges" or "be civil," something manageable that no one could object to and that no longer costs anything. The verse resists this. Read as written, it is one of the most demanding things ever said about human conduct, and one of the most precise.

Is it sentimental?

No, and the text is careful to make sure it is not. The love commanded is expressed entirely in verbs of action: bless, do good, pray. Nothing is said about feelings. Agape in this passage is a policy of the will toward another person's good, not an emotion about them. That immediately dissolves the fourth difficulty: one can pray for a persecutor, refuse to slander him, and act for his welfare while still feeling grief, anger, and fear. The command does not require that the anger vanish; it requires that the anger not govern.

Sentimentality, by contrast, is the pretense that the enemy is not really an enemy, that everyone is nice underneath, that conflict is a misunderstanding. Sentimentality collapses on first contact with an actual persecutor. Jesus' command is designed for that contact. It is the least sentimental account of love imaginable: love with the illusions removed, aimed at someone you have every reason to hate, with the reasons acknowledged.

Is it strategic?

Partly, and honestly, yes. Enemy-love often works. Refusing retaliation de-escalates; it removes the justification the enemy needs for further cruelty; it can shame him, isolate him, or win him. Paul's "heap coals of fire on his head" gestures at this, and the nonviolent campaigns of Gandhi and King showed that disciplined refusal to hate can be politically formidable. Anyone who has watched a feud consume two families knows that the first person to stop returning injury is often the one who saves both.

But the passage does not ground the command in results, and the difference matters. The reason given is "that ye may be the children of your Father which is in heaven," followed by the image of sun and rain falling on the evil and the good alike. The sun is not a strategy. God is not shining on the unjust in order to reform them; he shines because that is what he is like. The command is to resemble him: to be the kind of person whose goodness is not switched on and off by the conduct of its recipient.

If enemy-love were merely strategic, it would be conditional on working, and it would stop exactly when it is most tested, when the enemy does not soften, when kindness is met with a second blow. Strategic non-retaliation is still inside the economy of reciprocity, just playing a longer game; it is still trying to win. Jesus' version is not trying to win in that sense. Its success criterion is who you become, not what the enemy does. The verses that follow make this explicit: if you love those who love you, what have you proved? Even tax collectors do that. Reciprocal love is evidence of nothing. Only love that is not a reaction reveals what a person actually is.

A deeper form of moral strength

So the third option is the right one, but it needs to be spelled out, because "strength" here means something specific.

First, enemy-love is the refusal to be controlled by the enemy. Hatred feels powerful, but it is a form of being governed: the enemy's action determines my action, his malice sets the terms of my conduct, and I become an echo of him. The sun-and-rain image is an image of freedom. God's goodness originates in himself and is not dictated by its objects. To love an enemy is to become a source rather than a reflex, to say in effect, "You do not get to decide what kind of person I am." That is not softness; it is the hardest kind of self-possession there is.

Second, enemy-love does not erase moral judgment; it depends on it. The verse itself names the evil and the unjust as evil and unjust. You cannot love an enemy without first recognizing him as one. What the command forbids is not the judgment but the reduction of the whole relationship to the judgment, the move from "he has done evil" to "he is nothing but his evil and I owe him nothing but return." Enemy-love keeps the wrong in view while refusing to let it be the last word. This is why it is compatible with resisting the enemy, telling the truth about him, refusing to trust him, even restraining him. Love is not approval, forgiveness is not reconciliation on the offender's terms, and none of it requires a victim to submit. Bonhoeffer, who took this command with total seriousness, joined a plot against Hitler; he understood that acting for a persecutor's good may sometimes mean stopping him. The command corrects the second and fifth difficulties above rather than confirming them: it is the misuse of enemy-love as forced submission that is the betrayal of the victim, not enemy-love itself.

Third, enemy-love means bearing the cost rather than passing it on. Retaliation is a transfer of pain: I was hurt, so I make you hurt, and the injury continues down the line. The person who refuses to retaliate absorbs the injury and ends the chain, and that absorption is a form of strength that only someone larger than the injury can manage. The weak cannot do this; they are wholly occupied with their wound. Enemy-love is the conduct of someone who has something the enemy cannot take, and Jesus locates that something in sonship: you can afford to give to those who give nothing back because you are already provided for by a Father whose giving is indiscriminate.

Why it should be hard

The difficulty, then, is not a design flaw. The command is hard because it is a test, the one test that reciprocal virtue cannot pass. Anyone can be good to those who are good to them; it costs nothing and proves nothing. The enemy is the only person in whose presence it becomes visible whether my goodness is mine or merely a rebate on good treatment received. That is why the command sits at the climax of this section of the Sermon on the Mount, immediately before "Be ye therefore perfect, even as your Father which is in heaven is perfect." Perfection here does not mean flawlessness; it means completeness, a love with no gaps in it, no category of person it excludes.

So: enemy-love is never sentimental, is sometimes strategic, and is essentially a form of moral strength, specifically the strength to have one's character determined from the inside rather than by the worst person one has encountered. It is hard to take seriously because taking it seriously would indict nearly everyone, and because it looks, from inside the economy of reciprocity, like a failure of justice. Seen from the other side, from the standpoint of the sun and the rain, it is the only kind of goodness that is fully one's own.
