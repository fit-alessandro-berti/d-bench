In Mark 8:34–35, Jesus is speaking right after he has told his disciples that he will suffer, be rejected, and be killed. Then he turns to the crowd and says: “If anyone wants to follow me, let him deny himself, take up his cross, and follow me.”

So what kind of self is being denied? Not the self God created. Not your personality, your gifts, your humanity, or your worth. The self being denied is the *false self*—the ego that wants to be the center, the author of your own life, the one who says, “My will, my reputation, my safety, my comfort, my identity are the most important things in the universe.”

The Greek phrase is *aparnesasthai heauton*. It literally means “to disown oneself” or “to disown one’s own self.” It’s the same verb used when Peter denies Jesus: “I don’t know him.” In that sense, denying yourself means saying to your own inner ego: “You are not the center. I am not Lord. Jesus is.”

“Taking up his cross” is not just about carrying some annoying burden. A cross was an instrument of execution. Jesus is saying that the old self-centered life must be put to death. The path of following him goes through crucifixion, not self-improvement. That sounds extreme—and it is.

Jesus intensifies it in verse 35: “Whoever will save his life shall lose it; but whoever shall lose his life for my sake and the gospel’s, the same shall save it.” The word for “life” is *psychē*, which means soul, mind, self, or whole person. The deeper point is a paradox: the more you hold onto your own life as your own project, the more you lose it. The more you give it away to Jesus and the gospel, the more you truly have it.

## Why it sounds liberating

To some people, this is profoundly freeing. That’s because the self-centered self is a tyrant. It demands constant affirmation, comparison, control, approval, and security. We exhaust ourselves trying to protect our image, preserve our comfort, and manufacture our own worth. When you deny that self, you are released from an impossible burden.

If your identity is no longer built on “myself,” it can be built on Christ. You don’t have to prove yourself anymore. You don’t have to be your own god. You don’t have to save your own life, because Jesus has already done that. What feels like loss is actually freedom from fear, from shame, from competition, and from the exhausting need to be someone.

The Christian tradition says that you have to die to find life. This is not self-hatred; it is self-emptying in order to be filled with the love of God. It is liberation from the idol of self. Jesus says in the same gospel, “My yoke is easy, and my burden is light.” The self-denial is not the sacrifice; it’s the relief.

## Why it sounds offensive

To others, this is deeply offensive—and it’s easy to understand why. Our modern culture tells us: “Be yourself. Follow your heart. Discipline yourself. You deserve to live your best life.” Jesus says: “Deny yourself. Take up your cross. Lose your life.” That sounds like the opposite of liberation. It sounds like self-erasure, psychological repression, and submission to an authority that wants you crushed.

And honestly, it has been used that way. There are people who have been told, “You need to deny yourself,” as a way to silence their needs, justify abuse, force submission to toxic authority, or shame them as worthless. That is not what Jesus means, but it is a real distortion—and it makes the teaching rightly offensive to many.

Even apart from abuse, the cross itself is offensive. Jesus is not offering self-actualization; he is offering crucifixion and resurrection. He is not saying, “I will improve your life.” He is saying, “I am your life.” That demands absolute allegiance. “For my sake and the gospel’s” makes the claim even more exclusive: the true life is found in Jesus, not in any other path. To a modern person who believes every person is their own lord, that is scandalous.

The same words can sound liberating or offensive because they cut in two directions: they are good news to someone who is tired of being their own god, and bad news to someone who wants to keep being their own god. The call is not to hate yourself. It is to stop treating yourself as god, so that you can finally receive your true self—and your true life—from Christ.
