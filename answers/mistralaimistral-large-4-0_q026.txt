In Matthew 12:34–35, Jesus uses the metaphor of a **hidden treasury** (θησαυρός, *thēsauros*) to describe the heart—a storehouse of moral substance that exists prior to and beneath speech. The logic of the passage unfolds through three movements that together suggest words are primarily **symptoms and revelations**, but not autonomous choices.

## 1. Speech as Overflow (The Causal Mechanism)
The key phrase is *“out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaketh”* (ἐκ γὰρ τῆς περισσείας τῆς καρδίας τὸ στόμα λαλεῖ). The Greek word for “abundance” (*perisseia*) suggests something overflowing, spilling over, more than sufficient. Jesus is describing speech not as a calculated performance but as **involuntary effusion**. Just as a full cup overflows when tilted, a heart saturated with evil or good cannot help but produce speech of the same kind. The mouth is the spout; the heart is the reservoir.

This makes speech a **symptom**—a diagnostic indicator of the internal condition. Jesus asks the rhetorical question, *“How can ye, being evil, speak good things?”* The grammar assumes an impossibility: moral corruption cannot produce moral speech any more than a poisoned spring can yield fresh water.

## 2. Speech as Revelation (The Epistemological Function)
The second half of the passage establishes a parallel structure:
- The **good man** (ὁ ἀγαθὸς ἄνθρωπος) → **good treasure** → **good things** (ἀγαθά)
- The **evil man** (ὁ πονηρὸς ἄνθρωπος) → **evil treasure** → **evil things** (πονηρά)

Here, speech serves as **revelation**. The treasure is hidden—it is the “abundance” stored within, the accumulated dispositions, desires, and judgments that constitute the heart. But speech makes this hidden reality visible and audible. When the Pharisees call Jesus’s works demonic (the immediate context in vv. 22–32), their words are not neutral observations; they are the **eruption of the venom** stored in their hearts. Jesus calls them *“generation of vipers”* (γεννήματα ἐχιδνῶν)—their speech is literally venomous because their nature is venomous.

In this sense, words are **testimony**. They reveal what the speaker would prefer to keep concealed. The “treasure” is the true moral inventory; speech is the receipt that betrays the transaction.

## 3. Why Speech Is Not (Primarily) a Choice Here
The passage notably undercuts the idea of speech as **volitional performance**. Jesus is not saying, *“Choose to speak good words even if your heart is evil.”* Quite the opposite: He says such a thing is impossible. The Pharisees are attempting to manage their speech (to appear righteous while accusing Jesus), but Jesus exposes the futility: *“Being evil, how can ye speak good things?”*

The verb *“bringeth forth”* (ἐκβάλλει, *ekballei*—"casts out" or "throws forth") reinforces this. It suggests ejection, purging, or natural production rather than deliberate crafting. You do not “choose” to bring forth what is in your treasure; it comes out because the heart is full.

However, there is an **indirect** connection to choice. If speech is the overflow of the heart, then the only way to change speech is to change the **treasure** itself. The passage implies that moral transformation must occur at the level of the heart’s “stockpile”—the accumulated loves, judgments, and dispositions—before the mouth can speak differently. In this sense, the choice is not about policing words, but about **curating the heart**.

## Conclusion
In Matthew 12:34–35, words are **symptoms** (they indicate the heart’s condition), **revelations** (they expose the hidden treasure), and **consequences** (they flow inevitably from the heart’s abundance). They are not, in this passage, autonomous choices that can be divorced from inner reality.

The theological implication is stark: **authenticity is inescapable**. You cannot fake goodness with eloquence, nor can you hide malice behind politeness. The mouth speaks what the heart has been storing. Therefore, the only path to good speech is not rhetorical training, but **cardiac renovation**—the transformation of the treasure itself.
