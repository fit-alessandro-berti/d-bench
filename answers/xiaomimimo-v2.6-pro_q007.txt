# Judge Not — But Discern Carefully

## The Misreading

Luke 6:37 is one of the most frequently quoted and most frequently misunderstood verses in Scripture. The popular paraphrase — *"Don't judge"* — is often taken to mean **moral relativism**: that no action is better than another, that calling anything wrong is itself the sin. If that were the teaching, Christianity would collapse into incoherence, because the same Jesus who speaks these words immediately surrounds them with moral instruction. He calls people to repentance, names behaviors as sinful, warns of consequences, and distinguishes wolves from sheep.

So what is he actually forbidding?

## Two Postures Toward Sin

The verse names a **pattern**, not a single prohibition. Notice the parallel structure:

> *"Judge not… condemn not… forgive…"*

These three describe a particular **movement of the soul** — one that grasps eagerly at the power to sentence others while withholding mercy. This is not neutral observation. It is an **appetite**: a hunger to find fault, to sit in the judge's seat, to savor the moment when someone's failure is confirmed and punishment seems deserved.

Against this, Jesus sets something quite different — **discernment**, the capacity to see clearly what is true while refusing to feed on the destruction of the person before you.

## The Difference, Point by Point

| | **Discernment** | **The Appetite to Condemn** |
|---|---|---|
| **What it sees** | The act is wrong *and* the person bears the image of God | The act is wrong *and* the person is beneath me |
| **Its aim** | Restoration — the truth spoken *toward* repentance | Satisfaction — the verdict delivered *for* its own sake |
| **Its relation to self** | The one who discerns remembers his own sinfulness first | The one who condemns locates sin entirely in the other |
| **Its tone** | Grief — *"I wish you knew the weight of what you've done"* | Pleasure — *"Now everyone sees what you really are"* |
| **Its standard** | Applies the law to **himself first** | Applies the law to **others first** |

Discernment says: *"What you did is wrong, and I am willing to bear the cost of telling you so because I love you."* The appetite to condemn says: *"What you did is wrong, and I will make sure you pay — publicly, completely, and with my full satisfaction."*

## Why the Context Proves the Point

Look at what Jesus does immediately before and after verse 37:

- **Verse 31** (the Golden Rule): he calls for a standard of conduct.
- **Verse 35–36** (love your enemies): he calls for specific moral behavior — generosity, lending without expectation of return, being merciful *"as your Father also is merciful."*
- **Verse 43–45**: he distinguishes good fruit from bad fruit — *"A good tree does not bear bad fruit."* This is **explicit moral discernment**.
- **Verse 46**: he calls out hypocrisy — *"Why call me 'Lord, Lord,' and do not do the things I say?"*

Jesus is not abolishing moral judgment. He is **reordering its use**. The measure — the *metron* of verse 38 — that you use on others will be used on you. This does not mean measurement is forbidden. It means: **use the right measure, from the right posture, toward the right end.**

## The Danger Jesus Names

There is a spiritual pathology in which moral seriousness becomes a weapon of spiritual pride. The Pharisees, whom Jesus repeatedly addresses in the Gospels, were **exceptionally discerning** — they knew the law inside and out. But their discernment had curdled into something Christ calls *condemnation*: a way of using truth to crush rather than to heal.

Luke 6:37 warns against this inversion — the moment when the person who is called to be a physician starts enjoying the disease.

## The Balance Scripture Holds

The same Jesus who says *"Judge not"* also says:

> *"Do not judge by appearances, but judge with right judgment."* (John 7:24)

This is the seam where discernment lives: **right judgment** — clear-eyed about what is true, just, and good, yet refusing to feed the soul's hunger for the destruction of another. The Christian is not asked to stop seeing. He is asked to **stop eating what the seeing feeds his pride**.

The prohibition is not against truth. It is against **using truth as a throne to sit on**.
