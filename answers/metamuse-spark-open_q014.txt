It says the second — but in a way that includes the first.

We tend to read it backwards:

> You love something, therefore you treasure it.

That's true, but it's not what the verse says. Jesus says:

> You treasure something, therefore you will love it.

KJV gets the grammar right:

> "For **where your treasure is**, there **will** your heart be also."

Not: "where your heart is, your treasure will be." And not present tense "is," but future "will be."

Treasure doesn't just *reveal* the heart. Treasure *trains* the heart.

### 1. What "treasure" means here

The word is *thesauros* — a storehouse, what you stockpile and secure for the future. To *thesaurizo* — "lay up" — is to make something your savings account.

Notice three things:

1. It's **possessive**: "*your* treasure." Not what you admire in theory, or approve of morally, but what you actually possess, invest in, keep.
2. It's **located**: "upon earth" vs "in heaven." Treasure always has an address. It lives somewhere.
3. It's **vulnerable or invulnerable**: On earth, moth and *brosis* — eating/consumption, traditionally translated "rust," but broader: rot, decay — and thieves. In the ancient world garments and precious metals *were* wealth. They required constant defense against insects, corrosion, and robbery.

Earthly treasure by its nature enslaves your attention: you have to maintain it against decay and defend it against theft. That maintenance *is* the formation.

This is reinforced by the context in Matthew. Right after this comes:

* The eye as lamp: what you look at fills you with light or darkness — 6:22-23
* You cannot serve two masters, God and Mammon — 6:24

It's all one argument about attention and allegiance. What you store determines what you see, and what you see determines who you serve.

### 2. How possession -> attention -> love

In Hebrew thought, *kardia*, heart, is not just emotion. It's the center of will, mind, and devotion — what you are oriented toward.

Jesus is describing a feedback loop:

**1. Possession claims attention.**
What you claim as "mine" you must think about. You plan for it, worry about it, insure it, check its value, compare it, talk about it. A portfolio, a house, a reputation, an image, even a grievance — once stored, it requires tending.

Earthly treasure is high-maintenance. You live in anxiety about moth, rust, thieves, inflation, obsolescence, status.

**2. Attention cultivates love.**
We become like what we behold. Sustained attention is not neutral — it is formative. The psychologist's version: you love what you watch.

Simone Weil put it this way: attention is the rarest form of generosity, and ultimately a form of prayer. What you give your gaze to, you give your self to.

Jesus says it starkly: your heart *will go* to your treasure. Deposit it in one place long enough, and your affections will move in to live there too.

**3. Love justifies further possession.**
Once the heart settles, it rationalizes the investment. This is why it feels like revelation: "I finally realized what I truly love." But much of the time, you didn't discover a pre-existing love. You *built* a love by repeated deposits of time, money, thought, imagination.

That's why the warning is so sharp. It's not just "don't love bad things." It's: be careful what you store, because you *will* come to love whatever you store — whether it was worthy of love or not.

It's both revelatory and formative, but in this order:

> Treasure reveals your *past* choices, and trains your *future* heart.

You can tell where your heart has been by looking at your treasure. You can tell where your heart is *going* by looking at where you keep putting treasure now.

### 3. Then what is "treasure in heaven"?

Jesus isn't talking about earning heaven with merit points. In Second Temple Judaism, "treasure in heaven" was a common idiom — especially for almsgiving, mercy, righteousness — that which God secures because it can't be eaten or stolen.

Compare Luke's parallel: "Sell your possessions and give to the poor. Provide purses for yourselves that will not wear out, a treasure in heaven that will never fail." Luke 12:33-34

Heavenly treasure is by nature non-defensive. It doesn't need moth-proofing. You release it, and God keeps it. That re-trains attention away from anxiety and defense toward trust and generosity.

Earthly treasure says: Hold tight, guard, accumulate — and your heart will become anxious, guarded, possessive.

Heavenly treasure says: Release, give, entrust — and your heart will become open, trusting, free.

The principle is morally neutral and works either way. That's the point.

> We do not simply treasure what we love.
> We will love what we treasure.
