## The Logic of the Test

The verse argues from the easier case to the harder one. The brother is *seen*: present, concrete, near at hand. God is *unseen*. If someone fails at loving the one who stands in front of them, their claim to succeed at loving the one they have never encountered with their senses has no credibility. Failure at the accessible task discredits the claim to the transcendent one.

But John means more than "practice on the easy thing first." In his letter the two loves are not separate projects. They are one love that has two directions.

**1. God cannot be loved as a private object.** Just before this verse, John writes: "No man hath seen God at any time. If we love one another, God dwelleth in us, and his love is perfected in us" (4:12). Since God is invisible, the place where love for him becomes real and visible is love toward those he has made and begotten. The neighbor is not a substitute for God. The neighbor is where love for God has to take bodily form.

**2. Love of the Father includes love of his children.** The next verse makes this explicit: "Every one that loveth him that begat loveth him also that is begotten of him" (5:1). To hate the brother is to hate what God loves, and in some sense to hate God's own family. A love for God that despises his children is not love of *this* God.

**3. Love in John is deed, not sentiment.** "Let us not love in word, neither in tongue; but in deed and in truth" (3:18). John has just described the man who has goods, sees his brother in need, and shuts up his compassion (3:17). The test is practical. Love toward God, if it exists, must show up somewhere, and the only place it can show up is in how we treat people.

## The Self-Deception Being Exposed

The phrase "If a man say" belongs to a series of claims John dismantles throughout the letter: "If we say that we have fellowship with him, and walk in darkness, we lie" (1:6); "He that saith, I know him, and keepeth not his commandments, is a liar" (2:4); "He that saith he is in the light, and hateth his brother, is in darkness" (2:9). In each case the problem is a gap between profession and life, where the speaker believes the profession. Several kinds of self-deception are in view.

**Love that is safe because it is abstract.** An invisible God makes no inconvenient demands in the moment. He does not interrupt you, irritate you, need your money, or disagree with you at the dinner table. It is easy to feel warmly toward a God who, in practice, is shaped by one's own imagination. The brother resists this. He is stubbornly *other*, with needs and faults of his own. Dostoevsky captures the same delusion in *The Brothers Karamazov*, where a doctor confesses that "the more I love humanity in general, the less I love man in particular." Love kept at the level of the ideal can never be refuted, so it can never be tested.

**Religious feeling mistaken for love.** Someone may have intense devotional experiences, strong convictions, even correct doctrine, and conclude from these that they love God. John's opponents (those who "went out from us," 2:19) apparently claimed superior spiritual knowledge while showing contempt for the community. John answers that exalted inner states prove nothing if the heart is closed toward the brother.

**Love of God as self-flattery.** A person's "love for God" can quietly become love for their own spiritual standing: being among the enlightened, the righteous, the insiders. That kind of devotion often *requires* someone to look down on. Hatred of the brother is then not an inconsistency in the system but its hidden engine. John calls this lying because it cannot survive honest examination.

**Hatred disguised as zeal.** The example of Cain in chapter 3 is instructive. Cain was a worshipper who brought an offering, yet he hated his brother "because his own works were evil, and his brother's righteous" (3:12). Religious activity can coexist with, and even cloak, resentment. The verse strips away the cloak.

## A Necessary Balance

John does not reduce love of God to humanitarian kindness. A few verses later he runs the test in the other direction: "By this we know that we love the children of God, when we love God, and keep his commandments" (5:2). The two loves check each other. Love of neighbor without God can drift into sentiment or self-will. Love of God without the neighbor is exposed as illusion. John's point in 4:20 is that the second failure is the one most easily hidden from oneself, because it can hide behind the vocabulary of piety.

In short, the verse treats the visible brother as the place where an invisible love must prove itself real. The self-deception it exposes is the belief that one can have a relationship with God that bypasses the people God has put in one's path. That belief feels spiritual, but it is actually a way of loving no one but oneself.
