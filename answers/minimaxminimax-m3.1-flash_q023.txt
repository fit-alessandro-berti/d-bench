Repentance here is not merely a feeling of regret or an outward performance. It is a **whole-person turning**—expressed in the Hebrew by “way” (דֶּרֶךְ) and “thoughts” (מַחְשָׁבוֹת).

### Outward conduct: the “way”

The “way” is a person’s settled course of life: habitual choices, loyalties, relationships, and public conduct. To “forsake” it means more than abandoning one isolated sin; it means turning away from an entire direction of life.

Thus repentance must become embodied. A person cannot claim to have returned to God while continuing to live according to the same goals, habits, and loyalties that previously ruled his conduct. Outward change is therefore necessary, though it is not by itself sufficient.

### Inward imagination: the “thoughts”

“Thoughts” in this context means more than passing ideas. It includes counsel, intentions, desires, hopes, and plans—the inward orientation from which action grows. Sin has both a visible course and an inner source.

Repentance therefore reaches the “heart” or mind: the wicked counsel and desires that sustain the old way must also be renounced. Mere remorse—“I feel bad about what I did”—is not the same as conversion. True repentance involves turning one’s trust, desires, and purposes back toward God. At the same time, Isaiah does not prescribe two separate stages; outward conduct and inward allegiance cannot be cleanly separated. Outward reform may conceal continued inner rebellion, while inward regret may coexist with unchanged behavior.

### The possibility of mercy

The verse’s sequence is significant: God says, “Seek,” “Call,” “Forsake,” “Return,” and then promises, “He will have mercy… and will abundantly pardon.”

This does not mean that human repentance earns mercy. Repentance is necessary as the direction of return, but it is not the basis or price of pardon. The surrounding context presents God as the one who invites, draws near, and enables people to seek him. Grace causes and sustains the turning; it is not a reward for self-generated moral effort.

The promise is therefore genuinely open to “the wicked” and “the unrighteous”: no one is excluded merely because sin is deeply embedded in both conduct and heart. Yet it is not a promise of pardon while one continues to cling to the old way and its thoughts. The words “while he may be found” and “while he is near” add urgency: the opportunity to turn should not be presumed indefinitely.

In short, Isaiah presents repentance as neither mere self-reproach nor mere outward reform. It is abandoning both the settled course of life and the inner desires that sustain it, and turning back to God. The human part is the forsaking and returning; the divine part is abundant mercy and pardon.
