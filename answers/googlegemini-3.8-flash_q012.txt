This passage from Jeremiah sits at the intersection of theology, moral philosophy, and psychology. Rather than forcing an "either/or" choice between pessimism and realism, the text is best understood as **radically realistic about human self-deception in order to dismantle naive optimism.**

Here is a breakdown of how to understand the tone of the passage and how it reshapes the way we talk about motives.

---

### 1. Pessimism vs. Realism

#### Why it feels like **Pessimism**:
In modern English, words like “desperately wicked” (often translated in modern versions as "incurable" or "gravely sick") sound fatalistic. It seems to declare that humans are thoroughly rotten and incapable of any good impulse. If read out of context, it sounds like cynicism—a worldview that suspects the worst of everyone at all times.

#### Why it is better understood as **Theological and Psychological Realism**:
* **The Epistemic Problem (We can't see ourselves clearly):** The core of the verse is not merely that the heart is flawed, but that it is *deceitful* and *inscrutable* (“who can know it?”). Modern psychology—through concepts like confirmation bias, rationalization, and subconscious drives—deeply agrees with this. We are remarkably skilled at inventing noble-sounding explanations for self-serving actions. Jeremiah diagnosed this millennia ago.
* **The Literary Context (Jeremiah 17:5–8):** Just before verse 9, Jeremiah contrasts two types of people: the one who "trusts in man and makes flesh his strength" (who becomes like a dried-up shrub) and the one who "trusts in the Lord" (who becomes like a tree planted by water). Verse 9 explains *why* trusting human strength fails: the internal operating system is compromised. 
* **Verse 10 provides the antidote:** The passage doesn't end with "the heart is hopeless, give up." It ends with God saying, *“I the Lord search the heart.”* It is not nihilistic; it argues that self-knowledge has limits, and true clarity requires an external, objective judge.

**Summary:** The passage is **pessimistic about human autonomy** (our ability to accurately judge and fix ourselves), but **realistic about human nature** (our capacity for rationalization).

---

### 2. How to Talk About Motives When the Heart is Unreliable

If Jeremiah is right, the popular cultural advice to *"follow your heart"* or the common defense *"I know my own heart"* is fundamentally flawed. If the internal witness is compromised, how do we talk about motives responsibly?

#### 1. Adopt Epistemic Humility (Abandon "Purity of Intent")
When discussing our own actions, we should retire claims of total innocence. Instead of saying, *"I know my intentions were 100% pure,"* a realistic posture sounds like:
* *"My conscious intention was X, but I recognize I likely have blind spots or mixed motives I’m not fully aware of."*
* This prevents self-righteousness. It acknowledges that human beings almost always act from a tangled knot of altruism, self-interest, fear, and pride.

#### 2. Focus on "The Fruit" Rather Than Intentions (Verse 10)
Notice that in verse 10, God judges not merely by intentions, but *“according to the fruit of his doings.”* 
* When conflicts arise, people often hide behind intentions: *"That’s not what I meant, so you shouldn't be hurt."*
* A posture shaped by Jeremiah acknowledges that **impact matters as much as intent.** Because our hearts deceive us about our motives, the observable "fruit" (the actual outcome and harm caused) is often a truer diagnostic tool than our internal feelings.

#### 3. Rely on External Mirrors (Community and Accountability)
If you cannot fully know your own heart, you cannot be your own sole judge. Talking about motives requires external reference points:
* **Trusted outsiders:** Friends, mentors, or counselors who are permitted to say, *"You say you did that out of love, but it looked a lot like control."*
* **A standard outside the self:** Whether scripture, ethical frameworks, or objective moral duties that override subjective feelings.

#### 4. Shift from Self-Defense to Self-Examination
Instead of using motives as a shield to deflect criticism, the text invites a posture of ongoing examination. A classic biblical counterpart to Jeremiah 17 is **Psalm 139:23–24**:
> *“Search me, O God, and know my heart: try me, and know my thoughts: And see if there be any wicked way in me...”*

David does not say, *"I have searched my heart and found nothing wrong."* He asks an external, omniscient observer to search him, because he knows he cannot do it reliably himself.

### Conclusion
To talk about motives through the lens of Jeremiah 17 is to **speak with provisional modesty**. It means giving others the benefit of the doubt (since their hearts are complex and opaque to us) while remaining rigorously suspicious of our own self-justifications.
