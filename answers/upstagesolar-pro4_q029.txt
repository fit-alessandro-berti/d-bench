This passage from Galatians 6:1 outlines a specific ethical framework for community accountability. The moral posture required is not one of superiority or judicial harshness, but of **humble solidarity and cautious compassion**.

Here is an analysis of the posture required and why these three elements are inextricably linked.

### 1. The Required Moral Posture
The text assumes that correction is sometimes necessary ("if a man be overtaken in a fault"), but it strictly dictates *how* that correction must be carried out. The posture is defined by three boundaries:

*   **Who corrects:** "Ye which are spiritual." This does not necessarily imply a clerical class, but rather those walking in the Spirit and maturity. It implies that correction should come from a place of spiritual stability, not from someone equally entangled in sin.
*   **How to act:** "Restore... in the spirit of meekness." The goal is healing (restoration), not punishment. The manner must be gentle, patient, and free from self-righteous indignation.
*   **How to view oneself:** "Considering thyself." The corrector must maintain a realistic assessment of their own vulnerability.

### 2. Why Restoration, Meekness, and Self-Suspicion are Kept Together
These three elements function as a balanced triad. If you remove one, the process of correction corrupts into hypocrisy, abuse, or cowardice.

**A. Restoration requires Meekness**
If the goal is to restore a fallen brother to health and usefulness within the community, harshness is counter-productive. Anger, condemnation, or prideful correction usually drives the sinner further away or produces shame rather than repentance. Meekness creates a safe enough environment for truth to be received rather than resisted.

**B. Meekness requires Self-Suspicion**
It is nearly impossible to be truly meek toward a sinner if you believe yourself to be fundamentally superior to them. The command to "consider thyself, lest thou also be tempted" strips the corrector of moral high ground. It reminds the corrector that the difference between them and the sinner is often circumstance or grace, not inherent superiority. This self-awareness prevents the "spirit of meekness" from turning into a spirit of judgment.

**C. Self-Suspicion protects against Hypocrisy in Restoration**
Without self-suspicion, restoration becomes a power play. The Pharisee who "sees" the mote in his brother's eye is blind to the beam in his own. By keeping the corrector in a state of humble vigilance ("lest thou also be tempted"), the text ensures that the motivation remains the restoration of the brother, rather than the elevation of the corrector. It guards the heart against the temptation to feel righteous by comparing oneself to someone else's failure.

### Summary
The passage argues that **true spiritual correction is a collective act of humility rather than an individual act of judgment.** Restoration is the goal, meekness is the method, and self-suspicion is the safeguard. They are kept together because correction without meekness is cruelty, meekness without self-suspicion is hypocrisy, and self-suspicion without the goal of restoration is mere introspection.
