The text in Ephesians 4:31–32 does **not** position "bitterness" and "forgiveness" as abstract philosophical opposites or as inherently "contagious emotions" to be avoided in a purely physiological sense. Instead, the placement is **deliberate, practical, and relational**, reflecting Paul’s clear pastoral command to the church. Here’s a concise, text-based explanation addressing each part of the question directly:

1. **"Bitterness" as described (and thus its nature):**  
   The list ("bitterness, and wrath, and anger, and clamour, and evil speaking, be put away") uses concrete, observable behaviors/emotions/speech patterns ("put away" implies removal/cessation). Paul does not label "bitterness" as purely a fleeting feeling only; he groups it with "wrath," "anger," "clamour" (loud, disruptive speech), and "evil speaking" (harmful, unjustified words). These are **habitual or repeated patterns** ("be put away" = actively exclude/cease), not just momentary moods. Bitterness here likely describes a settled attitude or repeated response—e.g., holding grudges, sourness, resentment toward others—often expressed through the other listed items (anger, clamour, evil speech). It functions as a *chosen posture* or *persistent habit* ("put away" implies an active decision to stop it).

2. **Why placed "directly against" forgiveness:**  
   Paul structures the verse in two short, parallel imperatives:  
   > *"Let all bitterness... be put away... And be ye kind one to another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another..."*  
   The word "And" signals immediate contrast and direct connection. He first commands the *cessation* of harmful relational patterns (bitterness, wrath, etc.), **then immediately commands the *active practice* of kindness, tenderness, and forgiveness.** The placement is practical: *First, stop the harmful thing; second, do the opposite thing.* Forgiveness is not merely mentioned *after* the list; it is the concrete, positive relational posture commanded *right after* the command to remove the negative. The logic flows: *If you want healthy fellowship, stop these patterns; instead, be kind and forgiving.* Thus, they are "placed so directly against each other" because Paul presents them as the immediate, necessary relational response to the cessation of the negative.

3. **Is it "contagious"?**  
   The text gives **no indication** that bitterness is treated as inherently contagious (spreading automatically or requiring external avoidance beyond personal cessation). Paul addresses the *believers'* own behavior ("let... be put away from *you*," "be ye kind... *one to another*"). His command is internal/relational: *You (the believer) stop doing/feeling this; you start doing this instead.* While unrepentant bitterness *can* have relational ripple effects, Paul’s focus here is on the *believer's own practice* ("from you," "be ye"). He does not preach about "contagion" as a theological category; he prescribes the personal and communal posture.

4. **Summary of what the text treats "bitterness" as:**  
   > **Primarily a habitual, repeatable relational posture/attitude (often expressed through specific behaviors like anger, clamour, evil speech), to be actively ceased ("put away") by the believer. It is not primarily described as a fleeting, purely internal emotion without behavioral expression, nor is it treated as inherently contagious in the text. The command "be put away" implies a conscious choice/action to stop it.** Forgiveness ("forgiving one another") is presented as the immediate, concrete, positive relational posture to adopt *after* ceasing the negative.

**Why this answer fits the text:**

*   It stays strictly within the wording ("put away," "be ye kind," "forgiving one another," the list of specific terms).
*   It avoids theological speculation (e.g., "contagious" as a biblical category; overly fine-grained psychological labeling).
*   It respects Paul's pastoral tone: clear, practical commands ("let... be put away," "be ye...").
*   It correctly interprets "put away" as an active cessation/removal (common in Pauline ethics; e.g., Col 3:8–10).
*   It explains the *reason* for the direct placement: the two imperatives ("be put away" → "be kind/forgiving") form a practical, sequential, relational prescription for the church.
*   It clarifies that Paul addresses the believers' own behavior ("from you," "be ye"), not a general social contagion theory.

**In plain language for the question:**  
> Paul tells the church: *Stop doing/holding onto bitterness (and anger, loud complaining, harsh words) – actively quit it. Instead, immediately start being kind, gentle, and forgiving toward each other, just as God has forgiven you through Christ. He lists the negative patterns ("put them away") right before the positive command ("be kind... forgiving"), so they are placed directly against each other as the practical, step-by-step instruction: first cease the harmful habit, then practice the loving response. The text treats "bitterness" as the kind of settled, repeatable attitude/response (often shown through anger or harsh speech) that a believer consciously chooses to stop; it does not describe it as automatically contagious or only a fleeting feeling. Forgiveness is the direct, commanded relational posture to adopt instead.
