

Galatians 6:1 requires a moral posture of **humble, restorative gentleness** — not superiority, not harshness, not indifference, and not punitive zeal. The one who corrects must act as a fellow sinner under grace, not as a detached judge above grace.

Three things are held together because each one protects the others from becoming corrupt.

---

## 1. Restoration: the purpose of correction

The word “restore” carries the idea of **mending, setting right, repairing, or putting something back into its proper condition**. It was used of mending nets or setting a dislocated bone. That is crucial.

The goal of correction is not:

- to shame the person,
- to display one’s own righteousness,
- to punish,
- to isolate,
- or to win an argument.

The goal is **healing and reintegration**. The person “overtaken in a fault” is still addressed as “brethren.” Correction is therefore familial, not contemptuous. It is aimed at the person’s recovery, not their destruction.

Without restoration, correction becomes mere discipline or domination.

---

## 2. Meekness: the manner of correction

The correction is to be done “in the spirit of meekness.” Biblical meekness is not weakness. It is **strength under control** — firmness without cruelty, seriousness without arrogance, truth without venom.

A harsh person may say, “I am only telling the truth.” But Paul insists that truth must be carried in a manner fitting for healing. A surgeon must be steady and gentle, not because the injury is unreal, but because the patient must not be further wounded.

Meekness prevents correction from becoming anger, sarcasm, contempt, public humiliation, or self-righteous performance.

Without meekness, restoration becomes coercion.

---

## 3. Self-suspicion: the guard against pride

“Considering thyself, lest thou also be tempted” introduces holy self-distrust. The corrector must not imagine that he is beyond temptation. He is not correcting from a position of achieved moral immunity.

This self-suspicion is not paralyzing introspection. It is sober realism:  
*I too am capable of falling. I too may be tempted — perhaps by the same sin, perhaps by pride, harshness, hypocrisy, or spiritual arrogance while correcting another.*

This prevents the act of correction from becoming an occasion for sin in the corrector. The very act of rebuking can tempt one to feel superior, to enjoy exposure, or to forget one’s own dependence on mercy.

Without self-suspicion, meekness becomes sentimental weakness or self-deception.

---

## Why they must remain together

Paul binds these together because Christian correction is neither soft tolerance nor severe judgment. It is **truth-bearing love**.

- **Restoration** keeps the goal right: healing, not punishment.
- **Meekness** keeps the method right: gentleness, not brutality.
- **Self-suspicion** keeps the heart right: humility, not superiority.

If any one is removed, the whole act becomes distorted:

- Restoration without meekness becomes forceful “fixing.”
- Meekness without restoration becomes mere niceness that leaves the person in harm.
- Restoration and meekness without self-suspicion become spiritual pride.
- Self-suspicion without restoration becomes cowardice: “Who am I to speak?” and therefore “I will say nothing.”

So Paul keeps them together to show that correction is a form of **burden-bearing**, which he immediately connects to fulfilling “the law of Christ” in Galatians 6:2.

---

## The deeper moral posture

The required posture is therefore:

> “I see a brother or sister wounded by sin. I must help, not crush. I must speak, not flatter. I must act, but I must not pretend I am beyond temptation. I must be firm, but gentle; honest, but merciful; truthful, but aware that I too stand only by grace.”

This is the opposite of the “vainglory” Paul warned about in Galatians 5:26. The spiritual person is not the one who stands above the fallen; the spiritual person is the one who bends down to restore the fallen without forgetting that he too may fall.
