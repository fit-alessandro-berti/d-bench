I think it's destabilizing because it concedes everything the accusers say and still disarms them. Jesus doesn't dispute the facts, the law, or even the sentence. He authorizes the stoning and attaches a condition about who may carry it out. The question shifts from "what does she deserve?" to "who are you to administer it?", and the accusers have no prepared defense for that.

Each of your three readings captures part of it, but none is quite right.

- **It doesn't forbid judgment.** Jesus judges the act himself: "go, and sin no more" calls it sin. What he blocks is condemnation, the final, lethal verdict on a person. The story separates two things we habitually fuse: recognizing wrong and claiming the standing to destroy the wrongdoer.

- **It's more than an exposure of hypocrisy.** A hypocrisy charge would be "you're adulterers too." The condition is instead to be without sin at all. That isn't a *tu quoque*. It removes the line between the kind of person who gets stoned and the kind who throws stones.

- **It does interrupt the mob, and precisely.** The key word may be "first." Deuteronomy 17:7 required the witnesses to throw the first stones, so Jesus is invoking the law, not evading it. But a mob has no first. Its violence works because responsibility is diffused, and each person throws because others are throwing. By demanding a first thrower, he forces each person to act as an individual. So they leave "one by one, beginning at the eldest." The crowd dissolves into persons, each alone with his own history.

The deeper thing, I think, is that the sentence breaks the link between being right and being righteous. The accusers were right, yet they were using her guilt for other ends: as bait to trap Jesus, and as proof of their own innocence. Condemning someone is one of the cheapest ways to feel clean, and the sentence takes that away while leaving the moral law intact. It destabilizes because it offers neither the comfort of relativism ("who's to say what's wrong?") nor the comfort of righteousness ("at least I'm not her").

Then comes the final turn: by the story's own logic, the one person present who meets the condition declines to use it. "Neither do I condemn thee." The only one qualified to throw the stone chooses not to. Augustine's summary of the ending is that two were left, *misera et misericordia*, misery and mercy.

The sentence can't simply be a rule, though. Universalized, it would abolish every court, since no judge is sinless. I read it as an intervention in a particular scene, a lynching dressed as a legal proceeding, not as jurisprudence. But it leaves a permanent residue: all human judgment is the guilty judging the guilty, and should be done with that knowledge.

(The passage is absent from the earliest manuscripts of John and was likely a floating tradition added later. That complicates its textual status but hasn't dented its moral force.)
