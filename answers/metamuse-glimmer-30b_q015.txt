The image works because it is physically absurd and visibly self-defeating.

The Greek is *karphos* a tiny splinter of wood or a mote of dust, and *dokos* a building beam, a rafter. A beam in the eye is not just a bigger sin than a speck in a brother's eye, it is an impossibility. You could not open your eye, let alone see the speck in someone else's eye, let alone reach in and pull it out. The comedy is the point: the man who offers to do delicate surgery on another is himself blinded by a log.

The eye is the organ of moral seeing in the Sermon on the Mount. To behold the mote is to judge, to diagnose, to want to correct. The problem Jesus names is not the desire to help, it is the disproportion and the blindness that goes with it. The hypocrite, *hypokrites*, the stage-player, performs the role of the righteous judge while wearing the mask of his own unconfessed sin. He notices the small fault in his brother because his own great fault is the very lens through which he looks.

That is why the passage does not forbid correction at all. It forbids correction in that order and in that posture.

* First cast out the beam out of thine own eye. Self-examination and repentance precede any ministry to another. The beam is your own, the speck is the brother's. Your sin is the larger, more blinding one.
* And then shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote out of thy brother's eye. The final clause affirms the legitimacy of fraternal correction. Once you are unblinded, you *can* see clearly enough to help. The mote is real, and the brother does need help, but it can only be given effectively after humility has removed the obstruction in you.

So Jesus condemns hypocritical judgment, judgment that is selective, self-exonerating and blind, while preserving — and indeed requiring — the humble, self-aware correction of a brother. The sharpness of the image is that it makes the moral absurdity visible: you cannot be a surgeon for others while a beam is lodged in your own eye.
