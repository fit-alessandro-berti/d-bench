Matthew 5:44-45 is difficult because it is not a suggestion about feelings. It is a command about identity.

It is hard to take seriously for three reasons that run together.

First, it violates our moral accounting system. Human justice, even at its best, is conditional: you are good to me, I am good to you; you harm me, I punish or withdraw. The natural ethic is reciprocity. Jesus explicitly moves from "you have heard it said... eye for eye" to an ethic of non-reciprocity. Loving enemies, blessing those who curse you, doing good to haters, praying for persecutors removes the link between desert and treatment. That feels irrational, and even unsafe, because it seems to forfeit the right to protect oneself and to be defended.

Second, we confuse love with sentiment or approval. Sentimental love is selective, warm, and earned. It is easy and it is cheap. Jesus is not commanding sentimental affection for the enemy, nor is he commanding you to pretend there is no enmity. The verbs he gives are active and disciplined: bless, do good, pray. That is agape, not philia. It is a will to will the good of the other, to refuse to return evil for evil, even while recognizing the evil is real. To call it sentimental is to miss the cost. Sentiment fades when you are cursed. This does not.

Third, we often try to make it strategic. That is the "it's actually good for you" reading: if you love your enemy you disarm them, win them over, make peace. That can be true at times, but it is not why Jesus gives the command. He grounds it not in outcome but in origin: "That ye may be the children of your Father which is in heaven." The reason you love enemies is the same reason God gives sun and rain to the evil and the good. The Father's goodness is not a reward for moral performance, it is constitutive of who he is. To love enemies is to imitate that character.

That is why it is a deeper form of moral strength, and not weakness.

It is strength because it requires you to relinquish the role of judge and avenger. To love an enemy you must trust that God is the one who makes justice happen, so you do not have to. That frees you from the endless cycle of humiliation and retaliation that keeps both parties defined by the wound. It is kenotic: you give up the right to make the other person pay, which is a real power.

It is also strength because it is costly and unilateral. It is not contingent on repentance, nor does it guarantee reciprocity. It is not naive; it is actually the most realistic recognition of the human condition. Everyone is simultaneously enemy and beloved in God's economy. If the Father rains on the just and the unjust, then indiscriminate generosity is not sentimental idealism, it is the way reality is ordered.

In practice that does not mean endorsing abuse or removing boundaries. Love of enemies in Scripture still allows for firm confrontation, truth-telling, and self-protection. It means the response you offer is not defined by the other person's hostility. You bless, you do good, you pray because you have chosen to be a child of a Father who loves without first demanding moral qualification.

That is why it is so hard to take seriously: it asks you to live as if God's unconditional giving is more real than your need to be vindicated.
