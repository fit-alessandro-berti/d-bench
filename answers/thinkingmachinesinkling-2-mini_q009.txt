Romans 12:20–21 defines victory over evil not as defeating the enemy through force, but as refusing to let retaliation define one’s power. The “victory” is a moral and psychological victory: the capacity to respond to provocation by restraint, generosity, and moral self-definition rather than by escalation.

The passage is not best read as a strategy manual for harming the opponent. “Heap coals of fire” is idiomatic; many readings see it as intensifying the enemy’s conscience, guilt, discomfort, or self-condemnation—not as literal combustion. The practical command is simple: feed, give drink, do good. The “victory” is that kindness becomes a counterforce, not from weakness but from mastery.

So, is it weak, manipulative, or genuinely strong?

- **Not simply weak:** it does not mean submission, passivity, or being unprotected. It resists evil precisely when resistance is costly and uncomfortable.
- **Not intrinsically manipulative:** it becomes manipulative if done to trap, provoke, shame, or “win” over the enemy for personal advantage. If the generosity is sincere and not aimed at victory over the other person, it is not mere tactic.
- **Genuinely strong:** it is strong in the way self-mastery is strong. It costs resolve, patience, and exclusion of retaliation from the imagination and hand. It can be harder than aggression because it demands that one’s conduct remains morally intelligible under provocation.

The strongest reading is therefore: victory over evil means not being overcome by evil, but overcoming evil with good—an endurance and discipline, not an easy ethic. It also has limits: it should not be used to enable violence, ignore culpability, or ignore victims. “Feed the enemy” does not absolve someone of consequences for real harm; it redirects the response away from retaliation toward a higher moral action.
