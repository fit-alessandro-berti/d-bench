## How the passage defines victory

The military verb "overcome" (Greek *nikaō*, to conquer) appears twice in verse 21, and it implies two things:

1. **Defeat means becoming like your enemy.** "Be not overcome of evil" is addressed to the wronged person, not the wrongdoer. Evil wins when it reproduces itself in you, so retaliation is a form of surrender, because the enemy has dictated who you become.

2. **Victory means ending the enmity, not the enemy.** Good is the weapon ("overcome evil *with good*"). The goal is that the evil itself is defeated, ideally by the enemy ceasing to be one.

## Is it weak?

Nietzsche's critique was that such ethics are powerlessness dressed up as virtue. But the command is not passive. It does not say "endure" but "feed him," which takes initiative and resources. Hitting back is the reflex, and overriding it takes strength. Nor does the command abandon justice. Verse 19 hands vengeance to God, and Romans 13 assigns punishment to civil authority. It also does not require obeying the enemy. You meet his need (hunger, thirst), not his demands.

## Is it manipulative?

The "coals of fire" phrase, quoted from Proverbs 25:21–22, can sound like "be kind so he'll suffer." There are two main readings:

- **Burning shame that leads to repentance.** This is the majority view, from Augustine to most modern commentators.
- **Heightened judgment if he remains unrepentant.** This is a minority view.

Even on the second reading, the context rules out kindness as covert revenge: "Bless, and curse not" (v. 14), and the weapon must be actual *good*. Manipulation uses the appearance of good for one's own ends. Here the enemy receives real food for real hunger, whether or not he changes. The command can be practiced passive-aggressively, but that is a corruption of it. Kindness meant to wound would be vengeance by other means.

## Is it genuinely strong?

I think so, for three reasons:

- **It keeps the initiative.** The enemy does not set the terms of the encounter.
- **It breaks the cycle.** Retaliation gives evil a second act, and unearned good is the one response it cannot feed on.
- **It follows Paul's logic about God.** "When we were enemies, we were reconciled to God" (Rom. 5:10). Paul asks believers to treat enemies as he claims God treated them.

One caveat is that the passage does not promise the enemy will change. Its strength is that the first half of the victory, not being conquered, is secure either way.
