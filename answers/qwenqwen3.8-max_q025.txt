The moral danger in Matthew 6:1 is not simply that good deeds are **visible**, but that they are done **“to be seen of them.”** The verse warns against making human observation the *purpose* of the act.

In context, Jesus is condemning religious performance:

> “Take heed that ye do not your alms before men, **to be seen of them**…”

The key phrase is **“to be seen of them.”** It points to intention. Public goodness becomes dangerous when it turns into a kind of theater—when the person doing good is seeking the praise, admiration, or approval of other people rather than seeking God’s approval.

This is why Jesus later says:

> “Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father which is in heaven.”  
> — Matthew 5:16

So visibility itself is not evil. In fact, Jesus elsewhere says good works may be seen. The difference is the direction of the glory. In Matthew 5:16, people see good works and glorify **God**. In Matthew 6:1, people see good works and glorify **the performer**.

Therefore, the problem is not:

- being seen while doing good,
- giving publicly when necessary,
- having others witness righteous acts,
- or even receiving recognition in itself.

The problem is the **craving to be seen**, the desire to make human admiration the reward.

Jesus says that if you do your giving “to be seen of them,” you “have no reward of your Father which is in heaven.” The implication is that the person has already received the reward they wanted: human applause. Their act was not ultimately offered to God, but to the audience of men.

So public goodness is morally dangerous because it can easily become self-glorifying. It can take something genuinely good—charity, prayer, righteousness—and turn it into a performance. The danger is that the heart shifts from loving God and neighbor to loving one’s own reputation.

In short: **visibility is the occasion of temptation, but the craving to be seen is the sin.** Public goodness is not wrong in itself; it becomes wrong when it is done for the sake of human admiration rather than for the glory of God.
