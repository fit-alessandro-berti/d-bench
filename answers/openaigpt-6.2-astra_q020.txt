Bitterness is pictured as a **root** because the warning concerns not just a harmful act, but what takes hold in a person and keeps producing such acts. A root grows out of sight before a shoot breaks the surface. In the same way, a hurt may be rehearsed until resentment becomes a settled habit of judgment. The hurt itself is not the moral failure; the danger is that resentment begins to govern how one sees and treats others.

“Springing up” marks the point when what was hidden becomes visible. Someone who has long nursed a grievance may come to hear contempt in an innocent remark or retell an old wrong in a way that invites friends to take sides. That is how “many” can be defiled: others need not have suffered the original wrong to inherit the suspicion it produced.

The image also echoes Deuteronomy 29:18, where a “root” bearing poisonous fruit warns of a turning away from God within the community. Hebrews’ concern is therefore broader than private unhappiness. “Looking diligently” calls for shared care: take hurt seriously and address it truthfully, before resentment sets the terms on which the whole community lives together.
