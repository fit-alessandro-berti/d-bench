The first obstacle to that reading is the sentence itself. "Judge not" is a moral judgment — an imperative, a norm, a claim about what we owe one another. You cannot abolish the moral law by command; if moral judgments never mattered, the first casualty would be this verse. And the verse runs entirely on moral machinery: being judged, being condemned, being forgiven — a world of debts, audits, and returns. Jesus does not unmake the scale. He warns that the scale is load-bearing in both directions.

The difference in one line: the relativist says judgment is weightless; Jesus says it is far too weighty to be wielded as a final verdict on a human soul.

## What the words actually forbid

The Greek pairs the verbs: *krinete*... *me katadikazete*. *Krinein* is the broad word — to separate, distinguish, decide; it is the verb of telling good fruit from bad. *Katadikazein* is narrower: to sentence, to give judgment against. The escalation tells you the target. What is forbidden is not the faculty of moral distinction but the act of pronouncing sentence.

The context confirms it. In the same sermon: "every tree is known by his own fruit" (Luke 6:44) — an instruction in moral inspection. Later in the same Gospel: "If thy brother trespass against thee, rebuke him; and if he repent, forgive him" (Luke 17:3) — the rebuke is a judgment, and even the forgiveness waits on a judgment, since repentance must be discerned too. Elsewhere Jesus says it outright: "Judge not according to the appearance, but judge righteous judgment" (John 7:24). Discernment is commanded, not cancelled.

## The third clause is the proof

"Forgive, and ye shall be forgiven." Forgiveness is the most judgment-intensive act in the verse. To forgive someone is to insist the debt is real — really theirs, really owed — and then to release it. Excusing is not forgiving; where nothing is judged wrong, there is nothing to forgive, only things to shrug at. C.S. Lewis observed that you only need to forgive what you decline to excuse. So within one breath the verse forbids the verdict and commands the release — and the release is unintelligible without the verdict. Mercy is not the absence of judgment. It is judgment that declines to complete itself: the debt affirmed, then remitted.

## Discernment and appetite

An appetite is not directed at the good of its object. Hunger takes no interest in the bread's welfare; it consumes the bread for the eater's satisfaction. That is the precise difference. Discernment is a faculty ordered to truth — and through truth, to the good of the thing judged. The appetite to condemn asks a different question: *what will this verdict do for me?*

- Discernment keeps the judged person a **subject** — a story still running, still capable of repentance, still unknown to me in the ways that matter. Appetite consumes them as material; the verdict feeds the judge.
- Discernment holds the case open — to correction, to my own misreading, to mercy. Appetite needs closure; it needs the other to *stay* guilty.
- Discernment starts with the beam. (Note that Matthew's beam-and-mote passage ends with "then shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote out of thy brother's eye" — the speck is still to be addressed, only after, and in the light of, self-examination.) Appetite audits only specks.
- Discernment can weep. Jesus pronounces Jerusalem's judgment with tears (Luke 19:41–44); God declares he has "no pleasure in the death of the wicked" (Ezekiel 33:11). Appetite gloats.

And the crucial subtlety: condemnation, in the forbidden sense, need not be false. The Pharisee of Luke 18 is roughly accurate — he does fast, does tithe; the publican probably is a collaborator. Verdicts can be true and still be exactly what Jesus forbids, because the sin is not error but *usurpation* — taking facts and building a throne out of them. "I thank thee that I am not as other men." The speck in your brother's eye is real; what indicts the judge is the beam and the bench.

The elder brother exposes the appetite's tell. His grievance in Luke 15 is, on its face, defensible — the prodigal did waste the living. But the celebration ruins him. His righteousness needed the brother to stay lost. Hence a quick diagnostic: does news of another's possible innocence gladden me, or threaten me?

## The anatomy of the appetite

Why do we crave verdicts?

1. **It relieves.** "Wherein thou judgest another, thou condemnest thyself; for thou that judgest doest the same things" (Romans 2:1). Condemnation is guilt's favorite disguise — I pay my own arraignment with someone else's conviction.
2. **It pleases.** Indignation is the cheapest righteousness on the market: the rush of the verdict without the labor of obedience.
3. **It simplifies.** A sentenced person requires no further thought. Discernment is ongoing labor — proportioning, revising, holding complexity open. The verdict is cognitive rest.
4. **It builds identity.** The Pharisee's prayer is a liturgy of self-construction: my innocence erected, load-bearing, on his guilt.
5. **It binds.** Nothing knits a crowd together faster than a shared victim — which is why the appetite to condemn, given full scope, ends at a cross, where humanity renders its verdict and executes its Judge. His answer to that verdict is "Father, forgive them": judgment not abolished, but borne.

## The measure returns

"With the same measure that ye mete withal it shall be measured to you again" (Luke 6:38). This works at two registers. Divinely: the standard you apply becomes the standard applied to you (Matthew 18's unforgiving servant). Psychologically: the tribunal inside a condemning mind never adjourns. If you live by verdicts, you cannot be acquitted by them — you know what you know about yourself. The verse is less a threat than a description of how judgment propagates through a world, and through a soul.

## What the relativist reading would cost

Say moral judgments never mattered, and the Sermon doesn't get softer — it gets deleted. Nothing wrong has been done to you, so "love your enemies" loses its object; there are only adversaries, not wrongdoers. Nothing is owed, so "forgive" is void — an empty gesture costing nothing because it judged nothing. And the verse's own logic — measures meted and measured back — presupposes an ordered moral world more strongly than almost any text in the New Testament. The relativist doesn't step out of the courtroom; he empties it. And an empty courtroom forgives no one.

Jesus claims the opposite: judgment is so real, its returns so certain, its final form so demanding of total knowledge and an unblemished judge, that it is simply beyond us. "There is one lawgiver... who art thou that judgest another?" (James 4:12). We are never the bench. We are always also defendants, and at best witnesses.

"Judge nothing before the time, until the Lord come" (1 Corinthians 4:5). Every human verdict is a premature summary of a story God is still telling. Discernment is judgment that works, waits, and hopes; condemnation is judgment that feasts now and closes the book early.

The difference, finally: "moral judgments never matter" empties the scale; "judge not" tells us the scale is too heavy for our hands. And the two sayings part ways most sharply at the cross — where the false verdict was rendered by us, and the true judgment was borne for us.
