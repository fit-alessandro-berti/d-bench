The passage achieves its rhetorical force through vivid exaggeration and a carefully structured moral sequence.

### What makes the image so sharp?

1. **Absurd Hyperbole:** The contrast between a "mote" (a tiny speck of dust or splinter) and a "beam" (a massive structural timber or log) is intentionally comic and grotesque. A beam cannot physically fit in an eye, which makes the mental picture immediately arresting. It forces the listener to see how ridiculous disproportionate self-righteousness looks from the outside.
2. **The Vulnerability of the Eye:** The eye is one of the most delicate parts of the body. Even a speck of dust causes intense irritation, and removing it requires steady hands and sharp vision. Picturing someone with a plank protruding from their own face attempting delicate "eye surgery" on someone else highlights not just the absurdity of hypocrisy, but its danger: an unexamined critic is clumsy and likely to harm the person they claim to help.
3. **The Irony of Blindness:** Sight is a classic metaphor for moral perception and understanding. The would-be corrector assumes they see better than their brother ("beholdest thou the mote"), yet their own vision is completely obstructed. Their eagerness to fix another person’s minor flaw actually proves their own total lack of self-awareness.

### How it criticizes hypocrisy without forbidding moral correction

1. **It targets the posture, not the act:** Jesus calls the person *"Thou hypocrite"* (literally, an actor playing a role) because they are pretending to be a moral physician while ignoring a far worse condition in themselves. The condemnation falls on double standards, pride, and moral blindness—not on the idea that faults exist or need addressing.
2. **It establishes a sequence ("First... and then"):** Verse 5 does not say, *"Leave the mote alone."* Instead, it gives an order of operations: **first** deal with your own massive flaw, **and then** you will *"see clearly to cast out the mote."* Moral correction is explicitly permitted, and even expected, once the prerequisite of self-examination and repentance is met.
3. **It reframes correction as delicate care:** Note the familial language (*"thy brother"*) and the metaphor itself. Having a speck in one's eye is genuinely painful and impairs vision; removing it is a helpful, healing act if done gently and accurately. By demanding that the helper first clear their own vision, the passage transforms moral correction from an act of self-righteous superiority into an act of humility, competence, and genuine care.
