

In Matthew 6:1, the danger is not that goodness is public, but that it is done **in order to be seen**.

The key phrase is:

> “your alms before men, **to be seen of them**”

That “to be seen” changes the whole moral character of the act. The problem is not visibility itself; it is the **craving for human recognition** that public visibility can awaken, and that may become the real purpose of the good deed.

So the verse warns against public goodness when it becomes a performance. The almsgiver may technically do something right—giving, serving, helping—but if the hidden aim is reputation, applause, admiration, or social superiority, then the good deed is no longer truly offered to God. It becomes a means of self-praise. The crowd’s approval replaces the Father’s approval. In that case, the person has already received the reward they were really after: the good opinion of people.

This is why Jesus’ warning is not simply, “Do good deeds secretly.” Later in the passage he advises that your left hand not know what your right hand is doing, but the deeper principle is not that secrecy is intrinsically virtuous. Public acts can be good, and sometimes necessary. Visibility itself is morally neutral. It can be innocent, even useful. But when public visibility is deliberately cultivated, the motive can become distorted.

Public goodness becomes morally dangerous when it does several things:

1. **It turns duty into display.**  
   The act is no longer done for God’s sake or for the other person’s sake, but for an audience.

2. **It substitutes human approval for divine approval.**  
   The giver seeks the Father’s reward while actually living by the applause of men.

3. **It corrupts the moral agent.**  
   Pride, vanity, and self-righteousness enter what otherwise would be a humble act.

4. **It can harm the recipients of charity.**  
   Public almsgiving can make charity a social transaction, forcing gratitude, creating shame, or treating the poor as props in the giver’s moral performance.

5. **It creates a false moral economy.**  
   Goodness becomes something to be seen, rewarded, and repaid socially, rather than something freely given before God.

The moral line, then, is not:

> secret = good  
> public = bad

The line is:

> God-centered motive = good  
> self-display motive = dangerous

A public act done with humility, for the good of others, and before God, is not condemned merely because people see it. But a good act done “before men” in order to be seen by them has changed its nature. It may look like alms, but it is really the worship of reputation.

So the answer to your question is this:

**The verse does not make visibility itself the moral problem. It makes the desire to be seen the problem. Public goodness is dangerous when the desire for human recognition becomes the motive, the end, or the true reward of the act.**
