The Hebrew of Proverbs 3:5–6 calls for a **total, reordered trust**—one that relocates the center of gravity from the self to God without demolishing the faculties God gave the self. To see this, we must look at what the text actually asks us to distrust.

### 1. The Grammar of Distrust
The command *“lean not unto thine own understanding”* (וְאֶל־בִּינָתְךָ אַל־תִּשָּׁעֵן, *v’el-binatekha al-tishsha’en*) uses the verb **שָׁעַן** (*sha’an*), “to lean upon” or “to support oneself by.” It pictures a person resting their weight on a staff. The proverb forbids making your own **בִּינָה** (*binah*)—your discernment, insight, practical wisdom—the staff that bears your full weight.

But notice the first clause: *“Trust in the Lord with all thine heart”* (בְּטַח בַּיהוָה בְּכָל־לִבֶּךָ). The Hebrew **לֵב** (*lev*) is not mere emotion; it is the seat of will, intellect, and desire—the whole person. The trust demanded is therefore **integrated and ultimate**: you give God the “center of gravity” of your existence, not just a compartment labeled “religious.”

### 2. Humble Self-Distrust vs. Anti-Intellectualism
The crucial distinction lies in what is being distrusted: **the self as *ultimate* foundation** versus **the self as *instrumental* agent.**

**Humble self-distrust** is an epistemological and existential posture. It recognizes that human *binah* is:
- **Finitude**: Created, limited, and affected by the fall (Romans 1:21–22). It can discern means but often misidentifies ends.
- **Dependence**: We are creatures, not Creator; contingent, not self-existent. To trust in our own understanding *absolutely* is to build a house on sand (Matthew 7:26).
- **Penultimate**: Our reasoning is a gift to be used, but it cannot be the ground of meaning, morality, or salvation.

This is not a suspicion of thinking itself, but a **suspicion of self-sufficiency**. As Augustine put it, *“I believe in order to understand”* (*credo ut intelligam*). The mind is real and necessary, but it is a servant, not a sovereign. We distrust ourselves in the way a climber distrusts their own grip while still using their hands: not because hands are bad, but because the cliff is high and the fall is fatal.

**Anti-intellectualism**, by contrast, is a **rejection of the instrument**. It treats *binah* as inherently corrupting, dangerous, or spiritually irrelevant. It confuses the Creator with the creature by assuming that because we are fallen, our minds are useless. This is a form of **fideism**—faith without reason—which Scripture never commends. Proverbs itself is a book of *wisdom* (*hokmah*), a genre that assumes the mind is capable of grasping truth when rightly oriented toward God (“The fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom,” Prov 9:10).

### 3. The Positive Vision: Acknowledgment in “All Thy Ways”
Verse 6 provides the corrective: *“In all thy ways acknowledge him”* (בְּכָל־דְּרָכֶיךָ דָעֵהוּ). The Hebrew **דָּעֵהוּ** (*da’ehu*, from *yada’*, “to know”) implies intimate, experiential knowledge—not just intellectual assent, but a life oriented around God’s reality.

**“All thy ways”** (*kol-derakhekha*) is comprehensive: your career, your relationships, your doubts, your calculations, your art, your grief. This excludes both errors:
- Against **self-reliance**: You do not carve out “secular” spaces where God is irrelevant; every path is under his gaze.
- Against **anti-intellectualism**: You do not abandon the “ways” of reason, science, or prudence; you *acknowledge God within them*. The mathematician, the parent, the engineer—all are called to think God’s thoughts after him in their respective domains.

### 4. The Synthesis: A Thinking Trust
The trust of Proverbs 3 is therefore **robustly intellectual yet radically humble**. It is the trust of one who uses their mind vigorously but holds it loosely. As C.S. Lewis might say, it is not a “leap in the dark” but a “leap in the light”—the light of God’s revelation illuminating the path, while the feet still walk by faith.

- **Humble self-distrust** says: *“My understanding is real, but it is not ultimate. I need God to interpret my experiences, to correct my biases, and to supply the wisdom I lack. I think, therefore I trust—not in my thinking, but in the God who gave me the capacity to think.”*
- **Anti-intellectualism** says: *“My understanding is unreal or wicked. I must stop thinking to be spiritual.”*

The proverb rejects the second by commanding the first. It asks you to be **dependent** without being **dumb**, **submissive** without being **passive**. When you “acknowledge him in all thy ways,” you bring your *binah*—your best discernment, your sharpest questions, your most careful plans—before God, not as an idol to worship, but as an offering to consecrate. And in that posture, “he shall direct thy paths” (וְהוּא יְיַשֵּׁר אֹרְחֹתֶיךָ): not by bypassing your mind, but by straightening the road beneath your feet, so that your thinking and your walking finally align with the One who is the Way.
