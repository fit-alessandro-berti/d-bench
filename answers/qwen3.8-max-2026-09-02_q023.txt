Isaiah 55:6–7 presents repentance as a whole-person turning to God. It is not only sorrow for sin, nor only a change of behavior, but a renunciation of both the sinful road and the sinful mindset.

### 1. Why both “way” and “thoughts”?

The verse says:

> “Let the wicked forsake his way, and the unrighteous man his thoughts.”

In Hebrew poetry, the two lines interpret one another. The “wicked” and the “unrighteous” are not two unrelated classes of people needing two unrelated remedies; rather, both are called to forsake both “way” and “thoughts.” Sin is addressed at two levels:

- **“Way”** refers to the outward course of life: actions, habits, practices, relationships, patterns of behavior, public conduct, and the direction in which one is walking.
- **“Thoughts”** refers to the inward life: intentions, plans, imaginations, desires, assumptions, motives, self-talk, and the inner reasoning that shapes behavior.

So repentance is not merely external reformation. It must reach the inner world from which sin proceeds. A person may change outward conduct while still cherishing the same desires, resentments, fantasies, or self-justifying thoughts. Conversely, a person may feel inward sorrow or religious emotion while refusing to abandon sinful practices. Isaiah rejects both half-measures.

Repentance here is a forsaking: a deliberate turning away. But it is also a returning:

> “Let him return unto the Lord.”

True repentance is not only turning *from* sin; it is turning *to* God. Forsaking without returning can become mere moralism. Returning without forsaking can become presumption. Biblical repentance is the movement of the whole person away from sin and toward God.

### 2. What this implies about outward conduct

The mention of “way” makes clear that outward conduct matters. God is not content with inner spirituality that leaves behavior unchanged. If someone claims to seek the Lord but continues in dishonesty, sexual immorality, cruelty, greed, oppression, bitterness, or deceit, the “way” has not been forsaken.

The text does not allow us to reduce religion to private sincerity. The wicked must abandon the wicked way. Sin is not only a condition within us; it expresses itself in choices, relationships, and actions. Repentance therefore affects how we live, what we do, what we practice, and where we walk.

Yet the text also prevents us from reducing repentance to mere behavior modification. A person can outwardly conform without inwardly changing. God requires more than cleaned-up conduct; He requires truth in the inward being.

### 3. What this implies about inward imagination

The mention of “thoughts” is especially searching. Sin is not only what is done publicly; it also begins in the hidden interior life. The “thoughts” include:

- secret desires,
- rehearsed fantasies,
- proud ambitions,
- lustful imaginations,
- resentful memories,
- covetous plans,
- self-pitying narratives,
- thoughts that justify oneself and blame others,
- thoughts that deny God’s goodness or mercy.

These are not morally neutral. In Scripture, the inner life is a real arena of obedience and disobedience. The unrighteous person must forsake unrighteous thoughts. This means repenting not only of what we have done, but of what we have entertained, cultivated, delighted in, or used to feed the soul.

This is why repentance can be so deep. It is not enough to say, “I stopped doing it.” The heart must also say, “Lord, cleanse me from the desire, the imagination, and the thought-pattern that led me there.” Outward sin is often the fruit; inward thought is often the root. God deals with both.

### 4. The connection with God’s thoughts and ways

The wider context is important. Immediately after this call to repentance, God says:

> “For my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways, saith the Lord” (Isaiah 55:8).

The contrast is striking. The wicked have their “way”; the unrighteous have their “thoughts.” But God’s ways and thoughts are higher. Repentance, then, is the abandonment of our own crooked ways and self-generated thoughts in order to receive God’s way and God’s thoughts.

This means repentance is not only moral correction; it is the reorientation of the whole person toward the mind and will of God. We stop living according to our own limited, fallen imagination and submit to God’s higher wisdom.

### 5. The possibility of mercy

The most glorious part of the passage is the promise:

> “Let him return unto the Lord, and he will have mercy upon him; and to our God, for he will abundantly pardon.”

This shows that repentance is not a cruel demand. It is a doorway into mercy. God does not call people to forsake their way in order to crush them; He calls them to return because He is ready to show compassion.

Several things are implied:

1. **Mercy is available to the wicked and unrighteous.**  
   The passage does not address only respectable people. It speaks directly to the wicked and unrighteous. No one is excluded from this invitation while the opportunity remains.

2. **Mercy is received through repentance, not earned by repentance.**  
   The text does not say, “Forsake your way, and you will deserve forgiveness.” It says, “Return unto the Lord, and He will have mercy.” Repentance is the posture in which mercy is received, not the price by which mercy is purchased.

3. **God’s pardon is abundant.**  
   The phrase “abundantly pardon” is powerful. It means God’s forgiveness is rich, full, and overflowing. It corresponds to the depth of sin. If sin has multiplied in outward ways and inward thoughts, grace can multiply in pardon.

4. **There is urgency.**  
   The call says, “Seek ye the Lord while he may be found, call ye upon him while he is near.” Mercy is real, but the opportunity must not be presumed upon. The time to repent is now. Hardening, delay, and indifference are dangerous because they may cause one to miss the moment of divine nearness.

### Summary

Repentance involves both “way” and “thoughts” because sin affects both conduct and imagination. God requires more than outward reform without inner renewal, and more than inner remorse without outward change. He calls the sinner to abandon the sinful path and the sinful mind, and to return to Him.

The implication is both solemn and hopeful:

- **Solemn**, because God sees not only our actions but our inner thoughts, fantasies, and motives.
- **Hopeful**, because the same God who requires repentance promises mercy and abundant pardon to all who truly return to Him.

In short: outward conduct must be forsaken, inward imagination must be cleansed, and mercy is abundantly available to those who seek the Lord while He is near.
