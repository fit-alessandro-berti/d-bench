While all the listed traits are visibly present in the passage, they exist in a causal hierarchy. 

* **Anger and envy** are the immediate emotional *symptoms*.
* **Wounded fairness** is the cognitive *framework* through which he views the situation.
* **Pride in obedience** is the *foundation* of his identity.

However, the elder brother’s **deepest problem is a fundamental alienation from the Father’s heart, which manifests as an incompatibility with—and discomfort with—mercy itself.**

Here is why this is the deepest root:

---

### 1. He views sonship as a transaction, not a relationship
Notice the revealing language the elder brother uses in verse 29:
> *“Lo, these many years do I **serve** [literally slaved for, Greek: douleuō] thee, neither transgressed I at any time thy commandment...”*

He does not view himself as a cherished son; he views himself as a faithful hired hand. He believes the Father's favor and blessings are **wages** earned through obedience, not gifts given out of paternal love. 

Because his entire identity rests on merit, **grace is an existential threat to him**. If the Father can give the fatted calf freely to a ruined, repentant prodigal, then the elder brother’s currency of performative obedience is rendered worthless.

### 2. He is spiritually as lost as his brother, but "at home"
The younger brother wanted the father’s goods without the father (taking his inheritance to a distant country). The elder brother **wants the exact same thing**, but he attempted to secure it by staying home and following the rules:
> *“...thou never gavest me a kid, that I might make merry with my friends.”*

He didn’t want to celebrate *with the Father*; he wanted resources from the Father to celebrate *apart* from him. He lived under the Father’s roof, yet remained a total stranger to the Father’s character.

### 3. His rejection of mercy fractures the family
Mercy is the beating heart of the Father, and because the elder brother rejects mercy, he separates himself from the family:
* **He disowns his brother:** He refuses to call him brother, referring to him coldly as *“this thy son”* (v. 30).
* **He isolates himself from the celebration:** The father must come outside to plead with him, just as the father ran outside to embrace the younger son. 
* **He cannot share the Father’s joy:** The Father’s ultimate logic is, *“It was meet [fitting/necessary] that we should make merry, and be glad: for this thy brother was dead, and is alive again”* (v. 32). The elder brother would rather his brother remain dead than see justice bent by grace.

---

### Conclusion
The elder brother’s deepest crisis is **discomfort with mercy, born of self-righteous legalism.** 

Jesus aimed this parable directly at the Pharisees and scribes who were muttering against Him for eating with sinners (Luke 15:1–2). The tragedy of the elder brother is that **he is kept out of the banquet not by his sins, but by his "righteousness."** His pride in his record blinded him to the reality that everything the Father had was already his by grace (v. 31), leaving him standing outside in the dark, cold and bitter against the scandal of unconditional love.
