**The posture required is that of someone who stands beside the fallen person, not above them.** Paul asks for correction by a fellow member of the family ("brethren") who knows they are made of the same material as the one who fell.

## What the wording shows

- **"Overtaken in a fault."** The person is described as caught or tripped up, and the word for "fault" suggests a false step. Paul frames the offender as someone ensnared, not as an enemy.
- **"Ye which are spiritual."** This is not a superior class. In context it means those walking by the Spirit (5:16–25), whose fruit includes meekness (5:23). Handling another's failure is where spirituality is tested, not where it is displayed.
- **"Restore."** The Greek verb (*katartizō*) is used for mending nets and was a term for setting a dislocated bone. The goal is to make the person whole and functional again, not to punish, expose, or win.
- **"Considering thyself."** Paul shifts from the plural ("ye") to the singular ("thyself"). Restoration is the community's work, but self-examination cannot be delegated.

## Why the three belong together

Each element guards against a corruption of the others:

- **Restoration without meekness** has the right aim and the wrong touch. A bone must be set gently. Harshness re-injures, and it often turns correction into domination.
- **Meekness without restoration** is sentimentality. It is gentle, but it leaves the person in the fault. Paul commands action.
- **Self-suspicion without restoration** becomes paralysis: "Who am I to say anything?" Paul does not say that because you are fallible you should not correct. He says to correct *as* one who is fallible.
- **Restoration without self-suspicion** is the most dangerous. Another's fall becomes an occasion for self-elevation, the "vain glory" Paul has just warned against (5:26) and the self-deception he names next (6:3). The corrector may avoid the original fault and fall into pride, which is arguably worse.

## The deeper logic

Self-suspicion is what makes the meekness real rather than a technique. Gentleness that comes from knowing "this could be me" is honest. Gentleness performed from assumed superiority is condescension. Meekness in turn is what makes restoration possible, because people can only receive correction from someone who is not using their failure against them.

The aim (restoration) sets the manner (meekness), and the manner is sustained by self-knowledge (considering thyself). This is why the next verse follows naturally: "Bear ye one another's burdens." Correction, for Paul, is a form of burden-bearing, not a form of judgment.
