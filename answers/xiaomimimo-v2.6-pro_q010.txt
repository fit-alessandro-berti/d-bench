## The Power of Descent

The passage praises **kenotic power** — power expressed as voluntary self-emptying. Christ's authority is demonstrated precisely by *laying it down*: omnipotence that chooses helplessness, kingship expressed as service, and glory displayed as suffering. The direction is relentlessly *downward*. Nothing is seized, nothing is fought for. Every step of the hymn is a further step of descent — from the form of God, to the form of a servant, to the likeness of men, to the death of a criminal. This is not power as force but power as gift, authority as self-giving, might expressed through restraint.

## Why Humility in the Great Feels Unsettling

This is where the passage cuts deep — and the discomfort is not accidental.

**Open domination is legible.** When someone asserts raw power over you, your options are clear: submit or resist. You know where you stand. The oppressor gives you a role — the role of the one fighting back. Your anger has a target. Your narrative of justified resistance holds together.

**But when the one who has every right to dominate instead kneels before you, everything collapses.** The categories you relied on no longer work. Specifically:

- **Your alibi for self-will evaporates.** If the powerful *force* you into submission, you can build an identity around being victimized or around righteous defiance. But if the powerful *serve you and suffer for you* — voluntarily, when they could do otherwise — then whatever you do in response reveals something about **you**, not about them. Your hostility can no longer be disguised as justice.

- **It demands freedom rather than compulsion.** Domination leaves no choice; it dictates the terms. But humility in the powerful opens a space in which **you must choose how to respond**, and that is far more terrifying than any chains. Freedom means responsibility. It means you cannot hide.

- **It exposes the violence at the foundation of our ordinary dealings with each other.** As Girard would argue, Christ's refusal to retaliate breaks the entire cycle of reciprocal power — and by doing so, it *shows* how deeply that cycle structures human life. You are left with nothing to mirror, no opponent to push against, no enemy to define yourself over against.

- **It creates unbearable cognitive dissonance.** We have deep assumptions that power behaves like power — it asserts, it dominates, it protects itself. When authority voluntarily takes the lowest place, our mental framework groans. It feels wrong — not morally wrong but *structurally* wrong, like a law of nature has been violated.

- **It implicates you in love.** Open domination can be rejected on clean terms. But self-emptying love extended toward you without demand removes the safe distance. It says, in effect: *I am not doing this to control you. What now?* And the answer to that question is the most frightening thing in the world, because it is entirely yours.

This is the existential terror that runs through the tradition — from the Psalms ("*Kiss the Son, lest he be angry*") to Dostoevsky's Grand Inquisitor, who explains that humanity rejected Christ's return precisely *because* of His gentleness, preferring authority that manipulates over love that liberates. The Inquisitor understood: **people find comfortable tyranny more bearable than unsettling freedom.**

The humility-joined-to-greatness in this passage is not safe. It does not let anyone — supporter or opponent — remain unchanged. It does not affirm you as you are. It offers something far more dangerous: **the possibility of a response worthy of the freedom it creates.** And that is why it can feel less like comfort and more like judgment — not the judgment of condemnation, but the judgment of being truly seen and truly free, with no one to blame.
