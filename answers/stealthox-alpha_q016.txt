All five are present in the text—but they're not rivals. They're layers, and the question is which layer is load-bearing.

**Anger is only the surface.** It's the visible eruption, not the cause. The verse itself pairs it with the more revealing detail: he "would not go in." Something upstream is producing the rage.

**Envy and wounded fairness form the middle layer**, and the text gives us the mechanism: he thinks in ledgers. "These many years do I serve thee"—the Greek (*douleuō*) is the language of a slave or hired hand, not a son. "Neither transgressed I at any time thy commandment"—a claim of flawless performance that is almost certainly self-deception, which tells us his identity rests on a fiction of perfect accounting. Notice too that he knows about the harlots. He wasn't there. He has been keeping meticulous records of his brother's sins for years. And notice the contempt in "this *thy* son"—he refuses the word "brother," while the father quietly restores it: "this *thy* brother."

**Pride in obedience is the engine**, but look closely at the obedience itself. It was never filial—it was contractual. He obeyed for wages, deferred gratification, an expected payout. The proof is devastating: "thou never gavest me a kid, that I might make merry with my friends." The father's reply exposes the lie: "**All that I have is thine.**" The kid was always available. He never asked—not because the father withheld, but because a man who relates to his father as an employer doesn't feel free to take what a son simply possesses. The younger brother asked shamelessly and received everything; the elder never asked and resented what he never sought.

**So we arrive at the root: discomfort with mercy itself—and here's why it cuts deepest.** If standing with the father is earned, then the brother's restoration without payment is not merely generous; it is an *injustice against the elder*. It devalues his life's labor. Worse: if the father's love turns out to be free, then the entire account book is worthless—and with it, the elder's whole self. His anger is self-defense. He cannot come into the feast, because coming in would mean admitting he never earned anything and was loved anyway—which would have been the best news of his life, had he ever believed it.

This is why the younger brother's lostness was safer than his own. The younger broke the rules and *knew* he was lost; the elder kept the rules and didn't know. One was far away in a distant country; the other was far away in the driveway—physically home, existentially outside, listening to music he refused to join.

Two final observations seal the diagnosis:

1. **The father goes out to him too.** Just as he ran to the younger, he "entreated" the elder—the same pursuing love. And the parable ends *unresolved*, with the elder still outside. That open door is aimed directly at the murmuring scribes and Pharisees of Luke 15:1–2, for whom the elder brother is the mirror.

2. **He is the only character in Luke 15 who cannot rejoice.** The shepherd calls his neighbors, the woman calls her friends, the father kills the fatted calf—joy is the parable's refrain. The elder brother alone hears the music and stands in the dark. Inability to rejoice over mercy shown to another is the telltale signature of a heart still running on merit.

So: anger, envy, wounded fairness, and pride are the scaffolding. Discomfort with mercy is the wall. But beneath them all is the truest description of his deepest problem: **he lived for years in his father's house without ever knowing his father's heart**—a son who thought he was a servant, serving for a wage that was never the point, while the inheritance and the feast were his all along.
