The elder brother’s deepest problem is **not simply that he is angry, but that he sees his father’s mercy to his brother as an injustice to himself**. Anger is what he feels; wounded fairness is how he explains it. Pride in his obedience gives that complaint its force: “these many years do I serve thee.” He reasons that if faithfulness never earned him even a kid, unfaithfulness should certainly not earn his brother the fatted calf.

The father does not belittle those years of faithfulness: “Son, thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine.” Instead, he challenges the premise that the feast is something either son has earned. It is not a verdict on the younger son’s conduct. It is a celebration of his return.

The sharpest exchange is between **“this thy son”** and **“this thy brother.”** The elder brother asks his father to look at what the younger son has done; the father asks him to remember who the younger son is to him. So of the choices, *discomfort with mercy* comes closest—but specifically with mercy shown to someone he judges less deserving than himself. The parable ends without telling us whether he goes in. The invitation is to rejoice in his brother’s return without hearing it as a denial of his own worth.
