The decisive clue is Luke’s own explanatory heading:

> “And he spake this parable unto certain **which trusted in themselves that they were righteous**, and **despised others**”  
> — Luke 18:9

So the Pharisee is not condemned primarily because he taught a formal theological heresy. He is condemned because his piety had become a structure of **self-trust**, and self-trust produces **contempt for others**.

A good summary would be:

> **Pride is the root.**  
> **Contempt is its fruit.**  
> **Comparison is its method.**  
> **Religious self-display is its form.**  
> **A false practical doctrine of self-justification is its implicit teaching.**

---

## 1. What exactly condemns the Pharisee?

### A. Not false doctrine in the narrow sense

The Pharisee says true things. He thanks God. He does not identify himself with extortioners, adulterers, or the unjust. He fasts. He tithes. His external religious observance is, on the face of it, admirable.

Luke’s text does not say, “This man was a heretic.” It says he “trusted in himself that he was righteous.” The problem is not that he denies God’s existence, God’s law, or God’s worship. The problem is that he turns devotion into a claim of status before God.

He uses God as the audience for his self-congratulation rather than as the Lord before whom he kneels.

---

### B. The root sin: pride, or self-trust in one’s own righteousness

The Pharisee’s prayer is striking because it begins:

> “God, I thank thee, that I am not as other men are…”

He addresses God, but the content is overwhelmingly about himself. The repeated “I” is telling:

- “I am not…”
- “I fast…”
- “I give tithes…”

This is a prayer that looks devout but is actually self-referential. He stands before God as though he has something to show. He offers a résumé. He presents credentials.

The sin is not simply that he feels good about himself. The deeper sin is that he feels **sufficient before God on the basis of his own performance**. He assumes that his moral and religious record makes him exempt from the need for mercy.

That is the heart of the condemnation: **he trusts himself, not God’s mercy.**

---

### C. Comparison becomes a spiritual poison when it becomes contempt

The Pharisee compares himself with “other men,” and then with “this publican.”

Comparison in itself is not automatically sinful. Jesus tells the whole parable by comparing the Pharisee and the publican. The issue is the function of comparison.

The Pharisee’s comparison is not:

> “God, help me become more like You.”

It is:

> “God, see how much better I am than those others.”

He uses others as a ladder beneath himself. His comparison does not produce gratitude or repentance; it produces hierarchy. He does not merely notice sin; he uses other people’s sin to confirm his own righteousness.

That is why Luke 18:9 says he “despised others.” The Greek idea in “despised” is something like “to set at nothing,” “to treat as worthless,” or “to regard as of no account.” His contempt is not a minor fault attached to otherwise acceptable religion. It is proof that his religion has made him cruel rather than humble.

---

### D. Virtue used as self-display

The Pharisee’s fasting and tithing are not condemned in themselves. They are real acts of devotion. But in his prayer they are displayed as badges of identity.

He is not saying, “God, please help me keep the law.”  
He is saying, “God, observe that I have kept the law.”

That is the danger: **virtue becomes a trophy rather than a gift.**  
The good deeds become a wall between him and God instead of a path toward God.

He turns religious performance into spiritual exhibition. Even if no human audience is explicitly mentioned, he is displaying himself before God, as though God must be impressed by his moral accounting. His prayer is not petition; it is presentation.

---

### E. The implicit false doctrine: self-justification

If we want to call this “false doctrine,” we should specify the doctrine: **a practical theology of works-righteousness**, or more sharply, a self-justifying religious consciousness.

The Pharisee may not have formulated a heresy. But his life and prayer embody a false doctrine:

> “I can be justified before God by my own religious performance.”

That is the doctrine the parable opposes.

He treats righteousness as something he possesses, not as something he receives from God. He treats God as a witness to his moral record, not as the merciful Lord who must grant him forgiveness.

So his error is less a speculative heresy than a lived religious lie: that he does not need mercy.

---

## 2. Why is the tax collector’s posture spiritually truer?

### A. He stands before God with empty hands

The publican does not offer a moral inventory. He does not list his fasting or tithing. He does not compare himself with anyone. He does not point to another and say, “At least I am not that man.”

Instead, he says:

> “God be merciful to me, a sinner.”

In Greek, the verb is powerful: **ἱλάσθητι** — “be merciful,” “be propitious,” “show atoning mercy.” In a temple setting, this has strong overtones. The publican is asking God to deal with him mercifully rather than according to his own righteousness.

He comes as a petitioner, not as a plaintiff.  
He comes as one who needs mercy, not as one who demands recognition.

---

### B. His posture matches the truth of his condition

The Pharisee may know many true things about his own behavior: he fasts, he tithes, he avoids certain sins. But he does not know the decisive truth about himself: that he stands before God as a sinner in need of mercy.

The publican may know less theology, but he knows the truth of his condition. He knows he is a sinner. He knows he cannot present a righteous case. He knows he must throw himself on God’s mercy.

That is what makes his prayer spiritually truer.

Spiritual truth here is not merely accurate moral reporting. It is accurate self-understanding before God. The publican’s honesty about his need is more true than the Pharisee’s accurate description of his religious performances.

The Pharisee’s facts may be true.  
His self-understanding is false.

---

### C. He does not make his humility a new self-display

The publican stands “afar off.” He will not lift up his eyes. He smites his breast. This is the body language of shame, grief, and unworthiness before God.

His prayer is short:

> “God be merciful to me, a sinner.”

Notice what is absent:

- No comparison.
- No list of virtues.
- No demand for recognition.
- No claim to status.
- No contempt for others.
- No self-congratulatory humility.

He is not saying, “Look how humble I am.”  
He is saying, “God, have mercy.”

If he had turned his humility into a possession — “God, I thank You that I am so penitent” — he would have become Pharisee-like again. But his prayer avoids that. It is all direction toward God’s mercy and admission of his own need.

---

### D. His posture opens him to justification

Jesus says:

> “I tell you, this man went down to his house justified rather than the other…”

The word “justified” is legal: declared righteous, set in right standing before God. The publican goes down **justified**, not because he is morally superior to the Pharisee. The parable does not say tax collectors are secretly more ethical than Pharisees. Rather, the publican is justified because he does not justify himself.

The Pharisee has already justified himself in his own eyes. That is his problem. He leaves the temple with the verdict he already gave himself.

The publican leaves with God’s verdict.

He comes without defense, and therefore God can grant him mercy.

---

## 3. So which one is the real issue?

If we must choose among the options:

- **False doctrine?**  
  Not primarily a formal doctrinal error, but an implicit false practical theology: the belief that human righteousness can justify one before God.

- **Pride?**  
  Yes. This is the root: trusting in himself that he is righteous.

- **Comparison?**  
  Yes, as the instrument of pride: defining himself by looking down on others rather than by looking toward God.

- **Contempt?**  
  Yes, as the clearest fruit: he despises others, treating them as nothing.

- **Virtue as self-display?**  
  Yes, as the religious form: his good deeds become evidence offered for self-exaltation rather than gifts before God.

The core is **self-righteousness**: using what is good — fasting, tithing, moral discipline, gratitude — as proof that one does not need mercy.

---

## 4. The sharpest contrast

The two prayers reveal the contrast:

**The Pharisee:**  
> “God, I thank thee, that I am not as other men…”

**The publican:**  
> “God be merciful to me, a sinner.”

The Pharisee’s prayer is a prayer of **possession**: what he has, what he is not, what he does.

The publican’s prayer is a prayer of **dependence**: what he needs, what he is, and who God is in mercy.

The Pharisee says, in effect:  
> “I am above mercy.”

The publican says, in effect:  
> “I cannot live apart from mercy.”

And Luke’s parable says that the second man, not the first, goes home justified.

---

## One-sentence answer

The Pharisee is condemned not because he lacks religion, but because he turns his religion into self-justification, contempt, and spiritual display; the tax collector is spiritually truer because he comes before God without a résumé, without comparison, and without self-confidence, asking for mercy alone.
