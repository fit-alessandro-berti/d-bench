In modern usage, “Judge not” is frequently treated as a command to suspend moral reasoning altogether—as if Jesus were telling his listeners to shrug at cruelty, dishonesty, or harm and say, *“Who am I to say what’s right or wrong?”* 

Yet reading Luke 6:37 that way actually makes the verse collapse in on itself. Far from abolishing moral standards, Jesus’ words rely on them, while drawing a sharp line between **moral discernment** and **the appetite to condemn**.

---

### 1. Why This Does Not Mean Moral Judgments Never Matter

**First, the command to *forgive* requires moral judgment.**
Look at the third clause of the verse: *“forgive, and ye shall be forgiven.”* You cannot forgive someone unless a real moral wrong has actually occurred. If moral judgments didn’t matter, there would be nothing to forgive—only behaviors to ignore, excuse, or rationalize. Forgiveness is profoundly moral: it looks squarely at an offense, names it as wrong, refuses to pretend it didn’t hurt, and yet chooses to release the offender from vengeance. 

**Second, the immediate context demands moral evaluation.**
Luke 6:37 sits in the middle of Jesus’ “Sermon on the Plain,” which is saturated with moral distinctions:
* **Naming enemies and abuse:** A few verses earlier (vv. 27–28), Jesus tells his followers to love their enemies and pray for those who mistreat them. You have to exercise moral judgment just to recognize that an action *is* mistreatment rather than love.
* **Specks and logs:** Four verses later (vv. 41–42), Jesus uses the famous image of taking the wooden beam out of your own eye *so that you can see clearly to take the speck out of your brother’s eye*. Notice the goal isn't to pretend the speck isn't there; the goal is clear-sighted, humble help.
* **Inspecting fruit:** Immediately after that (vv. 43–45), Jesus says that a good tree doesn’t bear bad fruit, and each tree is known by its own fruit. Recognizing the difference between “good” and “bad” fruit is the very definition of moral discernment.

Jesus is clearly not telling people to gouge out their moral eyesight. He is confronting what we *do* when we see moral failure, and *why* we are looking for it in the first place.

---

### 2. Discernment vs. The Appetite to Condemn

In the original Greek, the movement from “judge not” (*krinete*) to “condemn not” (*katadikazete*) clarifies Jesus’ target. He is not forbidding ethical evaluation; he is forbidding us from climbing into God’s courtroom seat to pass final sentence on human beings. 

The difference between healthy discernment and the appetite to condemn shows up in four distinct ways:

#### 1. Diagnosis vs. Execution
**Discernment** acts like a physician; **condemnation** acts like a hangman. A doctor has to make severe, unflinching judgments: *This tissue is diseased; this habit is killing you.* To pretend otherwise isn't compassionate—it’s negligent. But the doctor diagnoses the sickness in order to save the patient. Condemnation, by contrast, uses the sickness as a reason to write the patient off. Discernment attacks the problem; condemnation attacks the person's worth.

#### 2. Humility vs. Ego-Inflation
There is a dark pleasure in condemning others—what the prompt rightly calls an *appetite*. When we condemn someone, we subtly elevate ourselves. Their failure becomes a pedestal for our own righteousness (*“I may have flaws, at least I would never do that”*). We crave the moral high ground. 

**Discernment**, however, operates without that ego-boost. Because it remembers the “log” in its own eye, it recognizes human frailty as a shared condition. Discernment says, *“That action is wrong and dangerous, and under the right pressures, I am capable of my own versions of ruin.”* Where condemnation feels smug or thrilling, true discernment often feels heavy or sorrowful.

#### 3. Openness vs. Finality
The appetite to condemn reduces a whole, complex human being down to their worst moment. It treats a person’s current sin as the final word on who they are—closing the book on their future. 

**Discernment** tells the truth about the present without foreclosing on the future. It can say, *“You lied to me, and therefore I cannot trust you with my money right now,”* (a necessary, discerning boundary) while refusing to say, *“You are irredeemable trash who deserves to be destroyed”* (condemnation). Discernment leaves room for repentance, grace, and change; condemnation locks the cell door and throws away the key.

#### 4. Accountability vs. Fault-Finding
A discerning person notices wrong when they encounter it, tests ideas and behaviors for wisdom, and protects the vulnerable from harm. A condemning (or censorious) person goes *hunting* for wrong. They interpret ambiguous motives in the worst possible light, keep meticulous ledgers of slights, and feel subtly disappointed if someone they dislike turns out to have acted well.

---

### Summary

Luke 6:37 sits directly beneath verse 36: *“Be ye therefore merciful, as your Father also is merciful.”* God is the ultimate moral realist—nothing evil escapes His notice—and yet His posture toward flawed humans is mercy. 

Therefore, “Judge not” does not mean closing your eyes to the difference between good and evil. **Discernment** is keeping your eyes wide open to the truth; **refusing to condemn** is keeping your heart open to mercy after you see it.
