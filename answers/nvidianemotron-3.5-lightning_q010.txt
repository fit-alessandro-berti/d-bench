The passage praises a form of power often called **kenotic power**—from the Greek *kenosis*, "self-emptying." It is the power of divine love that voluntarily limits itself, takes on the form of a servant, and entrusts itself to obedience and death rather than coercion or control. 

This is not "powerlessness"; it is power with its throttle deliberately pulled, directed toward reconciliation rather than domination. It is the power that creates freedom by surrendering the right to wield it, and it is most fully expressed in the cross, where the appearance of weakness is actually the decisive defeat of evil, sin, and death.

### Why humility joined to greatness feels more unsettling than open domination

1. **It inverts the equation of glory**  
   Open domination says: *I am great, therefore I control.* It is transparent, predictable, and demands only compliance. Humility + greatness says: *I am great, therefore I make myself small.* That inversion ruptures deeply ingrained cultural and psychological scripts. It forces us to ask why we equate authority with visibility, and worth with leverage.

2. **It demands a response, not just submission**  
   Domination can be endured passively; you can submit out of fear and remain unchanged. Kenotic power is alive and transformative. It doesn't allow you to remain a bystander. It confronts you with a standard of self-giving that either reorders your life or provokes resistance—both outcomes are unsettling because they refuse the safety of indifferent compliance.

3. **It exposes the ego’s hunger for power**  
   When power is overt, we can locate it in a person, institution, or system and either obey, oppose, or ignore it. When greatness hides itself in vulnerability, the source of power becomes ambiguous. That ambiguity threatens any worldview that needs power to look like power. It reveals that much of what we call "order" or "stability" is actually just the comfort of known hierarchies.

4. **The cross as a stumbling block**  
   The crucifixion is the ultimate collision of greatness and humility: the King on a throne becomes a criminal on a tree. For those committed to a vision of power as dominance, this is not just disappointing—it is an ontological crisis. It suggests that the most real, most effective, most "divine" power is most fully expressed when it looks like defeat. That undercuts the whole logic of coercive authority.

In short, the power praised is **generative, redemptive, and freely chosen**. It feels unsettling because it doesn't just demand your obedience—it invites you into a redefinition of what greatness, freedom, and God actually look like. It doesn't let you stay where you are, which is precisely why it both disturbs and saves.
