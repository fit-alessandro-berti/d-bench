The passage praises **greatness expressed in service**. The key contrast is between “the form of God” and “the form of a servant.” Christ does not treat equality with God as an advantage to use for his own benefit. Nor is his greatness a disguise that comes off to reveal a servant underneath. In Paul’s portrait, the choice to serve reveals what divine greatness is like.

That can be more unsettling than domination because domination leaves our idea of greatness intact. We may resent the person who takes the highest place and still wish that place were ours. But when someone who could claim it freely takes the servant’s place, the question changes: not “Who deserves to be on top?” but “What is my power for?”

“Let this mind be in you” makes the question personal. The cross is not praised because humiliation is good or because the powerful are entitled to demand obedience from others. The movement runs the other way: the one with the highest claim bears the cost himself.
