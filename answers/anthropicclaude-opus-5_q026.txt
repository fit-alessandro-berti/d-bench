## The chain of metaphors

Notice that Jesus stacks three different images in the space of two verses, and they don't all say the same thing.

**The tree and its fruit** (v. 33, immediately prior): production follows nature. Fruit is diagnostic because it is not chosen — a fig tree does not deliberate about figs. This is the logic behind the rhetorical question in v. 34: "*how can ye*, being evil, speak good things?" That's not an insult but a claim of impossibility.

**The treasure** (θησαυρός): here the register shifts entirely, from botany to economics. A *thēsauros* is both the hoard and the strongroom that holds it — contents and container at once. And a hoard is *accumulated*. Nobody is born with a treasury; it is stocked, deposit by deposit, over years. So the second metaphor smuggles history and agency into what the first metaphor presented as sheer nature.

**The abundance** (περίσσευμα): not simply "contents" but *surplus, overflow, what is left over beyond capacity*. The mouth speaks the spillage. This is subtler than "the heart's contents come out of the mouth" — it suggests speech is what escapes when the interior is too full to contain itself. What you say is the runoff, not the reservoir.

And the verb for the good and evil man's output is ἐκβάλλει — *casts out, throws out, expels*. It's the same verb used a dozen verses earlier for casting out demons. Speech is an expulsion, something forced across a boundary.

## So: symptom, choice, or revelation?

All three, but they are not three competing accounts of the same instant. They are distributed across time.

**Symptom, in the present.** At the moment of speaking, the passage treats words as involuntary indicators. This is the whole force of the encounter: the Pharisees have just alleged that Jesus works by Beelzebul (12:24), and Jesus does not chiefly argue with the claim — he *reads* it. The accusation is a lab result. Notably, they were not confessing anything; they thought they were making a theological point about someone else. That is precisely why the utterance is evidence. Symptoms are informative because they aren't performances.

**Choice, in the past.** The treasury image relocates freedom upstream. The moral drama is not in the split second before you open your mouth; it's in the ten thousand small deposits that determined what would be available to overflow. This is why the passage isn't fatalism dressed as diagnosis: the tree metaphor alone would be deterministic, but treasuries are filled by someone, and — unlike a tree's nature — they can be emptied and restocked. The metaphors are in productive tension, and the tension is the ethics. You are not responsible for what you find in your mouth; you are responsible for what you have been putting in the vault.

**Revelation, at the end.** Verses 36–37, which follow immediately, make this explicit: "every idle word... shall give account thereof in the day of judgment: For by thy words thou shalt be justified, and by thy words thou shalt be condemned." Words become *evidence at a trial*. The eschatological function of speech is disclosure — the mouth as an involuntary witness against its owner. This fits Matthew's persistent interest in the gap between surface and interior (almsgiving in secret, whitewashed tombs, the cup clean outside and foul within). Hypocrisy is possible in Matthew, but it is also unsustainable; the inside eventually surfaces, and speech is the primary leak.

## The hard edge

Two difficulties are worth naming rather than smoothing over.

First, the impossibility claim seems too strong. Can a corrupt person never say anything true or kind? The passage's answer is probably not that the words themselves will always be ugly, but that their *source* determines what they are. The Pharisees say pious things constantly. What Jesus denies is that pious speech from an evil treasury counts as "speaking good things" — the same phonemes, drawn from a different well, are a different act. This is also why the *overflow* image matters: under pressure, when the guard slips, what escapes is the surplus, not the curated sample. The Beelzebul accusation is exactly such a slip.

Second, the passage is uncomfortably close to authorizing us to diagnose each other's hearts from stray remarks — which is a tool of remarkable cruelty in ordinary hands. Two restraints sit in the text itself. The judgment scene in v. 36 is God's, not ours. And the grammar of v. 37 is second person singular: *thy* words justify or condemn *thee*. The instrument is calibrated for self-examination.

## The upshot

The passage denies that speech is a neutral instrument you pick up and use. It is metabolic — an exhalation of whatever you have been feeding on. Which means the practical counsel it implies is not "watch your words" (though Matthew says that too) but something stranger and slower: mind your deposits. Words are symptoms of a history of choices, and they will be read back to you as revelation.
