**Repentance here requires forsaking both the outward “way” (conduct, path of life, habits) and the inward “thoughts” (plans, purposes, imaginations, mindset) because sin is not merely behavioral; it is also rooted in the heart and mind.**

“Way” (Hebrew *derek*) points to the lived course of action—the wicked person’s established pattern of behavior. “Thoughts” (Hebrew *machashabah*) points to the inner devisings, intentions, and imaginations that generate and sustain that way. The pairing shows that God requires a comprehensive turning: not cosmetic reform of visible deeds while the heart remains unchanged, and not mere mental regret while the same path continues. Both must be abandoned as the person “returns unto the Lord.”

This implies:
- Outward conduct alone is insufficient; God sees and judges the inner life (cf. the broader biblical insistence that the heart is the wellspring). Superficial morality or ritual without a change of mind and affection does not constitute the repentance described.
- Inward imagination and planning are themselves morally accountable; lingering in wicked thoughts, schemes, or self-justifying narratives keeps a person from the Lord even if external behavior is temporarily restrained.
- Mercy remains possible and is described as abundant (“he will have mercy… he will abundantly pardon”) precisely for those who make this double forsaking and return. The invitation is urgent (“while he may be found… while he is near”) yet open: the wicked and unrighteous are not beyond pardon if they turn wholly to God rather than relying on their own ways or thoughts.

The verse therefore presents repentance as a whole-person reorientation toward God, not partial self-improvement, and grounds the hope of pardon in God’s character rather than in the completeness of the sinner’s prior reform. (Isaiah 55 as a whole frames this within God’s free invitation and the reliability of His word.)
