It is mainly a realism about self-deception, stated in moral rather than merely psychological terms. The point is not “people are gloomy,” but “you are a poor witness about your own motives, so do not treat the heart as the final court.”

In Jeremiah the warning sits inside a larger argument against false confidence. Judah trusts its own religious feelings, slogans, and self-justifications while its conduct tells another story. “Heart” here is not only emotion; it is the inner center of thought, desire, and choice. “Deceitful” means it misrepresents. The stronger phrase behind “desperately wicked” is closer to “gravely sick” or “beyond easy cure”: the problem is not a small blind spot you can fix by introspecting harder. The rhetorical question—“who can know it?”—expects the answer “not you, not fully.”

The next line supplies the alternative witness. God searches the heart and tests the inner life, and he does so in connection with “ways” and “the fruit of doings.” Motive is not dismissed, but it is not left to private report. What you repeatedly do, choose, excuse, and produce is treated as evidence. That is why the passage is not pure pessimism. It is pessimistic about unaided self-knowledge, and confident that judgment does not depend on our self-portrait.

### How to talk about motives
If the heart is an unreliable witness, speech about motives should be humble, provisional, and tied to what can be seen.

- Prefer patterns over inner verdicts. “I keep choosing this” or “this keeps producing that” is safer than “I know my heart is pure” or “I know exactly why you did that.”
- Treat self-explanation as a hypothesis, not a verdict. People can be sincere and still self-deceived. Sincerity is not the same as accuracy.
- Separate confessed motive from claimed knowledge. “This is what I think I wanted” is honest. “No one may question this, because I have searched my heart” overreaches the verse.
- Use outside checks: repeated actions, consequences, wise counsel, and—in the passage’s own frame—God’s judgment rather than one’s feelings of innocence.
- With other people, be slow to narrate their hidden motives. You can name behavior, inconsistency, and fruit without pretending to read the heart. Mind-reading usually flatters the speaker.
- Leave room for mixed motives. The text does not say every impulse is consciously malicious. It says the inner witness is crooked enough that you should not trust it alone.

So the practical rule is simple: talk about motives as partially known, easily edited, and accountable to conduct. Confidence belongs less in “I know my heart” and more in “God knows, and fruit tells.”
