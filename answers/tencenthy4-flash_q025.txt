The short answer is: **the craving to be seen is the problem, not visibility itself**—though Jesus warns that the two are so easily intertwined that he commands us to guard against public display when that is the aim.

Here is a fuller breakdown of what makes public goodness morally dangerous in this verse:

### 1. The issue is intention, not mere observation
The key phrase in the original Greek is *pros to theasthai autois*—literally **“with a view to being seen by them”** or “for the purpose of being observed.” It’s a purpose clause. Jesus isn’t saying that doing good in the presence of others is automatically sinful; he is saying that doing good *in order to* put on a performance is spiritually toxic. The moral danger lies in the **motive**: when the audience becomes the point, the act stops being about love of God and love of neighbor, and starts being about self-promotion.

### 2. Public goodness tempts us to trade rewards
Jesus immediately adds, *“otherwise ye have no reward of your Father which is in heaven.”* In the ancient world, public benefactors often gave alms precisely to gain honor, title, and social standing. Jesus is exposing that transaction: if you do a good deed to get a human reaction, you have already received your “reward” (the applause, the admiration, the reputation). There is nothing left for God to give. The danger is that **public visibility turns generosity into a performance and human approval into the payoff**, which empties the act of its spiritual value.

### 3. It breeds hypocrisy (play-acting)
In the verses right after this (Matthew 6:2, 5, 16), Jesus uses the word *hypokritai*—“hypocrites,” literally “actors” or “play-actors.” The moral danger is that public goodness can make us wear a mask. We start performing righteousness rather than being righteous. The act may look identical on the outside (giving to the poor, praying, fasting), but internally it is hollow because the heart is managing an image rather than seeking God.

### 4. Visibility itself isn’t condemned (the balance of Matthew 5:16)
To be fair to the broader teaching of Jesus, just a chapter earlier he says: *“Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works and glorify your Father in heaven”* (Matthew 5:16). So good works *will* be seen, and that’s not wrong—in fact, it’s expected. The difference is the **goal**: in Matthew 5, the goal is that people glorify God; in Matthew 6:1, the goal is that people glorify *you*. Visibility is the context; the craving for personal acclaim is the sin.

### 5. Why the warning is so sharp
Jesus knows human nature: it is very hard to do something praiseworthy in public without at least a twinge of wanting to be noticed. That’s why he uses such absolute language (“take heed,” “do not,” “no reward”). He is not banning good works in public; he is diagnosing a heart condition that public settings uniquely inflame. The danger is that we can be morally deceived—doing the right thing for the wrong reason and convincing ourselves we are still righteous.

### Summary
- **Visibility** is the *occasion* of the danger.
- **The craving to be seen** (the desire for human validation, honor, and applause) is the *moral danger itself*.
- When that craving takes root, public goodness becomes a hollow performance that forfeits God’s approval and reduces a sacred act to a social transaction.

In practical terms, the verse invites us to examine our hearts: *Would I still do this good deed if nobody ever knew?* If the answer is no, the act may be morally dangerous not because it was seen, but because we did it to be seen.
