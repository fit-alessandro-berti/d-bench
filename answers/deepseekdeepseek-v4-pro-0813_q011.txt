Isaiah moves from **“cease to do evil”** to **“learn to do well”** because biblical repentance has two sides: turning away from evil and turning toward active goodness. “Cease to do evil” is the necessary negative step—stopping the harm—but it is not yet goodness. A person or community can stop doing wrong and still remain passive, self-focused, or indifferent.

So the prophet immediately adds a positive command: **“learn to do well.”** The word “learn” is significant. It implies that goodness is not merely an inner feeling or a one-time decision; it is a discipline to be practiced, cultivated, and formed over time. Goodness must be learned.

The specific examples make this clear: **seek justice, relieve the oppressed, defend the fatherless, plead for the widow**. These are active, outward, social acts of love and justice toward the vulnerable. That is not mere restraint from wrongdoing; it is intentional advocacy and care.

So the text suggests that goodness is **not mere restraint**. It is a practiced form of love and justice. Restraint clears the ground, but love and justice build something on it.
