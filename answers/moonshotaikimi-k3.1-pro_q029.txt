Paul requires a posture of **humble, watchful solidarity**: correction must seek another person’s good without claiming moral superiority or invulnerability for oneself.

- **Restoration defines the purpose.** The aim is to mend and recover the person, not to punish, expose, humiliate, or demonstrate the corrector’s righteousness. This fits the following command to “bear one another’s burdens” (6:2): the offender’s failure becomes a burden to help carry, not merely an offense to condemn.

- **Meekness defines the manner.** Meekness is not weakness, vagueness about sin, or an inability to be firm. It is strength without the need to dominate—correction without harshness, contempt, or pleasure in another’s failure. Those “who are spiritual” are therefore not a sinless elite; their spirituality should be evident in the Spirit’s fruit, including gentleness (5:22–23).

- **Self-suspicion defines the corrector’s attitude toward himself.** “Considering thyself” means examining one’s motives and remembering one’s own vulnerability. Correction itself can become a temptation—to pride, anger, or self-righteousness—and the corrector is also capable of falling. This is not moral relativism or paralysis: Paul assumes that the fault is real and should be addressed. It is a refusal to imagine oneself as inherently above temptation.

These three belong together because they govern correction’s **goal, method, and agent**. Restoration without meekness can become cruel; meekness without restoration can become avoidance; self-examination without correction can become an excuse for doing nothing. Together they make correction an act of care rather than self-exaltation.

Paul thus holds together **moral seriousness and moral humility**: one may be right about the fault while still needing to be corrected in the spirit of one’s correcting. The corrector stands beside the offender as a fellow vulnerable sinner, not above them as a self-sufficient judge.
