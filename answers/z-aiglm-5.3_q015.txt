## The sharpness of the image

The image works by placing an enormous disproportion inside a single, tiny organ. A *karphos* is a splinter of dry straw or wood; a *dokos* is a roof-beam or rafter. Notice that both are wood—the faults are the same *in kind*, differing only in size. The hypocrite is not a different species of sinner confronting a lesser one; he is a magnified version of the very person he is correcting.

Second, the fault sits in the **eye**—the organ of perception. This makes the metaphor self-referential: the defect is lodged in the very instrument the man is using to assess his brother. And the image contains its own psychology, darker than we usually notice: he cannot see the beam *because the beam is what's blocking the view*. Small faults are visible to us; beam-sized ones occlude themselves. The verbs in verse 3 are exact—he "beholds" (stares at, scrutinizes) the mote while "considering not" the rafter. Hypocrisy here is an economy of attention: forensic precision outward, total amnesty inward.

Third, the staging of verse 4. The hypocrite is not a silent critic; he *volunteers his services*. Picture the blocking: a man with a rafter protruding from his face leaning in to perform delicate eye surgery. The comedy is deadpan—no editorial wink—which is precisely why it traps the audience: you laugh at the plank-man, then recognize him. This kind of hyperbole (compare the camel and the needle's eye) survives translation and twenty centuries because it compresses the whole argument into one picture that cannot be unseen. Abstract commands ("avoid hypocrisy") are forgettable; the man with the beam became an idiom.

## The logic that preserves correction

The hinge is the grammar of verse 5: **"first... then."** If correcting others were forbidden, the sentence could end at "cast out the beam out of thine own eye." It doesn't. It runs on: "and *then* shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote out of thy brother's eye." The mote still gets removed. What Jesus corrects is the *sequence*, not the surgery. Note also that he never denies the mote exists. The brother's fault is real, an irritant worth taking out. The passage repositions the judge, not the judged.

What is condemned, then, is the *state of the corrector*, not the act of correcting. "Hypocrite" (*hypokritēs*) is Matthew's word for a stage actor—elsewhere in the Sermon it labels those who give, pray, and fast "to be seen of men" (6:2, 5, 16). The beam-man is performing the role of a sighted ophthalmologist; his correction is theater for an audience, not service to his brother.

There is also an epistemic claim: "then shalt thou see *clearly*." Self-correction is not merely a moral toll; it is a competence requirement. Eye surgery is delicate work—a surgeon with a beam in his own eye will misdiagnose and injure. Only the beam-free see the mote at its true size, which the ratio implies is smaller than the corrector assumed, while his own was larger than he knew. And the man who has removed his own beam becomes a *better* surgeon: he knows what beams feel like, so he works gently. (Paul gives almost a commentary on this verse: "restore such an one in the spirit of meekness; considering thyself," Galatians 6:1.)

Finally, the passage is self-demonstrating. "Thou hypocrite" is itself a sharp moral correction—delivered legitimately, without hypocrisy, because the speaker has no beam. The verse performs exactly what it authorizes. Read this way, 7:1's "judge not" is not a blanket ban but a diagnosis: what is forbidden is condemnatory judgment issuing from unexamined blindness. The Sermon itself presupposes ongoing discernment—only a verse later, "give not that which is holy unto dogs," and soon after, "by their fruits ye shall know them" (7:6, 7:20). Judgment is not abolished; it is made to pass through self-judgment first.

The passage does not say "never operate on another's eye." It says you cannot do fine eye surgery with a rafter in your own face—remove it, and you will be fit for the work.
