**Bitterness and forgiveness stand in direct opposition here because the passage treats them as incompatible ways of relating—one that must be discarded as part of the “old self,” the other that must be practiced as the new self, patterned on God’s own action.**

Ephesians 4 as a whole is about walking worthy of the calling (4:1), maintaining unity in the body (4:3–16), and putting off the old humanity / putting on the new (4:17–24). Verses 25–32 give concrete interpersonal applications. Verse 31 lists a cluster of things to “put away” (ἀποτίθημι, like taking off clothing): bitterness, wrath, anger, clamour, slander, and all malice. Verse 32 immediately commands the opposite posture: kindness, tenderheartedness, and mutual forgiveness “even as God in Christ forgave you.” The juxtaposition is deliberate and sequential—the vices are not merely unfortunate feelings but the very things that destroy the unity and holiness just described; forgiveness is the Christ-shaped alternative that makes the new life possible.

In this context bitterness (πικρία) is more than a fleeting emotion. It is grouped with volitional and verbal sins (anger that erupts, shouting, slander, malice), presented as something believers are responsible to discard. Scripture elsewhere treats it as a “root” that can spring up and defile many (Heb 12:15), so it functions as an allowed or cultivated posture of the heart that readily becomes habitual and communal. It is contagious in the sense that it produces the very “clamour and evil speaking” listed next to it; once it takes hold it spreads through speech and attitude and poisons the body. The text therefore does not treat it as an involuntary feeling one merely suffers, nor as a purely private inner state. It is a chosen (or at least tolerated) stance that must be actively put off, precisely because it is incompatible with the forgiveness God has already extended.

The logic is grace-driven rather than merely moralistic: because God has forgiven “for Christ’s sake,” the community is both enabled and obligated to do likewise. Holding bitterness while claiming that forgiveness is incoherent. The passage therefore places them against each other as mutually exclusive ways of being the church.
