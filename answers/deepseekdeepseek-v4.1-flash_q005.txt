Enemy-love is hard to take seriously because it collides with some of our deepest instincts: reciprocity (“you get what you give”), self-protection, group loyalty, and a legitimate desire for justice. It sounds like letting evil win. It has also been badly misused by Christians—used to silence victims, avoid conflict, or demand “forgiveness” without truth or repentance. So suspicion is understandable.

But in Jesus’ framing, enemy-love is not primarily sentimental, and not merely strategic. It is a deeper form of moral and spiritual strength.

**Not sentimental.**  
Sentimentality depends on warm feelings, and warm feelings toward someone who harms you are not commanded. *Agape* love is volitional and concrete. Jesus doesn’t say, “Feel affection for your enemies.” He says, “Bless them… do good to them… pray for them.” You can act in enemy-love without liking the person. It can coexist with truth-telling, boundaries, justice, and protection of the innocent. Sentimental love collapses under persecution; cruciform love does not.

**Not merely strategic.**  
Enemy-love can have strategic effects: it breaks cycles of revenge, exposes the moral bankruptcy of hatred, and creates space for repentance. But if it is only a strategy, it becomes manipulation—love as a technique to get a desired outcome. Jesus grounds it instead in the character of God: “that ye may be the children of your Father… for he maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good.” The point is not “this will work,” but “this is what God is like.” God’s common grace falls on the just and unjust; His children are to resemble Him.

**A deeper moral strength.**  
Enemy-love is the strength to absorb evil without becoming evil. It refuses to let the enemy define your soul. It says, “You may hate me, but I will not hate you back. I will not dehumanize you. I will seek your true good, even while I tell the truth about your wrong.” It trusts God as judge, so it does not have to take vengeance into its own hands (Rom. 12:19). It is cruciform: Jesus prays for his executioners, “Father, forgive them” (Luke 23:34). It overcomes evil with good (Rom. 12:21).

This does **not** mean:
- staying in abusive situations,
- refusing to seek justice,
- pretending wrong is not wrong,
- reconciling without repentance,
- having no boundaries.

It means refusing hatred and revenge, praying for the enemy’s repentance and good, doing good where possible, and leaving ultimate justice to God. Boundaries can be loving. Justice can be loving. Truth can be loving.

So why is it so hard to take seriously? Because it is not a mood or a tactic. It is a death and resurrection of the self. It is the moral strength of children who look like their Father—who gives sun and rain to all—and who choose to overcome evil not by mirroring it, but by redeeming it.
