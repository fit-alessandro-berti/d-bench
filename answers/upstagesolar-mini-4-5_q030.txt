This passage—usually called 1 Corinthians 13:4–7, translated here in the older "charity" idiom—is striking precisely because it is almost entirely negative. Love is described by what it *refuses* to do: it does not boast, puff itself up, behave unseemly, seek its own, become easily provoked, think evil, or rejoice in iniquity. The vision of strength being proposed is one in which the surest test of power is a person's freedom from domination—of others and of oneself.

A few reasons the negations are so central:

**1. Negations expose what pride does.** Pride is not merely a positive trait of self-importance; it is a relational distortion. Boasting and envying are two sides of the same coin: the proud person measures themselves against others and is either inflated or diminished by the comparison. Irritability is what happens when one's honor is at stake; suspicion is what happens when one cannot trust others. By naming these, the text names the concrete mechanisms by which "self" becomes an idol. Love is defined as the corrective: it does not need to rank, resent, or watch for threats.

**2. The negatives are not weakness but a different kind of potency.** The Greek behind "suffers long" and "bears all things" (*hypokopē*, *hypomone*, *pistis*, *elpis*, *hypomenē*) are endurance terms—verbs of holding fast under strain. The portrait proposes that real strength is *capacity to remain in relationship under provocation and uncertainty*. A person who does not seek their own, who is not easily provoked, and who thinks no evil has not become smaller; they have become less brittle, less ruled by reactive self-defense. This is strength of restraint—like a stronghold that needs no walls, or like the patience of someone who has stopped needing to win.

**3. The negatives guard against a counterfeit of love.** A love that tolerates iniquity, rejoices in another's failure, or believes all things in a credulous way is not the love described. The text pairs each negative with a positive: love "rejoiceth in the truth," "believeth all things" (i.e., takes the charitable reading, not naivety), "hopeth all things." The negations are the fence around a positivity that is honest: truthful, hopeful, and durable. Love's strength is shown in *not* being the enemy of truth—even when truth is unflattering.

**4. There is an eschatological horizon.** In context, the list is bracketed by the claim that love "never fails" (v. 8) and the famous "faith, hope, charity, these three abide" (v. 13). The negations describe a love that can be *endured* because it is not internally at war with itself. The proposed strength is therefore not self-assertion but self-giving that remains reliable—consistent over time, not a mood.

So the vision of strength is this: the strongest thing a person can be is *non-coercive*—able to be in truth without boasting, in company without envying, in conflict without resentment, in uncertainty without suspicion. It is the strength of a heart that has stopped treating others as rivals and threats. That is why the passage uses so many negatives: they are not absence of love but the visible shape of a love that has conquered the ego's compulsions.
