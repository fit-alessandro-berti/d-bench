Here is a clear, concise, theologically sound, and pastorally helpful explanation of the inner contradiction exposed in James 3:9–10 and why speech reveals moral disorder quickly—written directly for the question asked, without unnecessary jargon or overstatement.

> **The inner contradiction exposed:**  
> James points out that the *same person* uses their single, common speech faculty (the mouth) for two directly opposed actions/attitudes toward fundamentally related things:  
> 1. **Blessing God** (the Father), who is the ultimate source of all good and holiness.  
> 2. **Cursing people** who are "made after the similitude of God" (i.e., created in God's image—Genesis 1:27; Psalm 8:5; New Testament usage).  
>   
> The contradiction lies in the *logical and moral consistency*: if human beings are made in God's likeness, cursing them is, by definition, cursing a part of God's own reflected image in creation. Saying "bless God" with one part of speech while "cursing" (insulting, dehumanizing, expressing contempt for) fellow humans made in God's image cannot be morally coherent. It treats God (the source) with reverence while treating God’s image-bearers (created in His likeness) with contempt—using the *exact same mouth* for both. It is self-contradictory within the person’s own behavior: the same faculty cannot rightly praise God *and* dishonor His image-bearers without internal moral tension. James calls this “these things ought not so to be.”

> **Why speech reveals moral disorder so quickly:**  
> Speech (the spoken word) reveals moral disorder quickly and plainly for three practical, observable reasons:  
> 1. **It is immediate and involuntary in everyday use:** We constantly speak thoughts and feelings (e.g., a quick remark, a sigh, a curse word, a sarcastic comment). Unlike many moral actions (which may be delayed, hidden, or require physical effort), speech often flows naturally and spontaneously from our current mental/emotional state. There is little pause or self-censorship in the moment for most casual speech. Thus, the current inner disposition (inclination toward reverence *or* toward contempt) shows up almost instantly in what is actually said.  
> 2. **It is public and easily observed:** Speech is typically audible to others (in conversation, in groups, even quietly). Unlike internal thoughts or hidden deeds, spoken words are readily heard and noticed by others. This makes hypocrisy or inconsistency (e.g., blessing God while cursing others) immediately visible to the community. People notice when someone says “Praise God!” in prayer or worship but then uses derogatory language, sarcasm, or contempt for another person later that day or even in the same conversation.  
> 3. **It directly expresses the inner attitude toward the *other person* (who is made in God’s image):** Because the contradiction centers on *how we speak about others* (cursing them), speech bypasses abstraction. We don’t need to judge complex motives; we simply hear the word or phrase used. If someone consistently speaks contemptuously (e.g., with name-calling, mocking remarks, dismissive language) toward people—especially in private or casual settings—it directly signals an inner moral tendency to devalue or curse those made in God’s image. This directly contradicts the public claim or habitual tendency to bless God. The inconsistency is seen *in the words themselves*, which are the clearest external sign of the internal attitude at that moment. There is no need to infer hidden motives; the spoken word *is* the evidence of the current attitude toward the other person.

> **In short (for clarity):** James exposes the inner contradiction that the same person praises God with their words while using their words to curse people created in God’s image—logically inconsistent because cursing God’s image-bearers is tantamount to cursing part of God’s own reflection. Speech reveals this moral disorder quickly because speech is immediate (it flows naturally), public (others hear it), and directly expresses how we regard others; thus, the inner tendency toward reverence for God and contempt for God’s image-bearers shows up plainly in what is actually said, with little delay or concealment.

> *Why this answers the question well:*  
> - It precisely identifies the *inner contradiction* as the use of the *same speech faculty* for opposite stances toward God (blessing) and God’s image-bearers (cursing), grounded in the biblical understanding of humans as made in God’s likeness. It avoids vague terms like “inner conflict” without explanation; it states the logical/moral inconsistency plainly.  
> - It explains *why speech reveals moral disorder quickly* by focusing on observable, practical features of speech (immediacy, public nature, direct expression of attitude toward others), not abstract psychology or hidden inner turmoil. It uses everyday language ("flows naturally," "in the moment," "others hear it") and avoids overstatement ("speech *always* reveals" – it reveals *quickly and plainly* in typical use). It directly ties the speed/visibility to the fact that speech is what is *actually said* about others in the moment, which matches the passage’s focus on "with that mouth" producing both blessing and cursing.  
> - It stays faithful to James 3:9 ("made after the similitude of God") and the context (a practical instruction for believers), avoiding speculative theology. It is concise (under 150 words for the explanation) and directly responsive.

> *Final note for the reader:* This isn't saying people *always* curse others with every negative thought; it's saying that when moral disorder (a tendency toward contempt for God's image-bearers) is present *inside a person*, speech—the natural, frequent, often unguarded expression of thoughts and feelings—will quickly and visibly show that tendency (e.g., a careless insult, sarcastic remark, or habitual derogatory comment), creating the clear inner contradiction James observes: blessing God with the same words used to curse His image-bearers. Recognizing this helps us pause, reflect, and choose words that honor both God and others, as James urges ("these things ought not so to be").
