The self being denied is not the self as a person of worth. It is the self in its claim to have the final say. To “deny himself” is not primarily to give up a pleasure, much less to despise the person one is. It is to give up the right to say, “I will follow Jesus, but only as long as my life is safe and my standing is secure.”

Peter has just rebuked Jesus for saying that he must suffer and be killed. Peter wants to follow him, but he objects to where Jesus is going. That gives “take up his cross” its edge: to Jesus’ hearers, a cross was an instrument of public shame and execution, not a metaphor for an everyday burden. The words “for my sake and the gospel’s” matter, too. Jesus does not praise suffering for its own sake; he asks for a loyalty that can hold even when it costs a life.

That can sound **liberating** because if safety and approval are not the highest goods, fear of losing them need not govern you. You are free, for instance, to stand beside someone even when doing so puts your own standing at risk. It can sound **offensive** because Jesus really does challenge the conviction that your life is yours alone to direct. And for someone who has been told that being good means having no needs or enduring abuse, “deny yourself” can sound like one more demand to disappear. The distinction lies in “follow me”: this is a call to allegiance to Jesus, not a grant of authority to anyone who wants your submission. The paradox is that the self is not to be erased; the life one stops guarding at all costs is the very life Jesus promises to save.
