All five are present, but they are not equal. They are layers of the same problem. Anger is the surface, mercy is the root.

Jesus told this parable to the Pharisees and scribes who were murmuring, *"This man receiveth sinners, and eateth with them"* - Luke 15:2. The elder brother *is* them. He is not outside the house, he is inside the house and still lost.

Here is the progression:

### 1. The Surface: Anger - v.28
> *"...he was angry, and would not go in"*

Anger is the symptom, not the disease. It is what everyone can see. He refuses to join the feast. This mirrors exactly what the Pharisees were doing - refusing to enter the joy of Jesus eating with repentant sinners.

But why is he angry?

### 2. The Middle: Envy and Wounded Fairness - v.29-30
> *"Lo, these many years do I serve thee... and yet thou never gavest me a kid... But as soon as this thy son was come... thou hast killed for him the fatted calf."*

This is his conscious complaint: **Fairness.**

He keeps a ledger. He counts years, commandments kept, kids not received. The fatted calf vs. a kid is deliberately insulting - a kid was a cheap, everyday meal for friends, the fatted calf was reserved for a once-in-a-lifetime village celebration. To him, the father is unjust. The sinner gets more than the saint.

Envy is there, but it's driven by this deeper sense that the world is not operating on the rules he agreed to. If you work hard, you get paid. If you sin badly, you get punished. Mercy breaks that system.

This is why he says *"this thy son"* and not *"my brother."* He disowns the relationship. And the father gently corrects him: *"this THY BROTHER was dead..."* v.32

### 3. The Root: Pride in Obedience and Discomfort with Mercy Itself - v.29

This is the deepest problem. Listen to how he describes his own obedience:

> *"these many years do I serve thee"*

The word for "serve" there is *douleuo* - to serve as a **slave**. He did not live as a son, he lived as a hired man trying to earn his father's favor.

And the father's answer exposes the tragedy:

> *"Son, thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine."* v.31

The elder brother already owned everything. The fatted calf was already his. The father's presence was already his. But he never enjoyed it because he saw the relationship as transactional, not filial. He obeyed not out of love, but out of leverage - *I obey so you owe me.*

That is why mercy offends him.

If the father's love is free, unearned, and can be given to a failure who wasted his life with harlots, then what was the value of all his years of flawless obedience? Grace relativizes his achievement. It means his obedience did not earn him more sonship. And a pride built on being the "good son" cannot tolerate that.

Tim Keller summarizes it well: **Both sons wanted the father's *things* but not the father himself.** The younger wanted them by demand, the elder by earning. Both were estranged. One was lost far away, one was lost at home.

So to answer your list directly:

*   **Anger?** That's the *outburst*.
*   **Envy?** That's the *comparison*.
*   **Wounded Fairness?** That's the *argument he makes to himself*.
*   **Pride in Obedience?** That's the *engine* that makes fairness feel so wounded.
*   **Discomfort with Mercy?** That's the *theological heart* of it.

His deepest problem is not that he was too obedient, but that his obedience was joyless, loveless, and self-righteous. It allowed him to stay physically close to the father while his heart was far away. He could not rejoice when his brother was found because he had never understood that he himself was a son by grace, not by merit.

That is why the parable ends without an ending - we are never told if the elder brother went in. The father *came out and intreated him* v.28, just as he had run out to the younger son. Grace pursues the rebellious and the religious.

The question Jesus leaves with the Pharisees - and with us - is: Will you come into the feast when you realize you cannot earn your seat at the table?
