Luke gives the diagnosis before the parable begins: Jesus addresses people who **“trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and despised others”** (v. 9). Those two things condemn the Pharisee. His fasting and tithing are not presented as sins, and the passage does not say he is lying about them. The problem is what he makes of them: they become grounds for confidence before God and for looking down on the tax collector. Even his thanks to God turns into a comparison—“I am not as other men are.”

So it is more than pride in the abstract, and not chiefly a warning about performing virtue for a human audience. It is **self-reliance expressed through comparison and contempt**. If there is false doctrine here, it is implicit in that mistaken account of how one stands right before God.

The tax collector’s posture is spiritually truer because it matches his prayer. Standing apart, lowering his eyes, and beating his breast express his refusal to claim a favorable standing on his own merits. He asks for mercy. The posture does not *earn* his justification; it shows the humility and dependence his words confess. Jesus’ reversal is that the man who asks God to make him right goes home justified, rather than the man who presents God with reasons he believes he already is.
