## A short answer

Anger, envy, and wounded fairness are all real in the text, but they are symptoms. His deepest problem is that he understands himself as a **servant earning wages rather than a son receiving a inheritance**. Once obedience is a transaction, mercy to someone else feels like theft. So "pride in obedience" and "discomfort with mercy" come closest, but they grow from that deeper misreading of his relationship to the father.

## What the text shows

**1. He describes himself as a laborer.**
"These many years do I *serve* thee" uses the language of slavery or hired service. He has lived in the father's house without experiencing it as a son's home. Obedience was his way of accumulating credit.

**2. He keeps a ledger.**
"Neither transgressed I at any time thy commandment… yet thou never gavest me a kid." That is the language of payment withheld. The grievance is that he was owed and not paid, which is a different thing from simply being unloved.

**3. He wants the goods without the father.**
He wanted a kid "that I might make merry with *my friends*." Even his imagined celebration is away from the father. He resents the feast, but he never asked for one, and he never saw that everything was already available to him. Like his brother, he treats the father as a source of resources.

**4. He severs the relationship.**
He says "this thy son," not "my brother." The father corrects him: "this thy *brother*." He also claims the money went "with harlots," which the story never says. Resentment has made him an accuser, and he has filled in the worst details himself.

**5. He can't rejoice, and mercy is the cause.**
The father says "it was meet," or necessary, to celebrate. For the elder brother, the restoration of a lost person is an offense because it doesn't fit his moral accounting. If the younger son can be welcomed without earning it, then his own years of service look like they bought nothing.

## How the options relate

- **Anger** is the surface reaction.
- **Envy** is what he feels when he compares himself to his brother.
- **Wounded fairness** is the justification he gives, and it isn't baseless by ordinary standards.
- **Pride in obedience** is the source of the fairness claim: he believes his record entitles him.
- **Discomfort with mercy** is the result. Grace threatens the system that gives his life meaning.

The root beneath them all is a **mistaken identity**: he lives as a servant in a father's house.

## The parallel with the younger son

The younger son also prepared to say, "Make me as one of thy hired servants." Both sons think sonship is something you earn or forfeit. The younger is cured by experiencing the father's welcome. The elder is offered the same thing. The father leaves the feast to plead with him, just as he ran to the younger, and says, "Son, thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine." The gift the elder wanted was already his.

The parable ends without telling us whether he went in. That openness is probably the point, since the question is being put to the listener, and in Luke's context to the Pharisees who were complaining that Jesus welcomed sinners.
