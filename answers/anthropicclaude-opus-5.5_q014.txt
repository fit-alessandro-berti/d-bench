## Reveal or train? The grammar leans toward "train," though it does both

The sentence runs in a surprising direction: *"where your treasure is, there will your heart be also."* We might expect Jesus to say the reverse, that where your heart is, your treasure will follow, meaning we spend on what we already love. That would make treasure a symptom of the heart. Instead, treasure comes first and the heart follows. The Greek uses a future verb (*estai*, "will be"). It may be a general "will," as in "this is how things go," but either way the heart is pictured as moving *toward* wherever the treasure has been placed.

Two features of the passage push toward the "training" reading:

1. **The saying is attached to commands.** "Lay not up… but lay up…" Verse 21 opens with "For," giving the *reason* for the commands. If treasure merely revealed a fixed heart, commanding people to relocate their treasure would be pointless. The logic runs like this: *move your treasure, because your heart will go with it.* Where you invest becomes a way of steering what you love.

2. **Heavenly treasure is built through concrete acts.** In Jewish thought of the period, treasure in heaven came especially through almsgiving (Tobit 4:8–9; Sirach 29:10–12). Jesus uses the idea the same way elsewhere: "sell that thou hast, and give to the poor, and thou shalt have treasure in heaven" (Matt 19:21), and Luke places this saying right after "Sell that ye have, and give alms" (Luke 12:33–34). Storing treasure in heaven is not first a feeling. It is something you *do* with money and goods, and the heart is expected to follow.

The diagnostic sense is still present. The verse also works as a mirror: look at what you store, guard, and worry about, and you will see where your heart has gone. The best reading is probably a loop. The heart chooses where to invest, and the investment then deepens and holds the heart. Jesus intervenes at the point most within reach, which is what you do with your possessions.

## How possession, attention, and love are tied together

The surrounding verses show that verses 19–21 open a tight sequence. Matthew places three sayings side by side, and each names a different dimension of the same reality:

| Verses | Image | Dimension |
|---|---|---|
| 6:19–21 | Treasure and heart | **Possession**: where you store value |
| 6:22–23 | The eye as the lamp of the body | **Attention**: what you look at and how |
| 6:24 | Two masters, God and mammon | **Love and allegiance**: whom you serve |

**Possession draws attention.** You watch what you own. Earthly treasure in this passage is vulnerable: moths eat stored garments, "rust" (literally "eating," possibly corrosion or vermin in the grain) consumes, and thieves "break through," digging through mud-brick walls. Treasure that can be lost must be guarded, and guarding it occupies the mind. That is why the passage flows directly into "Take no thought [do not be anxious] for your life" (6:25–34). Anxiety is attention captured by perishable treasure.

**Attention shapes the whole self.** The next saying, "if thine eye be single, thy whole body shall be full of light," uses loaded language. In Jewish idiom an "evil eye" meant stinginess or envy (compare Matt 20:15, "Is thine eye evil, because I am good?"), and the "single" eye (*haplous*) carries overtones of undivided focus and even generosity. So how you *see*, whether grasping or open-handed, affects your whole inner life. Possession and perception are bound up together.

**Attention becomes love and service.** Then comes the conclusion: "No man can serve two masters… he will hate the one, and love the other." Wealth (*mammon*) is personified as a rival lord. What began as storage ends as servitude. What you hoard, you watch; what you watch, you come to love; what you love, you serve.

## The "heart" is more than feeling

"Heart" (*kardia*, reflecting Hebrew *lev*) in biblical usage is not mainly the seat of emotion. It is the center of the person: thought, will, desire, and loyalty together. So the claim is stronger than "you will feel fond of your stuff." It means that your deepest orientation, the direction your whole life faces, gets located wherever you have put your treasure. If that treasure can rot or be stolen, the self is staked on something that will not last.

## A final observation

Both commands say "for *yourselves*." Jesus does not ask people to stop caring about their own good. He challenges their judgment about where that good is secure. The appeal is almost prudential: invest where nothing corrodes. But the payoff is not only safer treasure. It is a heart that is no longer divided, anxious, or captive, because it rests where loss cannot reach.

So: **treasure reveals the heart, but in this text it chiefly trains it.** What you do with your possessions leads your attention, and your attention leads your love.
