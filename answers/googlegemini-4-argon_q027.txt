To understand what condemns the Pharisee and justifies the tax collector (publican), we only have to look at the introductory verse (v. 9), which acts as the interpretive key for the entire parable. Luke tells us explicitly that Jesus targeted those who **"trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and despised others."** 

Therefore, the Pharisee is not condemned by just one of the flaws you listed, but by the way they all form a single, interconnected spiritual pathology. However, at the root of it all is **pride manifested as self-trust**, which naturally gives birth to **comparison, contempt, and spiritual blindness**.

Here is a breakdown of what condemns the Pharisee, and why the tax collector’s posture represents true spiritual reality.

---

### Part 1: What Condemns the Pharisee?

**1. It is NOT technically "False Doctrine" (in the traditional sense)**
Interestingly, the Pharisee starts his prayer with, *"God, I thank thee..."* Doctrinally, he gives a nod to grace. He acknowledges that God is the source of his ability to be good. Furthermore, his actions go above and beyond the Law of Moses: the Law required only one fast a year (the Day of Atonement), yet he fasted twice a week; the Law required tithing only on certain agricultural produce, yet he tithed on everything he acquired. By the standard of religious performance, he was exemplary.

**2. The Root Sin: Self-Trust (Pride)**
The Pharisee’s fatal flaw is that he uses his virtue as currency. He believes his moral achievements place God in his debt. The text says he *"stood and prayed thus with himself"* (or *to* himself). God is merely a prop in the Pharisee’s monologue of self-congratulation. He asks God for nothing—no pardon, no help, no grace—because he believes he is spiritually self-sufficient. You cannot receive grace from God if your hands are already full of your own merit.

**3. The Horizontal Symptom: Comparison and Contempt**
Because the Pharisee has no true concept of the infinite holiness of God, he has to measure his righteousness horizontally—against other people. 
* **Comparison:** He defines himself entirely in the negative (*"I am not like other men..."*). To feel tall, he must stand next to people he perceives as short. 
* **Contempt:** Comparison inevitably curdles into contempt (*"despised others"*). The Greek word for "despise" here (*exoutheneō*) literally means to "treat as nothing" or "utterly despise." He weaponizes his own virtue to dehumanize the tax collector, reducing a fellow image-bearer of God to a mere foil for his own ego. 

**4. The Use of Virtue as a Shield**
Rather than using his fasting and tithing to draw closer to God and love his neighbor, the Pharisee uses his virtues to insulate himself from both. His piety has become an idol; he worships his own reflection in the mirror of the Law.

---

### Part 2: Why is the Tax Collector’s Posture Spiritually Truer?

In the first century, tax collectors were viewed as the ultimate traitors and moral outcasts—collaborators with the Roman occupiers who enriched themselves by extorting their own people. Yet, Jesus says this man went home "justified" (declared righteous by God). Why?

**1. He Possesses "Truth in the Inward Parts"**
Spiritual truth is, at its core, alignment with reality. The tax collector’s posture is spiritually truer because **he sees reality as it actually is**: God is holy, and he is not. In the Greek text, he does not just say, "God be merciful to me, *a* sinner." He uses the definite article: *"God be merciful to me, **the** sinner."* Unlike the Pharisee, who compares himself to the whole world, the tax collector is so aware of his own brokenness before God that it is as if no other sinner exists in the room. 

**2. His Body Language Reflects His Theology**
Every detail of the tax collector's physical posture aligns with his spiritual reality:
* **Standing afar off:** He recognizes the chasm that sin creates between humanity and a holy God.
* **Not lifting his eyes:** He possesses genuine shame and humility, refusing to presume upon God’s favor.
* **Beating his breast:** A traditional Middle Eastern gesture of profound grief and anguish, targeting the heart—the seat of his corruption.

**3. He Appeals to Atonement, Not Merit**
The most profound theological truth in the parable is hidden in the tax collector’s plea: *"God be merciful to me."* 
The Greek word he uses for "be merciful" is not the usual word for mercy (*eleeō*). Instead, he uses the word **hilaskomai**, which means **"to make propitiation"** or **"to atone for."** It is directly related to the *hilastērion*—the "Mercy Seat" on the Ark of the Covenant where the High Priest sprinkled blood on the Day of Atonement. 

Remember, this parable takes place in the Temple, likely during the hour of the daily sacrifice. The tax collector is looking at the altar of sacrifice and essentially praying, *"God, let that blood-sacrifice be for me. Atonement is my only hope."*

### Summary
The Pharisee is condemned because **he went to the Temple to report his goodness, not to receive God's grace.** His pride blinded him to his own sin and led him to despise his neighbor. 

The tax collector’s posture is spiritually truer because **emptiness is the only true posture a creature can take before the Creator.** He brought nothing to God but his sin, threw himself entirely on the mechanism of God's atonement, and therefore walked away with the only righteousness that actually counts—the righteousness that God gives as a gift to the humble (v. 14).
