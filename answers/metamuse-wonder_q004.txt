Proverbs 3:5-6 is not calling for blind faith *instead* of thinking. It's calling for a transfer of ultimate reliance — from autonomous, self-sufficient reasoning to trusting dependence on God.

That distinction is important, because the same book that says "lean not unto thine own understanding" also says, "Happy is the man that findeth wisdom, and the man that getteth understanding" (Prov. 3:13).

### 1. "Trust in the LORD with all thine heart"

In Hebrew thought, the "heart" (*levav*) is not just the seat of emotion. It is the center of thought, will, desire, and decision-making.

So this trust is whole-person trust:
- **Intellectually:** believing God is wise and truthful
- **Volitionally:** choosing His way over your own preference
- **Emotionally:** resting in His character rather than being driven by fear or control

And it is trust "in the LORD" — the covenant God who has revealed Himself — not in a vague feeling, intuition, or religious idea. It is *fiducial* trust: personal reliance based on who God has shown Himself to be.

### 2. "Lean not unto thine own understanding"

The verb "lean" (*sha'an*) pictures putting your full weight on something, like a wounded man leaning on a staff. If the staff breaks, he falls.

The command is not, "Do not use your understanding." It is, "Do not make your understanding the thing that ultimately supports you."

Why not? Not because reason is evil, but because our reasoning is:

1. **Limited** — we don't see the future, all the facts, or the hearts of others
2. **Partial** — we see from one vantage point in time and culture
3. **Distorted** — pride, fear, desire, and sin bend how we interpret even good data

Humble self-distrust says: *My reason is a real gift, but it is not infallible, unbiased, or final.*

Anti-intellectualism says something different: *Thinking itself is suspect, so I will stop inquiring.*

Proverbs rebukes that. It commands us to seek counsel (15:22), listen to correction (12:15), observe the world diligently (6:6), weigh words, and pursue wisdom as treasure (2:4-6). Biblical humility actually *fuels* learning, because if I might be wrong, I need Scripture, community, evidence, and prayer all the more.

Ironically, anti-intellectualism is often just another form of leaning on your own understanding — it treats your own feelings, suspicions, or unexamined impressions as unquestionable, while refusing to test them.

### 3. "In all thy ways acknowledge him"

"Acknowledge" (*yada'*) means to know relationally and practically. It's the opposite of compartmentalizing God to religious occasions.

It means bringing God consciously into ordinary decisions — work, money, relationships, ambition, conflict — through prayer, obedience to His Word, and openness to correction. Not: "I decided, then asked God to bless it," but: "Lord, You are Lord of this way too."

### 4. "And he shall direct thy paths"

"Direct" means to make straight or level — to clear and guide the road. It is a promise of God's providential and moral guidance, not a promise that life will be easy, predictable, or that we will get a detailed map in advance.

Often He directs precisely *through* the means Proverbs commends: renewed thinking, wise counsel, circumstances, conviction, and Scripture.

**In short:**

- **Anti-intellectualism** distrusts the mind.
- **Humble self-distrust** distrusts the mind's *autonomy and pride*, and therefore submits the mind to God.

The trust asked for here is not "stop thinking and feel instead." It is: *Think rigorously, plan diligently, listen humbly — but do not rest your weight on yourself. Rest it on God, and let Him correct, overrule, and guide the thinking as you walk.*
