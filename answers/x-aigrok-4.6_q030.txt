**The many “nots” exist because this passage is diagnosing (and offering an alternative to) the default human operating system of self-protection and self-exaltation.** Pride, envy, irritability, score-keeping, and suspicion are not random vices; they are the predictable fruits of a heart that treats the self as the center that must be defended, enlarged, or avenged. Paul is writing to a church already marked by factions, boasting, lawsuits, and spiritual one-upmanship (1 Cor 1–6, 11–14). The portrait of love therefore functions as both description and implicit rebuke: this is what love *is not*, because those traits are what love must first dethrone.

The structure itself is revealing. After two brief positives (“suffers long and is kind”), the text spends most of its energy on negation before returning to four sweeping positives (“bears… believes… hopes… endures all things”). The negations are not mere moralism; they clear the ground. Love cannot coexist with:
- envy (which resents another’s good),
- vaunting or being “puffed up” (which inflates the self),
- unseemly or self-seeking behavior (which treats others as means),
- easy provocation or “thinking evil” (which keeps a ledger and assumes the worst).

These are the habits that make relationships transactional and brittle. By naming them, the passage shows that love is not an emotion that sometimes appears when conditions are favorable; it is a settled orientation that refuses the usual weapons of the ego.

The vision of strength that emerges is therefore *non-reactive, other-centered endurance*. It is not the strength of assertion, dominance, or invulnerability. It is the strength that can absorb injury without retaliation, refuse to keep score, persist in hope and trust even when evidence is mixed, and remain kind under prolonged pressure. This is closer to the strength of a load-bearing wall or a long-distance runner than to the strength of a conqueror. It looks weak to a culture that equates power with self-assertion, yet it is the only kind of strength that can actually hold a community together over time.

In short, the passage proposes that real strength is the capacity to *not* need the usual defenses—because one’s security and worth are located elsewhere (in the God whose own character this love reflects). That is why the list ends not with triumph but with four verbs of persistence: love keeps going when the usual motives for going have already collapsed.
