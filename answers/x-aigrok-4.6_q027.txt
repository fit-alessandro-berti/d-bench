**The Pharisee is condemned for self-righteous pride expressed as comparison, contempt, and using his virtues as self-justification/display before God (and implicitly others), not primarily for “false doctrine” in the abstract.** The tax collector’s posture is treated as spiritually truer because it is humble dependence on mercy rather than claimed merit; Jesus says *this* man went home justified.

The parable is explicitly framed for “certain which trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and despised others” (Luke 18:9). The Pharisee’s prayer is “with himself.” He thanks God he is not like other men (extortioners, unjust, adulterers) “or even as this publican,” then lists his fasting and tithing. The good practices themselves are not the problem; the attitude is: he treats them as evidence of his superiority and as the basis of his standing. He does not ask for mercy because he does not see himself as needing it. The comparison and the contempt for the tax collector are the visible fruit of that self-trust.

The tax collector stands far off, will not lift his eyes, beats his breast, and prays only “God be merciful to me a sinner.” He makes no defense, no comparison, and no claim of virtue. Jesus’ verdict (“this man went down to his house justified rather than the other”) follows immediately: God justifies the one who acknowledges his need, not the one who recites his résumé. The same chapter and Gospel repeatedly contrast self-exaltation with humility (Luke 14:11; 18:14 itself ends with the principle that whoever exalts himself will be humbled).

In short, the Pharisee’s error is not that he performed religious duties or held a particular doctrinal list, but that he used those things to stand before God as already righteous and to look down on another. The tax collector’s posture is “truer” because it matches the reality the parable assumes: all need mercy, and justification is granted to the one who asks for it rather than the one who claims he has already earned it. This is consistent with the broader Lukan and New Testament emphasis on repentance, humility, and God’s initiative rather than self-justification.
