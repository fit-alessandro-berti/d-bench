**Short answer**

Genesis 4:6‑7 paints Cain as a **free‑willed, self‑determining agent** who chooses to turn away from God’s covenant.  
Evil is not presented as a “beast” or an inherited habit; it is shown as the **result of an impulsive, selfish choice**—a lapse in judgment prompted by his own desires and pride (often described as a moment of temptation or an unchecked impulse).  

The text puts **full responsibility on Cain**: he must answer for his sin, accept God’s “desire” for him, and recognize that he will be subject to that desire and will come to rule over those he touches.

---

### 1. What kind of moral agency is depicted?

| Aspect | How the text describes it |
|--------|---------------------------|
| **Agency** | God speaks *directly* to Cain (“unto thee…”) and asks him to explain his anger. The use of “shall” signals that Cain is expected to **justify his actions** and to take responsibility. |
| **Choice** | Cain’s question “Why art thou wroth?” reveals that he **recognizes his own wrongdoing**. He is aware that he has deviated from God’s command and that his face is “fallen.” |
| **Nature of the act** | He abandons God’s covenant, goes with his brother, and seeks to live apart from Him. This is a **personal decision**, not a hereditary trait or a mere habit. |

Thus, the passage portrays Cain as an **active moral agent** who can choose to obey or rebel, and the choice itself creates the problem.

---

### 2. Is evil shown as an impulse, a beast, a habit, or a temptation?

| Option | Evidence in the text |
|--------|----------------------|
| **Impulse / sudden urge** | Cain reacts with anger and shame (“why art thou wroth?”) and feels his “countenance fallen.” The shock and regret suggest an **uncontrolled, impulsive reaction** to God’s demand. |
| **Beast (Satan)** | No reference to a demonic creature or the devil is made. The “beast” motif belongs to later narratives (e.g., the beast under the sea); here it is absent. |
| **Habit** | Nothing indicates a long‑standing pattern of sin. The sin is fresh and specific to this episode. |
| **Temptation** | The invitation to join his brother and enjoy “life without God” functions as a **lure**—the “flesh’s desire” that tempts Cain. Yet the emphasis is on *his* response, not merely the external pull. |

**Conclusion:** The dominant reading is that evil arises from **an impulsive, self‑serving choice**—a momentary lapse in judgment driven by his own desires and pride. While temptation is present, the text stresses Cain’s **own agency** in rejecting God’s will.

---

### 3. What responsibility does the text place on Cain?

1. **Personal accountability** – God tells Cain: “If thou doest well, shalt thou not be accepted? … and if thou doest not well, sin lieth at the door.” He is warned that his current state is a direct result of his actions; he must admit the error.
2. **Submission to God’s desire** – “And unto thee shall be his desire, and thou shalt rule over him.” God promises to have *His* (divine) desire for Cain, and Cain will become the ruler of that desire. This means:
   - He cannot claim innocence; he must **accept** God’s plan for him.
   - He will be **subject to** God’s will and will **exercise authority** over others (including his brother).
3. **Consequences await** – By refusing to follow God, Cain invites the consequences: murder of Abel, the death of Abel, and ultimately his own ruin.

In short, **the entire moral burden falls on Cain**. He is the author of his own sin, the one who must answer for it, and the one who will now serve as the instrument of God’s will.

---

### 4. Putting it together – the picture of moral agency

- **Human beings are agents of choice.** They can either align themselves with God’s covenant or stray into rebellion.
- **Sin is not an inevitable “beast” or inherited habit; it stems from a personal decision**—here, Cain’s impulsive turn away from God, fueled by his own desires and pride.
- **Responsibility is absolute.** Cain is called to confront his sin, submit to God’s desire, and recognize that he will be ruled by that very desire and will govern the people he touches.

This passage therefore sets the stage for the rest of the story: Cain’s initial rebellion leads to murder, betrayal, and eventual death, underscoring that **our moral agency determines our fate** and that we must take ownership of the consequences of our choices.
