This passage presents a strikingly coherent picture of human moral responsibility, the nature of evil, and the dynamics of spiritual struggle. Below is a breakdown addressing each part of your question, grounded in textual, linguistic, and literary analysis.

### 1. Picture of Moral Agency
The text portrays **robust, dialogical moral agency**. God addresses Cain personally, questions his emotional state (`Why art thou wroth?`), and offers him a conditional path forward. This implies:
- **Cognitive-moral awareness**: Cain understands enough to offer a sacrifice and react when it is rejected. He is not acting blindly or mechanically.
- **Volitional freedom**: The `if... then...` structure (`If thou doest well... If thou doest not well...`) frames reality as contingent upon human choice, not predetermination.
- **Relational accountability**: Divine speech assumes mutual recognizability. God does not override, coerce, or preempt Cain’s response but invites deliberation and repentance.
- **Effective agency**: Cain’s choices genuinely alter his standing (`shalt thou not be accepted?`) and his relationship to sin (`thou shalt rule over him`). Moral action is not futile theater.

In short, moral agency here is **responsive, rational, and efficacious** within a covenant-like framework where divine warning meets human volition.

### 2. Nature of Evil
Evil is **not** presented primarily as mere impulse, psychological flaw, ingrained habit, or abstract temptation. Instead, it is **personified and depicted with predatory, almost biological urgency**:
- `"Sin lieth at the door"` (Hebrew: `חַטָּאת רֹבֶץ פֶּתַח`): The verb `רֹבֶץ` (rovecs) literally means "crouches" or "lies in wait," used elsewhere for a lion or wild beast awaiting prey (Prov 22:13; Job 10:13). Sin is pictured as a **predator poised to strike** when Cain’s guard drops or his anger festers.
- `"Unto thee shall be his desire"`: The noun `תְּשׁוּקָה` (teshukah) denotes a strong, driving craving. Remarkably, it echoes Gen 3:16 (`he shall rule over thee` / `thy desire shall be to thy husband`), creating a literary parallel: just as Edenic harmony fractured into relational domination, so now moral life involves a struggle for mastery between human will and sin’s pull.
- **Grammatical/personification note**: Though `חַטָּאת` (sin) is grammatically feminine in Hebrew, the text uses a masculine pronoun (`his`). This break in expectation deliberately casts sin as an active, rival agent rather than a passive condition.

Thus, evil is **an active, coercive force that seeks to hijack human will**, exploiting emotional vulnerability (anger/shame) and demanding resistance. It is neither excused nor minimized, but its power is not absolute.

### 3. Responsibility Placed on the Person
The text assigns **direct, non-transferable moral responsibility** to Cain:
- **No externalization of blame**: God does not attribute Cain’s anger to genetics, upbringing, cosmic fate, or even the unfairness of the situation. The locus of decision rests squarely with Cain.
- **Conditional outcomes tied to conduct**: Acceptance or rejection hinges on whether Cain `doest well` (i.e., makes his heart/heart’s posture properly aligned with divine expectation). Moral causality is immediate and personal.
- **Imperative mastery**: `"Thou shalt rule over him"` (Hebrew: `וְאַתָּה תְּמֹשֵׁל בּוֹ`) carries strong normative force. The verb `מָשַׁל` (to rule/dominate) echoes the creational mandate (Gen 1:26–28), now repurposed morally: humans retain the God-given capacity to subdue disruptive forces, including inner sin. This is not a suggestion but a call to exercised stewardship over one’s own moral landscape.
- **Preemptive warning as grace-with-responsibility**: God’s discourse is itself an act of mercy (giving time to repent), yet it sharpens accountability. Warning ≠ removal of choice; it elevates it.

### Synthesis
Genesis 4:6–7 sketches a **post-lapsarian moral ecology** in which:
- Humans remain true moral agents capable of discernment, choice, and self-governance.
- Sin is a real, predatory presence that tempts, waits, and desires mastery, but lacks ultimate sovereignty.
- Responsibility is absolute: one must `do well` and actively `rule over` the inward threat. Failure to resist is counted as rebellion, not inevitable collapse.

This passage avoids both moral fatalism (`sin controls me`) and naive voluntarism (`just choose good effortlessly`). Instead, it presents a **struggle-of-wills framework** where divine warning, human freedom, and moral vigilance intersect. It lays groundwork for later biblical theology: the persistent reality of sin, the enduring image of God in humanity’s capacity for righteous rule, and the necessity of conscious, sustained moral resistance.
