The verse itself answers your question, if you look closely at its grammar. The danger is located not in being seen but in the *purpose* of the act: πρὸς τὸ θεαθῆναι αὐτοῖς — "in order to be seen by them." That's a purpose clause. And the verb chosen is telling: θεαθῆναι comes from the same root as θέατρον, the theater. The almsgiver has turned charity into a stage production; the poor become the set, and the street becomes the house. What's condemned is not exposure but *aim*.

The Sermon on the Mount proves this internally. Only verses earlier, Matthew 5:16 commands the opposite behavior with a different end: "Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works and *glorify your Father*." Identical visibility, opposite teleology. The same deed can be witness or performance depending on who is meant to receive the glory. Jesus does not practice or prescribe invisibility; he practiced and commanded very public goodness. What he attacks is the craving that converts an audience into a payroll.

That economic logic runs through the whole passage. "They have their reward" — ἀπέχουσιν τὸν μισθόν — uses language from commercial receipts: *paid in full*. Human applause is a wage, and it settles the account completely. You cannot collect twice. Notice that Jesus never condemns desiring reward as such; he simply asks which treasury you're drawing on. The Father's reward is deferred, unseen, relational. The crowd's is instant, total, and therefore final.

Two further observations deepen the point. First, the word "hypocrite" (ὑποκριτής) originally meant a stage actor — one who speaks from behind a mask. The hypocrite's tragedy is not insincerity in the modern sense but self-division: he becomes simultaneously performer and his own admiring audience. Which leads to the second point: the craving survives privacy. You can commit this sin entirely alone, performing for the internalized crowd, rehearsing how the story will be told later. This is why secrecy, though prescribed as the remedy (the left hand ignorant of the right, the closed door, the anointed face), is medicine rather than metaphysics. Hiddenness doesn't make an act holy; it *detoxes* the motive. Enforced obscurity is training in living before an Audience of One — what Kierkegaard called purity of heart, willing one thing, coram Deo rather than coram hominibus.

So why does the warning open with "take heed"? Because visibility is genuinely dangerous — not as evil, but as occasion. An audience recruits the craving; it flatters it; it offers immediate payment precisely when the act is at its most virtuous-looking. Worse, it quietly converts the beneficiary into a prop: the hungry person stops being someone I love and becomes material for my self-portrait. And it makes goodness fragile — a virtue that dies in the dark was never quite virtue, only reputation management.

(One textual footnote worth knowing: many manuscripts read "righteousness" here rather than "alms," which would make this verse the heading for everything that follows — almsgiving, prayer, fasting. Either way, the principle generalizes beyond charity.)

The cleanest formulation, then: the verse forbids not *being seen* but *needing to be seen*. Public goodness is dangerous the way any powerful thing is dangerous — it depends on what it's feeding. Light that shines so others see the Father is obedience; light arranged so others see the lamp is idolatry with excellent lighting.
