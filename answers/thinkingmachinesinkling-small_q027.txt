The parable’s opening line tells you exactly what is being judged: **“those who trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and despised others”** (v. 9). The Pharisee is not condemned for bad theology in the abstract—he is technically correct about fasting and tithing—but for the **orientation of his heart**. All four of your options (except false doctrine as the *primary* charge) are present and intertwined:

| What he does | What it reveals |
|---|---|
| **Self-trust** | He treats his fasting and tithing as grounds for confidence before God: “*I* am not… *I* fast… *I* give…” His prayer is technically addressed to God, but Luke adds he prayed **“with himself”** (v. 11)—he is really talking to himself. |
| **Comparison** | He measures himself against others—“as other men are… or even as this publican.” Righteousness becomes a relative scorecard rather than a gift from God. |
| **Contempt** | The introduction explicitly says they **despised others**; he names the publican with disdain, using him as a foil to make himself look better. |
| **Virtue as self-display** | His “thanks” is actually self-congratulation. The works of God’s law become props in a performance of superiority rather than tools of love or dependence. |

So the condemnation is **pride** expressed through **comparison**, **contempt**, and **self-displaying virtue**, all rooted in **trusting in himself** rather than in God. He does not ask for anything because he believes he needs nothing.

### Why the tax collector is “justified rather than the other” (v. 14)

The publican’s posture is truer for three reasons that mirror the Pharisee’s failures:

1. **He knows his real condition.** He calls himself “*a sinner*” (singular, emphatic in Greek: *ho hamartōlos*). He does not compare himself to others; he compares himself to God’s holiness and finds himself lacking.
2. **He stands “afar off” and will not lift his eyes.** He abandons the posture of self-assertion. His body language is one of **humility and shame**, not performance.
3. **He asks for *mercy***—not for promotion, not for a reward based on past behavior, but for undeserved grace: “*God be merciful to me*” (using *hilastheti*, related to propitiation/atonement).

In Jesus’s theology, **justification** (being declared righteous before God) is not awarded to those who compile a record of virtue but to those who, recognizing their sin, **receive God’s mercy on the basis of His grace**, not their own merit. The tax collector goes home “justified” because he has stopped relying on *self* and has instead trusted in *God’s merciful action*.

### The deeper point

The Pharisee’s error is not that he is wrong about what behaviors God requires; it is that he has turned **obedience into a weapon of self-justification and elitism**. He uses virtue to create distance from the “unrighteous,” which reveals that he does not understand grace: **if God justifies the ungodly (Rom. 4:5), anyone who thinks he is “not like other men” has already misunderstood the gospel.** The tax collector’s posture—honest about sin, dependent on mercy, alone with God—is the only bottom from which grace can lift a person.

So to answer directly: **Pride is the root; comparison, contempt, and self-display are the branches; false doctrine is not the issue here, but self-righteousness is.** The publican is spiritually truer because he offers God exactly what God requires: a repentant heart that receives justification as a gift rather than earning it as a wage.
