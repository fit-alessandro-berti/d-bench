## What condemns the Pharisee

Luke tells us the target in advance: people who "trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and despised others." So the condemnation has two named parts, **self-trust** and **contempt**. The other items on your list are mostly how those two show up.

**False doctrine?** Not in the ordinary sense. Nothing in the Pharisee's prayer is creedally wrong. He addresses God, thanks Him, and avoids real sins. His claims are probably true, and his practice exceeds what the Law required: Torah mandated one annual fast, and he fasts twice weekly. Jesus' first hearers would have expected him to be the hero. But there is a *functional* theological error. He behaves as if he is righteous by his own record and needs no mercy. His prayer contains thanks but no petition. A God who is thanked but never needed has been quietly demoted from Savior to auditor.

**Pride** is the root, but pride is abstract. Luke shows what it looks like in practice:

- **Comparison** is the mechanism. His righteousness is defined negatively, by what he is *not*: "not as other men... or even as this publican." He needs the publican as a foil, so his standing depends on someone else's being lower. That is why the comparison is damaging rather than neutral.
- **Contempt** is the fruit. "This publican" is dismissive, and the accusation may well be accurate. Tax collectors often were unjust. The problem is not a false assessment but that he sees a sinner as a contrast to himself rather than as a fellow needy person.
- **Virtue as self-display** is the form. "I fast... I give tithes" is an inventory submitted to God. The prayer is literally *pros heauton*, "to/with/about himself," which may mean he is praying to himself. Fasting and tithing are not condemned; Jesus says nothing against them. What is condemned is using good works as a foundation for confidence before God.

So the order is: trust in self → need for comparison → contempt → a prayer that reports rather than asks.

## Why the tax collector is "truer"

It isn't that sinners are better than the devout, or that low self-regard is a virtue. His posture is truer because it **matches reality**.

- **He sees only God and himself.** He doesn't look sideways. He says *"the* sinner" (literally), not "a sinner among others," and he doesn't rank himself against anyone.
- **His body tells the truth.** He stands far off, doesn't lift his eyes, and beats his breast, a gesture of anguish.
- **He asks for what only God can give.** "Be merciful" (*hilasthēti*) carries the sense of atonement or propitiation. In the temple setting, where sacrifice is offered, he appeals to God's provision for sin rather than to his own record.
- **He offers no résumé.** He does not say "I'll make it right first." His hands are empty, and so they can receive.

The word "justified" is also passive. It is something God does, not something the publican achieves by being sufficiently humble. Humility here is not a meritorious work that outscores the Pharisee's fasting. It is simply the condition in which mercy can be received. Self-reliance refuses the gift.

## A caution built into the parable

The story is easy to misuse: "God, I thank thee that I am not like that Pharisee." That is the same prayer with different names. The parable works by exposing the reader, and verse 14's principle ("everyone that exalteth himself shall be abased") applies to anyone who turns humility into a credential.
