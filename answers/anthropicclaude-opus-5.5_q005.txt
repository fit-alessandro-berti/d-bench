## Why it's hard to take seriously

Enemy-love tends to fail in one of two ways: people reject it as impossible, or they shrink it into something harmless. Several things make it resist us:

1. **It breaks reciprocity, our most basic moral intuition.** Most ethics runs on fairness: good for good, harm answered by proportionate response. Jesus names this directly in the next verses: "if ye love them which love you, what reward have ye? do not even the publicans the same?" (5:46). Reciprocity is treated as the baseline, not the achievement. That feels almost unjust, as if it rewards the wrongdoer.

2. **It seems to command a feeling.** You can't make yourself feel warmly toward someone who has harmed you. If love means affection, the command looks psychologically impossible.

3. **It seems politically naive.** Reinhold Niebuhr and others argued that it may work between individuals but can't govern nations facing aggression. Loving your enemy looks like a way to lose.

4. **It has been abused.** The powerful have invoked it to keep the wronged quiet: forgive, submit, don't resist. Many people are rightly suspicious of any ethic that asks the victim to carry the moral burden.

5. **It has been domesticated.** In ordinary use it often shrinks to "be nice to people you find annoying," which empties it of its force.

## Is it sentimental?

The text itself resists this reading. Notice what the "love" consists of: **bless, do good, pray.** These are actions and dispositions of the will, not emotions. The Greek *agapē* here is closer to "actively will the good of" than to "feel fondness for."

Jesus also doesn't pretend enemies aren't enemies. He calls them "them that curse you," "them that hate you," "them which despitefully use you." There is no illusion that everyone is secretly a friend. The command operates with full clarity about the hostility involved. Sentimentality blurs reality; this saying keeps it sharp.

## Is it strategic?

It has strategic power, and people have used it that way. Paul, echoing Proverbs, says that feeding your enemy "heaps coals of fire on his head" (Romans 12:20). Martin Luther King Jr. argued in his sermon "Loving Your Enemies" that hate multiplies hate in "a descending spiral," and that love is "the only force capable of transforming an enemy into a friend." Walter Wink read the surrounding sayings (turning the cheek, going the second mile) as creative, nonviolent ways of exposing and shaming oppressive power in first-century Roman Palestine.

But the rationale Jesus gives is not outcome-based. It isn't "so that they will change." It is **"that ye may be the children of your Father."** The justification is identity and imitation, not results. This matters, because a purely strategic love collapses the moment it stops working. If the enemy doesn't soften, the strategy has failed and you're released from it. Jesus's version doesn't allow that exit.

## The sun and rain image

The grounding image is remarkable. Sun and rain falling on good and evil alike could easily be read as proof that the universe is *indifferent*, that nature doesn't care about moral desert. Jesus reinterprets that same fact as *generosity*. God's goodness isn't calibrated to merit. It is given before, and regardless of, the recipient's worthiness.

That is the scandal at the heart of the passage. Enemy-love isn't a special rule for difficult people. It's a participation in the way God is said to relate to everyone, including you.

## A deeper form of moral strength

I think this is the most defensible reading, with a specific sense of "strength": **refusing to let your enemy determine who you become.**

When someone wrongs you, they exert power not only over your circumstances but potentially over your character. Hatred can make you resemble the one who harmed you. Enemy-love declines that. It says: *you can injure me, but you do not get to decide my response.* That is a kind of freedom.

This also answers Nietzsche's objection that such love is "slave morality," weakness dressed up as virtue, resentment disguised as forgiveness. He was right that it *can* be that. The test is whether it comes from fear or from freedom. Love that is merely the inability to resist is not what's being described. Love chosen by someone who *could* retaliate and declines is something else entirely.

## What it does not require

Taking the command seriously also means understanding its limits:

- It does not require pretending harm didn't happen.
- It does not forbid seeking justice, accountability, or protection.
- It does not require staying in an abusive situation. You can pray for someone's good while keeping firm distance from them.
- It does not mean trust. Willing someone's good is different from making yourself vulnerable to them again.

Bonhoeffer, who wrote powerfully on this passage in *The Cost of Discipleship*, later joined resistance against Hitler, which shows how agonizing the application can become. The command doesn't dissolve moral complexity. It changes the spirit in which you face it.

## A closing thought

The best answer to your question may be: **it's hard to take seriously because it's meant to be hard.** An ethic anyone could follow without effort wouldn't be worth stating. Jesus seems to be deliberately setting the bar above what is natural. It isn't a sentimental ideal or a clever tactic but a picture of a moral character that is free, generous, and not held hostage by the hostility of others.

Whether that is achievable, or only something to be moved toward, is perhaps the real question the passage leaves you with.
