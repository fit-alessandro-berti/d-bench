The trust asked for in Proverbs 3:5–6 is **whole-person, reverent reliance on the Lord**—a trust that dethrones self-sufficiency and submits one’s plans, judgments, and life direction to God. It is not a call to stop thinking. The Hebrew “heart” includes the mind, will, and affections; “lean” means to put one’s weight or support on something; and “acknowledge him” means to know/recognize God in all one’s ways—not merely mention him, but live before him and under him.

So the contrast is not **understanding vs. no understanding**, but **trusting the Lord vs. making one’s own understanding ultimate**. The verse says “lean not unto *thine own* understanding,” not “do not seek understanding.” In fact, the same book commands us to seek wisdom and understanding diligently (Prov. 2:1–6), and says “the Lord gives wisdom; from his mouth come knowledge and understanding” (Prov. 2:6). Human reason is a God-given tool; it is simply not a safe final foundation.

**Humble self-distrust** says:  
- I am finite, fallible, and affected by sin.  
- My understanding is real but not exhaustive or autonomous.  
- I will use my mind seriously, seek wisdom, pray, consult Scripture and wise counsel, and hold my conclusions with teachability.  
- God’s wisdom, not my personal insight, is the foundation of my life.  
- I can think confidently precisely because I am thinking under God rather than pretending to be God.

**Mere anti-intellectualism**, by contrast, says or assumes:  
- Thinking, learning, evidence, and careful reasoning are spiritually suspect.  
- Ignorance or strong feelings are safer than study.  
- “Trusting God” means rejecting expertise, critical thought, or hard questions.  
- A slogan or intuition can replace patient obedience and discernment.

That is not humility; ironically it can be another form of self-trust—trusting one’s own preferred intuitions, slogans, or chosen authorities without examination. Proverbs calls us to the fear of the Lord, which is “the beginning of knowledge” (Prov. 1:7), not the end of thinking.

Practically, the person who trusts the Lord with all their heart may use medical knowledge, study Scripture carefully, seek counsel, do rigorous scholarship, and make prudent plans—while refusing to treat any of these as ultimate. They hold their own understanding like a lamp, not like a foundation. They pray, “Lord, make my path straight,” and then pursue wisdom through the means God normally provides: his word, prayer, community, reason, experience, and providence.

So Proverbs 3:5–6 asks for **humble, worshipful, wholehearted dependence on God**—not credulity, not blind leaps, and not the suppression of the mind. It asks us to use our understanding faithfully while refusing to bow before it.
