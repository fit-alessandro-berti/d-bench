**Short answer:** Jeremiah is realistic about self-deception, but with a pessimistic premise: humans cannot fully audit their own motives or perfectly discern another person’s heart. The passage does not mainly say that people have no motives; it says that motives are not reliably transparent—especially to the person possessing them.

### What the emphasis is

“The heart” in biblical usage includes thoughts, desires, intentions, loyalties, and moral character—not merely emotion. Jeremiah’s point is that the inner person is both morally corrupt (“desperately wicked,” or in some translations “beyond understanding” or “incurably corrupt”) and opaque.

The rhetorical question, “Who can know it?”, is answered in the next verse: **God**. “The Lord searches the heart” does not mean God is uncertain; it means His knowledge is exhaustive, while human self-knowledge is limited.

Thus the passage is:

- **Realistic** about self-justification, rationalization, and the fact that people may sincerely believe misleading explanations of themselves.
- **Pessimistic** about unaided human self-knowledge and self-judgment.
- Not necessarily a comprehensive doctrine that every motive is secretly evil or that moral accountability is impossible.

In its immediate context, Jeremiah is challenging confidence in human self-justification and covenant faithfulness. The appropriate response is not constant suspicion but humility before God.

### How to talk about motives

When motives cannot be known with certainty, speech should distinguish three things:

1. **What happened** — observable facts.
2. **What someone says their motive was** — a report, not automatic proof.
3. **What their motive probably was** — an inference from their conduct, patterns, incentives, and willingness to be accountable.

For example:

> “I do not know what you intended. But you promised confidentiality, shared the information, benefited from doing so, and then blamed others. That pattern is consistent with self-interest, even if you sincerely describe it differently.”

Such language is more faithful than either pretending to know the hidden motive or refusing to draw reasonable conclusions from the evidence.

A few principles help:

- **Judge motives provisionally, not omnisciently.** Say, “This pattern suggests…” rather than “I know your heart was….”
- **Look at fruit over time.** Repeated conduct, especially choices made at personal cost, is more informative than a single statement.
- **Do not confuse outcome with motive.** A harmful action may come from cruelty, ignorance, weakness, or conflicting motives. A good outcome may result from mixed motives.
- **Do not use uncertainty to avoid accountability.** Someone’s motive may be partly unknowable, but their conduct is still their conduct. Boundaries and consequences do not require omniscience.
- **Reserve final judgment to God.** This does not mean never confronting deception; it means not claiming a certainty that belongs to God.
- **Invite confession and correction.** We can ask, “What were you actually trying to accomplish? What did you fear? What benefit did this serve?” without pretending our questions can perfectly search the heart.

Proverbs 16:2 expresses the same tension: “All ways of a man are pure in his own eyes, but the Lord weighs the motives.” Psalm 139:23–24 models the proper response—ask God to search and expose what we cannot see in ourselves.

So the right posture is not naive trust in self-report or cynical mind-reading. It is **truthful acknowledgment of what is known, calibrated suspicion about what is merely inferred, and humility about what remains known fully to God alone**.
