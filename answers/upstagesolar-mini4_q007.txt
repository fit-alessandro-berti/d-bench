This verse is often misunderstood as a call to abandon all moral evaluation, but Jesus is specifically targeting the **appetite to condemn**, not the **practice of discernment**. The difference is not about whether we make moral judgments; it is about *why* we make them, *how* we make them, and *what* we do with them.

### 1. Judging vs. Discernment: Two Different Acts
To say moral judgments never matter is to confuse **discernment** (the ability to recognize truth, good, and harmful patterns) with **condemnation** (the desire to pass final, sovereign judgment on someone’s soul or worth).

- **Discernment** is a mental and moral capacity. It recognizes: “This action is harmful,” “This teaching is false,” “This behavior is destructive.” It is essential for personal integrity, healthy relationships, and wise decision-making. Christ Himself constantly discerned the hearts of people and called out sin and error.
- **Condemnation** is the emotional or spiritual appetite to pronounce someone “lost,” “unworthy,” or “under God’s final sentence.” It is the desire to sit in the seat of moral arbiter and demand punishment or downgrade another person’s standing in the world or before God.

Jesus’ command is not “Do not recognize right and wrong.” It is: **Do not act from the appetite to condemn.** The structure of the verse is striking: “Judge not, and ye shall not be judged.” The Greek is not “Do not exercise judgment,” but “Do not be quick to pass definitive, condemning verdicts.” It warns against the arrogance of assuming we have the final verdict on another person’s heart, and against using moral superiority to justify harshness, exclusion, or self-righteousness.

### 2. The Appetite to Condemn vs. The Practice of Wisdom
The key distinction lies in **motive and posture**:

- **The appetite to condemn** says: “I know who you are, I know your worth, and I demand you be corrected or cast out.” It is often rooted in pride, fear, or a desire to protect one’s own status. It tends to be absolute, rigid, and self-justifying. It also ignores the reality that we ourselves are imperfect and in need of mercy.
- **Discernment**, when grounded in humility, says: “I see this pattern, but I am not the final judge of your heart. I recognize the good and the bad in myself too, and I operate from a posture of truth and compassion, not from a desire to tear down.” It aims at restoration, wisdom, and clear boundaries, not at moral injury.

Jesus is not saying we should ignore issues of right and wrong. In fact, the very context of Luke 6:37 places it alongside calls to love enemies, do good to those who hurt you, and give to everyone in need. He is saying that discernment without grace becomes an instrument of condemnation, and condemnation without wisdom becomes self-deception.

### 3. Why the Verse Works as a Discipline, Not a Null
There is a profound psychological and spiritual logic here: **the appetite to condemn is self-defeating.** When we constantly look for reasons to condemn others, we train our eyes to be critical, we harden our hearts, and we set up a trap where we expect the same harshness to be turned on us. Forgiveness, by contrast, breaks that cycle. It does not deny truth; it refuses to let truth be used as a weapon for final judgment.

This does not mean we are free to tolerate evil or ignore moral clarity. It means that **truth without love is often a vehicle for self-exaltation**, while **love without truth is blind**. The call to “not judge” is an invitation to examine our own hearts, to release the illusion that we can or should have the final word on another person’s soul, and to approach moral reality with both clarity and mercy.

### Summary
- **Moral judgments matter** when they are exercised with discernment, truth, and a desire for clarity or restoration.
- **Moral judgments become dangerous** when they are driven by the appetite to condemn, which assumes final authority, fosters pride, and ignores our own need for grace.
- **Jesus’ command is not “don’t know right from wrong”** but “don’t let the desire to condemn control you. Instead, practice discernment with humility, and let forgiveness break the cycle of harshness.”

In short: discernment recognizes the truth; the appetite to condemn demands the verdict. The verse calls us to do the former, while guarding against the latter.
