Here is a concise answer:

The passage represents evil as a **judgment of the person’s direction of will**, not as beast, appetite, temptation, or habit.

- The Lord interrogates Cain’s **disposition**: “Why art thou wroth? and why is thy countenance fallen?” Wrath and fallenness suggest that the will itself has gone amiss; evil is detected in the bearing of the agent, not in some alien force sneaking in.
- “If thou doest well ... shalt thou not be accepted?” implies that sin is **not being well done**: that is, a failure of right action/willing.
- “Sin lieth at the door” places sin at the threshold of acceptance or exclusion: evil is **what bars admission into good standing**, a state resulting from how one acts.
- “Thou shalt rule over him” suggests that once one acts well, one’s authority follows; agency is **constitutive of social standing and rule**.

So the text portrays moral agency as the exercise of a will measured by its performance, its visible disposition, and its capacity to rule. Evil is neither beast nor impulse but a **defect in agency**: one whose will is “fallen” or “wroth” is not well ruled and therefore not accepted.
