This passage is sharp precisely because it is absurd, and that absurdity is the point.

Jesus, preaching in the Sermon on the Mount in Matthew 5-7, is not speaking in abstract moral terms here. He gives you an image you can't forget:

### 1. What makes the image so sharp?

**a) Extreme disproportion:**
In the King James Version you quoted, *mote* is *karphos* in Greek — a speck, sawdust, a shard. *Beam* is *dokos* — a rafter, a load-bearing plank, a log.

It's not a big speck versus a small speck. It's microscopic sawdust versus a telephone pole. The exaggeration is deliberate hyperbole, a common technique in Jewish wisdom teaching. It makes hypocritical judgment look not just sinful, but ridiculous.

**b) Comic blindness:**
A person with a plank sticking out of his own eye trying to perform delicate eye surgery on someone else is slapstick. You immediately picture someone stumbling around, blind, dangerous, and completely unaware of how he looks.

The hypocrite is blinded by his own fault — he literally cannot *see clearly* — and yet he presents himself as having the sharp vision needed to help another.

**c) Intimacy and presumption:**
The eye is one of the most sensitive, vulnerable parts of the body. To touch another person's eye requires gentleness, trust, and close proximity — "Let me pull out the mote out of *thine* eye."

The image exposes the presumption: to get that close to a brother to correct him requires a delicacy the hypocrite does not have, because he has not even examined himself with the same closeness.

**d) The carpenter's metaphor:**
To an audience of villagers who knew woodwork, sawdust and beams were everyday materials. Some scholars note the resonance: Jesus, known as the son of a carpenter, uses carpenter's imagery to talk about moral construction. You can't build rightly for others if your own house is structurally unsound.

### 2. How does it criticize hypocrisy without forbidding correction?

Read in isolation, Matthew 7:1 — "Judge not, that ye be not judged" — can sound like a total ban on moral judgment. Verses 3-5 clarify that Jesus is not forbidding discernment, but *hypocritical* judgment.

He does this in three moves:

**1. He attacks the sequence, not the act.**

> "Thou hypocrite, *first* cast out the beam out of thine own eye; *and then* shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote out of thy brother's eye."

He does not say: never cast out the mote. He says: first, then.

Moral correction is preserved as a final goal, but it is made conditional. Self-examination must come first.

**2. He redefines the qualification for helping.**

The critic is called "hypocrite" — *hypokrites*, an actor, someone playing a part. The sin is not that he sees the speck. The speck is really there. His brother *does* need help.

The sin is performance: pretending to be a clear-sighted helper and healer while ignoring a much larger fault in himself. It is a failure of proportion and self-knowledge. He magnifies the other's fault and minimizes his own.

In that sense, the passage is not about whether we should see faults, but *how* we see them. As Luke's parallel 6:41-42 and later teachings like John 7:24 ("judge righteous judgment") and Galatians 6:1 ("restore such an one in the spirit of meekness; considering thyself") show, the New Testament ethic is: correct with humility, not superiority.

**3. He makes self-correction the source of clarity, not an excuse to never act.**

The purpose of removing the beam is not endless self-obsession. It is so that "then shalt thou *see clearly* to cast out the mote."

A plank in your eye distorts all vision. Pride, resentment, lust, greed, or anger — whatever the "beam" is — makes you incapable of judging proportions fairly. Once it is dealt with honestly, you don't see *less* clearly about others, you see *more* clearly: with mercy, accuracy, and without the need to condemn in order to feel righteous.

In short: Jesus forbids judgment *from above*, but not help *from alongside*. The image allows for fraternal correction, but only after, and as a form of, self-correction.
