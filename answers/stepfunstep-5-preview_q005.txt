Enemy-love is hard to take seriously because it conflicts with our deepest reflexes: self-protection, resentment, and the desire for justice to mean punishment. It asks us to treat someone who has harmed or hated us not as a mere enemy, but as a person whom we still wish to receive good. That feels like it might excuse evil, erode boundaries, or make us weak.

It is not sentimental. If “love” means warm affection, then enemy-love would be impossible—and Jesus does not ask for instant fondness. In the text, love is concrete: *bless, do good, pray.* It is a discipline of the will and imagination, not an emotional mood. You can love an enemy while still hating the evil they have done.

It has a strategic dimension, but it is not merely strategic. Returning good for evil can break cycles of revenge, shame the adversary into reflection, and make reconciliation possible. Yet if enemy-love is only a tactic for self-interest, it collapses when it fails. And it must fail sometimes. A persecutor may never change.

The deeper answer is that enemy-love is a form of moral strength rooted in trust in God. It refuses to let the enemy control the terms of one’s life. It does not require pretending that injustice does not matter, but it places final judgment and mercy in God’s hands. The image of the sun and rain is crucial: God does not approve of evil, yet God remains patient toward both the evil and the good. To love enemies is to imitate that patience and give others the space to repent.

So it is both costly and freeing. It is not weakness, sweetness, or naïve niceness. In Matthew’s vision, enemy-love is the visible sign that a person has been formed by the character of God.
