In Ephesians 4:31–32, Paul is not just giving a random list of bad behaviors to avoid and good behaviors to adopt. He is diagnosing the anatomy of human conflict and prescribing the only theological cure strong enough to stop it. 

Here is a breakdown of why bitterness and forgiveness are placed in direct opposition, and how the text understands the nature of bitterness.

### 1. Why Bitterness and Forgiveness are Opposites
Bitterness and forgiveness are placed against each other because they represent two completely different ways of **keeping accounts** after an offense has occurred. 

Notice the progression in verse 31: **Bitterness** leads to **wrath** (wild rage) and **anger** (internal smoldering), which then erupts into **clamour** (brawling/shouting) and **evil speaking** (slander). Bitterness is at the head of this list because it is the *root* from which the other destructive behaviors grow. 

Bitterness is the decision to hold onto a debt and demand that the offender pay for it—if not in reality, then at least in your own mind. **Forgiveness** (Greek: *charizomai*, meaning to "grant as a favor" or "show grace") is the decision to cancel the debt. 

Paul places them in opposition because you cannot merely "put away" bitterness through sheer willpower. Human nature abhors a vacuum; if you try to stop being bitter without replacing it with something else, the resentment will simply return. The only way to uproot the *resentment* of a debt (bitterness) is to *cancel* the debt (forgiveness). 

Furthermore, Paul ties this to the Gospel ("even as God for Christ's sake hath forgiven you"). Bitterness says, *"You owe me."* Christian forgiveness says, *"Christ paid my massive debt to God, so I release you from your smaller debt to me."* 

### 2. What is Bitterness in this Text?
The Greek word translated as "bitterness" is *pikria*, which originally meant something pointed or sharp, and came to mean something acrid, sour, or poisonous to the taste. Based on the language and the broader context of Ephesians 4, the text treats bitterness as a combination of the categories you mentioned, specifically moving from a **chosen posture** to a **habit**, and finally to **something contagious**.

**It is more than a fleeting feeling:**
In biblical thought, we are rarely commanded to "put away" involuntary, split-second emotions. Earlier in the chapter (v. 26), Paul actually says, *"Be ye angry, and sin not: let not the sun go down upon your wrath."* Anger can be an immediate emotional reaction to an injustice. Bitterness, however, is what happens when the sun *does* go down on your anger—again and again. Bitterness is anger that has been fermented.

**It is a chosen posture and a habit:**
The command "Let all bitterness... be put away from you" follows the imagery Paul used a few verses earlier about taking off old, filthy clothes and putting on new ones (vv. 22–24). You do not accidentally wear a coat; you put it on. By using this phrasing, Paul treats bitterness as a **chosen posture**—an animus or a spirit of un-reconciled hostility that a person chooses to nurse and maintain. Over time, this posture hardens into a **habit** of the heart (the opposite of being "tenderhearted" in v. 32). A bitter person eventually views the whole world through a lens of cynicism and grievance.

**It is deeply contagious:**
Perhaps most importantly, the context of Ephesians 4 is entirely about the unity of the community (the Church). When Paul addresses the Ephesians, the "you" is plural. 

In the New Testament, bitterness is viewed as a toxin that refuses to stay contained within one person. (Hebrews 12:15 explicitly warns against a "root of bitterness springing up" because by it "many be defiled.") In Ephesians 4:31, you can watch the contagion spread: 
1. It starts silently inside one person (**bitterness**).
2. It heats up the internal temperature (**wrath and anger**).
3. It breaks out into the open, infecting the volume and tone of the room (**clamour**).
4. It actively seeks to destroy the reputation of others in the community (**evil speaking/slander**). 

### Summary
In this passage, bitterness is treated as a **chosen, habitual posture of the heart that becomes socially contagious**. It is the refusal to let go of a wrong, which inevitably poisons the community. Forgiveness, backed by a tender heart and kindness, is placed directly against it because grace is the only antidote strong enough to neutralize the poison and restore the community to health.
