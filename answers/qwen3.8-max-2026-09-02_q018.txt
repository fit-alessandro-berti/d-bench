They are placed directly against each other because Paul is not merely listing bad attitudes and good attitudes. He is contrasting two ways of living in the body of Christ: one that hardens the heart and fractures community, and one that reflects God’s mercy in Christ.

In context, Ephesians 4 is about the “old self” being put off and the “new self” being put on. The commands in verses 31–32 are not random. They describe what that transformation looks like in ordinary relationships.

## 1. Bitterness is treated as a root, not only a feeling

The word translated “bitterness” is **πικρία** (*pikria*), related to something sharp, acrid, or poisonous. It is not simply the pain of being wronged. It is resentment that has been allowed to settle in the soul.

The passage does not deny that hurt exists, nor does it condemn the initial flash of anger. Earlier in the same chapter Paul can say, “Be ye angry, and sin not” (Ephesians 4:26). So the text is not saying that the mere presence of painful emotion is sin.

But bitterness is more than a passing feeling. It is what anger becomes when it is nursed, rehearsed, and refused to God. It is a feeling that has hardened into a stance.

That is why bitterness stands at the head of the list:

> bitterness → wrath → anger → clamour → evil speaking → malice

There is a kind of progression here. Bitterness is the inward root. Wrath and anger are the heated expressions of that root. Clamour and evil speaking are the social outbursts. Malice is the settled desire to harm rather than bless.

So bitterness is not treated as a neutral emotion. It is treated as a dangerous spiritual condition.

## 2. Bitterness is a chosen posture of the heart

The grammar of verse 31 is important. Paul does not say, “Try to have less bitterness.” He says:

> “Let all bitterness … be put away from you.”

The command implies responsibility. Bitterness is not presented as something that simply happens to a person and can never be resisted. It is something that must be put away, removed, renounced.

This does not mean a person can instantly switch off emotional pain. But it does mean we are responsible for what we do with our pain. We can feed it, or we can bring it to God. We can rehearse the offense, or we can entrust it to the Lord. We can keep score, or we can remember that we have been forgiven.

In that sense, bitterness is a posture. It says:

> “I will not release this. I will hold onto it. I will let this wound define how I see that person.”

Forgiveness, by contrast, says:

> “I release this into God’s hands. I will not keep this debt alive in my heart.”

That is why bitterness and forgiveness are such direct opposites. Bitterness refuses to let go; forgiveness lets go. Bitterness keeps the wound open; forgiveness begins the healing of the wound.

## 3. Bitterness is also a habit

The next verse says:

> “And be ye kind one to another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another…”

The command to forgive is not a one-time act only. The Greek construction suggests an ongoing way of life: keep being kind, keep being tenderhearted, keep forgiving one another.

That implies that bitterness also has a habitual form. It is not always a single dramatic explosion. Often it is the slow practice of resentment: replaying the offense, rehearsing the argument, imagining future retaliation, interpreting everything in the worst possible light.

Paul therefore does not merely tell people to stop being bitter in one moment. He tells them to adopt an entirely different pattern of life:

- instead of bitterness, kindness;
- instead of hardness, tenderheartedness;
- instead of grudges, forgiveness.

The old habit must be replaced by a new habit. Bitterness is not overcome only by suppression. It is overcome by learning the way of grace.

## 4. Bitterness is socially contagious

The passage is addressed to a community: “from you,” “one to another,” “one another.” Paul is not only speaking about private inner feelings. He is speaking about the spiritual health of the church.

Bitterness rarely remains private. It leaks. It spreads through:

- wrath,
- anger,
- clamour,
- evil speaking,
- malice.

A bitter person often does not simply hurt himself. He creates a climate. He raises the temperature of relationships. He recruits others to his offense. He turns private wound into public division.

This is why “clamour” and “evil speaking” are included. Bitterness often expresses itself in raised voices, harsh speech, gossip, slander, and character assassination. It does not merely dwell inside someone; it contaminates relationships.

This is why forgiveness is also commanded communally:

> “forgiving one another”

The remedy is not only individual emotional management. The remedy is a community shaped by mercy.

## 5. Forgiveness is placed against bitterness because it is the gospel-shaped antidote

The climax of verse 32 is crucial:

> “…even as God for Christ’s sake hath forgiven you.”

Forgiveness is not presented as a mere moral duty. It is grounded in the gospel.

Paul does not say, “Forgive because it is healthy.” He says, in effect, “Forgive because you have been forgiven.” The Christian’s willingness to forgive comes from remembering that God has already dealt with us in mercy.

This is why bitterness is so spiritually serious. It is not simply an emotional problem. It is a grace problem. The bitter person begins to live as though he has not received mercy, or as though he deserves special exemption from the command to show mercy.

But the gospel says otherwise. We who have been forgiven much must forgive freely.

So forgiveness is placed directly against bitterness because bitterness is the refusal to extend what God has already given. Forgiveness is the practical expression of the mercy we have received.

## 6. So what is bitterness in this passage?

It is all of the things you mention, but especially a settled posture that becomes habitual and contagious.

- **Is it a feeling?**  
  It involves feeling, but it is more than a passing emotion. It is anger that has been harbored.

- **Is it a habit?**  
  Yes. It can become a practiced way of seeing people and relationships.

- **Is it a chosen posture?**  
  Yes. The command to put it away implies moral responsibility. We may not choose the first wound, but we are responsible for whether we nurse the wound or bring it to God.

- **Is it contagious?**  
  Absolutely. The surrounding terms—clamour, evil speaking, malice—show how bitterness spreads through a community.

The best summary would be this:

> In Ephesians 4:31–32, bitterness is not treated as a harmless feeling. It is a root of hostility that hardens the heart, shapes behavior, poisons speech, and threatens the unity of the body. Forgiveness is placed directly against it because forgiveness is the grace-formed practice that removes bitterness’s power and reflects God’s forgiveness in Christ.

So the text is not saying, “Never feel hurt.” It is saying, “Do not let hurt turn into a bitter kingdom in your heart. Be kind. Be tenderhearted. Forgive, because God in Christ has forgiven you.”
