The parable itself tells us exactly who Jesus was addressing: **“some who trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and despised others”** (Luke 18:9). That is the key. So the thing that condemns the Pharisee is not his fasting, tithing, or moral habits—it is his **self-righteousness**.

To be more precise:

- **Pride is the root.** He trusts in himself, not in God’s mercy. His prayer is not really a request; it is a self-congratulating monologue. He even prays “with himself” (Luke 18:11), which suggests he is talking to himself rather than to God.
- **Comparison is the method.** He defines his righteousness by saying, “I am not like other men” — especially the tax collector. But comparing yourself to someone worse is not the same as being righteous before God. God’s standard is God’s holiness, not the failure of your neighbor.
- **Contempt is the fruit.** He “despised others.” He treats the tax collector as a prop to make himself look good. That is not love of neighbor, nor love of God.
- **Virtue becomes self-display.** He fasts twice a week and gives tithes—good practices—but he uses them as badges of merit. His good works are not offered to God in gratitude; they are displayed as credentials. This turns religion into self-promotion.
- **There is also a false doctrine underneath.** The Pharisee is not a heretic in the ordinary sense, but he has a false gospel: he believes a person is made right with God by external achievements and separation from sinners. He has no need for mercy because he believes he has already earned God’s approval.

So the answer is: all of those things appear in the parable, but **pride / self-righteousness is the core**. Comparison, contempt, and the use of virtue as self-display are its symptoms.

Now, why is the tax collector’s posture spiritually truer?

Because he tells the truth about himself. He stands “afar off,” will not even lift his eyes to heaven, and beats his breast—a sign of mourning and repentance. His prayer is the exact opposite of the Pharisee’s: he brings no record of good deeds, no comparison to others. He simply says, **“God be merciful to me, a sinner”** (Luke 18:13).

That plea is a real act of faith. He is saying, “I have nothing to offer. I need God’s mercy, not my own merit.” He asks for the very thing the Pharisee never thinks to ask for: forgiveness. And the word Luke uses for “be merciful” is related to atonement—he asks God to cover his sin, to be the one who provides mercy.

Jesus then gives the verdict: **“This man went down to his house justified rather than the other”** (Luke 18:14). It is not because the tax collector was morally better. He is justified because he humbles himself and casts himself on God’s grace. The Pharisee leaves unhealed because he is already full of himself.

Jesus’ final sentence is the point: **“Everyone who exalts himself will be humbled, but the one who humbles himself will be exalted”** (Luke 18:14). The tax collector’s posture is truer because it is honest about who God is and who we are: God is merciful; we are sinners in need of mercy.
