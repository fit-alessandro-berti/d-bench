The passage exposes a contradiction between **honoring God and dishonoring those who bear God’s image**.

To bless God is to acknowledge his goodness and worth. Yet to curse another person—to wish them harm or speak of them with contempt—is to treat someone made in his likeness as though they possess no God-given dignity. James’s point is that these acts cannot be morally separated: **we cannot consistently reverence the Creator while despising his image in our neighbor.**

The contradiction is therefore deeper than inconsistent manners. It reveals a divided heart: devotion toward God alongside hostility toward people. “These things ought not so to be” means that such speech is incompatible with the allegiance our blessing professes.

### Why speech reveals moral disorder so quickly

Speech is where inward dispositions become outward actions. Anger, pride, envy, and contempt can remain concealed until someone frustrates us; then a few words expose how we actually regard them. The tongue can move faster than reflection, making it an especially immediate test of whether reverence has shaped our character or remains merely on our lips.

Speech also does more than reveal disorder—it **acts it out**. A curse makes another person the target of our hostility. With the same mouth, we can praise goodness and then inflict injury.

James’s nearby images of a spring and a fruit-bearing tree sharpen the point: what comes out should correspond to its source. When blessing and cursing flow together, something within needs healing.

This does not rule out truthful criticism, rebuke, or resistance to wrongdoing. It rules out treating the wrongdoer as beneath human dignity.

**The central question is: Has our love for God changed the way we speak to—and about—the people who bear his likeness?**
