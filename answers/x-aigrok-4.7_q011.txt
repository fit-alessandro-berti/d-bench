The sequence treats stopping evil as necessary but incomplete. “Cease to do evil” clears the ground; “learn to do well” then names what must be acquired in its place.

The verbs that follow are not abstract virtues. They are practiced acts: seek justice, relieve the oppressed, defend the fatherless, plead for the widow. Goodness here is not mere restraint—the absence of wrongdoing—but a learned habit of justice and care, especially toward those who cannot secure it for themselves. Ritual cleansing (“wash you, make you clean”) is rejected if it is not joined to that active practice. The text therefore presents righteousness as something formed by doing, not as a passive state left once evil has been put away.
