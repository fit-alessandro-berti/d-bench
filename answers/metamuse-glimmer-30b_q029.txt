Galatians 6:1 is a hermeneutic of correction, not a technique for discipline. *Paraptōmenos* is not a rebel but a man overtaken, caught off guard, like a prey. *Katartizo* is what is said of mending a broken bone, putting a joint back into place. The aim is restoration to wholeness and fellowship, not exposure and expulsion.

The one who restores is *hoi pneumatikoi*, the spiritual, i.e. the mature, the ones who live by the Spirit and not by the law code that divides the Galatians. Maturity is not authority to condemn but capacity to bear the other's weakness. Hence the manner is *en pneumati prautētos*, in the spirit of meekness.

Prautes is not weakness. It is strength held in check, the gentleness of one who can be firm without being crushing. Meekness is required because restoration is done to a brother, not to an enemy, and the goal is the other's return to communion. Harshness would re-traumatize and confirm the fault; gentleness keeps the door open for repentance.

That is why self-suspicion is tied to it: *skopos heautou*. Consider yourself. The corrector is not a spectator over sin but a fellow exposé. *Hina mē kai su peirastheis*, lest you also be tempted. The Greek *peirazō* here is to be tried and fall. Paul is not counseling morbid introspection but realistic anthropology: the same weakness that caught the brother can catch you. Pride in correcting is itself a temptation.

The three are kept together because each checks the other.

* Restoration without meekness becomes legalistic correction, punishment dressed as piety.
* Meekness without self-suspicion becomes condescending compassion, the self-righteous mercy of one who feels safe from the fault.
* Self-suspicion without restoration becomes paralysis and moral quietism.

Together they form a posture of humble solidarity: you intervene because you are responsible for the body, you intervene gently because you are not superior to the body, and you intervene with self-distrust because you belong to the body. It is the logic of Matt 7:3-5 and Rom 2:1, the correction that is practiced by those who know they stand by grace and can fall by the same grace. In Pauline terms it is the ethic of the cross: to restore the other in the same meekness in which Christ restores us, and to know oneself as the first candidate for mercy.
