Repentance involves both **“way” and “thoughts”** because Isaiah describes a turning of the whole person—not merely a change in visible behavior.

- **“Way”** refers to one’s course of life: habits, choices, actions, and the direction one is taking. To forsake a wicked way means actually leaving wrongdoing behind, not simply regretting it.
- **“Thoughts”** refers to the inward purposes, intentions, and judgments that shape that course. A person can outwardly conform while inwardly cherishing the same pride, resentment, or desire to harm. Repentance therefore reaches beneath conduct to what motivates and sustains it.

The two expressions form a poetic parallel: the wicked person and the unrighteous person are not necessarily two different kinds of people. Together, they portray wrongdoing in its outward and inward dimensions.

This has an important implication for **inward imagination**: what we deliberately entertain, justify, and cultivate matters, even when it has not yet become an action. But the verse need not be read as equating every unwanted thought or passing temptation with a settled sinful purpose. Its emphasis is on forsaking the inward orientation that supports an unrighteous life.

Yet repentance is more than abandoning something. It is also **returning to someone**: “let him return unto the Lord.” The movement is away from wrongdoing and toward restored relationship with God.

And the promised response is not reluctant acceptance but **mercy and abundant pardon**. The call to repent does not imply that the sinner must first achieve flawless conduct and a perfectly purified mind before approaching God. It invites the person to turn toward God precisely because forgiveness is available.

In short: **outward reform without inward change is incomplete; inward regret without a changed direction is also incomplete. But neither depth of wrongdoing nor the need for deep inward renewal places the returning person beyond God’s mercy.**
