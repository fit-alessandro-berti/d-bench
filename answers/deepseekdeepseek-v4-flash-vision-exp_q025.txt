The verse’s danger is not **visibility itself**, but the **craving to be seen** — what early Christian tradition calls “vainglory.”  

Jesus says, “Take heed that ye do not your alms before men, **to be seen of them**.” The phrase “to be seen” gives the motive. The problem is not that people see you; the problem is that you perform the good deed **in order** to be seen. The public eye becomes your audience, and human applause becomes your reward.

## Visibility is not the sin

Jesus elsewhere tells His disciples:

> “Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father which is in heaven.” — Matthew 5:16

There, public goodness is commanded. But the goal is different: the purpose is that God the Father gets the glory, not that the disciple gets the attention. So the issue is not the visibility of the act; it’s the **direction of the heart**.

## The craving to be seen is the root

Why is craving attention morally dangerous? Because it makes **approval** the motive for goodness. That reverses the created order:

- Good works should be **fueled by love for God and neighbor**.
- Good works for attention are **fueled by pride and self-love**.

When the “craving to be seen” is the driving force, goodness is reduced to a performance. You begin to care more about **looking righteous** than being righteous. You may even choose public, applaudable acts over quiet, truly sacrificial ones. The soul gets trained to value appearances over truth.

And this is a dangerous corruption: a person can give generously, pray beautifully, and fast visibly — all for the “reward” of being admired. The outward deed looks the same, but the inner worship is actually **self-worship**. That is why Jesus says, “By the time you have received the reward of men, you have already been paid in full. You have no reward with your Father who is in heaven.”

## The reward is not lost because God is stingy

It is lost because **the act was not directed toward God**. If you do a kindness so that people will praise you, you have chosen your reward. The applause of men is the payment. God does not owe you a double payment. His reward is for the one who acts for His sake, not for the sake of reputation.

## So, is public goodness dangerous?

Yes — but only if you are not careful. Public goodness is dangerous because:

- It can feed pride without your noticing.
- It can turn a genuine love into a desire for recognition.
- It can make you addicted to the praise of others, so that your worth becomes dependent on human opinion.
- It can undermine even the holiest acts, making them a means of self-glorification.

But public goodness is not always bad. The good Christian must ask: **“If no one ever saw this, if no one ever thanked me, would I still do it?”** If the answer is yes, then your aim is not praise but faithfulness.

Jesus says elsewhere in the same passage:

> “But when thou doest alms, let not thy left hand know what thy right hand doeth: that thine alms may be in secret: and thy Father which seeth in secret himself shall reward thee openly.” — Matthew 6:3–4

Secret giving is not a permanent rule for all public charity; it is a **heart-training exercise**. It teaches you to be truly good without needing an audience. The goal is to be free from the craving to be seen, so that whether your good work is seen or unseen, your one audience is God.

**In short:** The moral danger is not the act of being seen. The moral danger is **worshipping the eye of the beholder instead of the face of the Father.**
