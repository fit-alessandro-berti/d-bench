### 1. Why pride links to “blindness and collapse” (not just guilt)

In the proverbial frame, pride isn’t merely a moral failing that earns divine or social disapproval—it’s structurally destabilizing. Here’s why it tends toward blindness and collapse rather than just culpability:

**Cognitive narrowing / “tunnel vision”**  
Pride operates as a self-reinforcing frame. When someone identifies strongly with being “superior,” “right,” or “above,” the psyche selectively admits information that confirms that identity and screens out anything that threatens it. This isn’t accidental; it’s a defense mechanism. The result is a functional blind spot: the proud person cannot see risks, limits, or the needs of others because those things would require ego-deflation. Guilt, by contrast, orients a person toward repair; pride orients a person toward preservation of the self-image.

**Loss of reality-testing**  
Arrogance often manifests as an inflated sense of control or foresight. In systems theory, this is a positive feedback loop: the more one assumes control, the less one seeks feedback, the more unexpected shocks arrive, and the harder the fall. The “collapse” isn’t necessarily a moral punishment—it’s the inevitable result of a system that has disabled its own corrective pathways. When the fall comes, it’s often total because the prideful structure had no contingency, no humility-buffered humility to absorb shock.

**Relational rupture as a collapse catalyst**  
Most human systems (families, teams, polities) survive on reciprocity and mutual constraint. Pride breaks the reciprocity loop. Others withdraw, withhold information, or actively resist. The proud person then experiences the consequences not as “justice” but as sudden isolation. The collapse is thus social first, structural second.

### 2. What the contrast with “the lowly” reveals about the moral psychology of arrogance

> *“Better it is to be of an humble spirit with the lowly, than to divide the spoil with the proud.”*

This contrast does a lot of heavy lifting. It’s not just “humble = good, proud = bad.” It maps two distinct orientations toward power, knowledge, and self:

**Pride treats relationships as hierarchical competitions**  
“Divide the spoil with the proud” implies a transactional, zero-sum model: there’s a pile of gain, and one must take their share from a position of dominance. The proud person’s moral psychology is shot through with *comparative reference*—worth is derived from being above others. Humility, by contrast, operates with *circular reference*: worth is derived from integrity, connection, or contribution rather than ranking.

**The “lowly”/humble spirit is grounded in reality-testing**  
The Hebrew word often translated “lowly” (`anav`) carries shades of “afflicted,” “meek,” but also “teachable.” The contrast suggests that humility isn’t weakness—it’s a form of epistemic honesty. The humble person can walk alongside those with fewer resources or status because they aren’t threatened by comparison. Their identity isn’t on the line in every interaction. Arrogance, by definition, makes every interaction a test of rank; humility makes most interactions simply *transactions* or *shared tasks*.

**Why the proverb pairs “blindness/collapse” with the “lowly” option**  
The structure implies that the proud person’s downfall isn’t just poetic justice—it’s a failure mode. The humble person, walking “with the lowly,” stays connected to the ground, to feedback, to the real limits of situations. The proud person, trying to *divide spoils* from a height, eventually loses the spoils *and* the position.

### Summary
Pride links to blindness and collapse because it short-circuits the feedback loops that keep individuals and groups stable. It’s not just “sinful”—it’s psychologically brittle. The humility/lowly contrast reveals that arrogance is a relationship strategy built on comparison and defensiveness, while humility is a strategy built on groundedness and openness. One leads to a fall because it can’t bend; the other survives because it can yield without breaking.
