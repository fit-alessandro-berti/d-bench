Jesus isn’t saying, “Moral judgments never matter.” He is saying something more demanding: don’t take the seat of God and condemn people. The difference is between **discernment**—which is necessary, good, and commanded—and **the appetite to condemn**—which is the self-righteous, merciless desire to sit in judgment over others.

First, look at the context. Luke 6:37 follows straight after the command, “Be merciful, just as your Father is merciful” (Luke 6:36). The whole passage is about mercy, not moral indifference. Jesus is addressing people who use judgment as a weapon: they are quick to point out faults, quick to condemn, slow to forgive. He tells them to stop.

The Greek word for “judge” here, *krinō*, can mean to distinguish, decide, or pass sentence. The word for “condemn,” *katadikazō*, is stronger: it means to pronounce someone guilty and sentence them. So Jesus is prohibiting a certain kind of judgment: the final, punitive, hypocritical judgment that writes a person off. He is not prohibiting the ordinary discernment that tells right from wrong.

In fact, Jesus commands discernment elsewhere: “Do not judge by appearances, but judge with right judgment” (John 7:24). He also says, “You will know them by their fruits” (Matthew 7:20). The Bible repeatedly calls believers to test the spirits, evaluate teaching, and lovingly correct one another. That is impossible if moral judgments never matter.

So what is the difference between discernment and the appetite to condemn?

**Discernment** is a matter of sight. It sees a situation honestly, names good and evil, and aims at healing. It is humble because it knows its own limits and its own sin. Discernment begins with self-examination: Jesus says, “First take the log out of your own eye, and then you will see clearly to take the speck out of your brother’s eye” (Matthew 7:5). The goal is not to humiliate the brother but to help remove the speck. Discernment is motivated by love, and it leaves room for repentance and forgiveness.

**The appetite to condemn** is a matter of appetite. It craves the thrill of being right and the power of putting someone else down. It does not want truth; it wants a verdict. It is proud, blind to its own faults, and merciless. It treats the other person as a case, not a person. It enjoys the role of judge and forgets that it, too, stands under judgment.

The verse itself proves that moral judgments matter. Forgiveness only makes sense if something real has happened. If there is no such thing as sin, then there is nothing to forgive. But Jesus says, “forgive, and you will be forgiven.” That presupposes that offenses exist and that our response matters. It also presupposes that God’s judgment is real. The verse is not saying “judgment is meaningless”; it is saying “the measure you use will be measured back to you” (Luke 6:38). Our moral judgments are so significant that they shape how God will deal with us.

So the key is not to stop judging altogether, but to refuse to condemn. We can call an act wrong. We can warn against harmful behavior. We can hold people accountable. We can even, in a church setting, treat unrepentant sin seriously. But we must not act as if we are the final judge. We are not God. We are fellow sinners who have been forgiven, and we are called to extend the same mercy we have received.

In short, Luke 6:37 is not a blank check for relativism. It is a warning against the lust to condemn. Discernment says, “This action is wrong, but you are more than your worst moment, and I want restoration for you.” Condemnation says, “You are guilty, and I am done with you.” Jesus tells us to practice the first and drop the second. Moral judgments matter greatly—but they must be shaped by humility, love, and forgiveness.
