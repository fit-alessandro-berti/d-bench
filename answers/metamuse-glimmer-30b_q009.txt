Romans 12:20-21 is not a call to passive niceness or to clever psychological warfare. It is the climax of Paul's section on the ethics of the renewed mind in Romans 12:9-21.

The victory Paul defines is not winning over the enemy, it is not being won *by* the enemy.

*Be not overcome of evil* - Greek *mē hupaireō* - is literally "do not be overpowered, overpowered so as to be carried away". The point is not to return evil for evil and thus be assimilated into the same cycle of retaliation that defines evil. Victory is to keep your moral and spiritual integrity intact when evil is pressing on you.

*but overcome evil with good* - *to kakon* is overcome by *to agathon*. The verb *nikao* is the same word used for conquering. Paul is saying a moral conquest is possible, but it is conquest by a different means than evil uses. You disarm evil by refusing to play by its rules and by acting in the logic of God, which is love and generosity.

The "heap coals of fire on his head" is a quotation of Proverbs 25:21-22. In Proverbs the image is of hospitality offered to an enemy: feed him when he is hungry, give him drink when he is thirsty. The coals are not a threat from you. In ancient Near Eastern hospitality the coals were needed to warm food and water. In the Jewish tradition the image is of melting, not burning: just as hot coals soften metal, undeserved kindness softens a hard heart and can lead to shame and repentance. It is the way God deals with Israel, and now with the world, in Romans 12:19-21.

That is why the command is genuinely strong, not weak and not manipulative.

It is not weak because it is not avoidance. It requires the active, costly restraint of vengeance, and the active generosity of feeding and giving drink to someone who has harmed you. It is the opposite of self-protection. Weakness would be "be not overcome of evil" meaning do nothing and absorb harm. Paul commands a deliberate, costly action.

It is not manipulative in the modern sense of kindness as a tactic to make the enemy feel guilty so you win. The kindness is offered for the enemy's good, and for your own conformity to God. The "coals" are not something you heap as a punishment; they are the natural consequence of God's kind treatment being reflected in you. The aim is not your vindication but the enemy's potential transformation, and the preservation of your own likeness to God who "makes his sun rise on the evil and on the good" in Matt 5:45.

In short, victory over evil is defined here as refusing to be mastered by it, and instead mastering it by breaking its cycle with good. That is strong precisely because it is self-giving and non-retaliatory.
