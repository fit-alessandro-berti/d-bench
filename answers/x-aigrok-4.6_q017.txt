**Light is hated because it removes the option of remaining unseen, unaccounted-for, and unrevised.** The passage treats “light” as that which makes deeds, motives, and inconsistencies visible—truth as exposure rather than mere information. People who prefer darkness are not usually rejecting facts in the abstract; they are protecting a self-image, a set of practices, or a social position that cannot survive clear seeing.

### Exposure and the mechanics of hiding
Evil (or even ordinary self-serving) deeds typically require opacity: secrecy, rationalization, compartmentalization, or selective attention. Light functions like an audit. Once something is fully visible, three things become harder:
- Continued denial (“I didn’t really do that / it wasn’t that bad”).
- Continued performance of the act without internal friction.
- Continued social cover, because others can now see the same thing.

The text’s claim is psychological as well as moral: the person “hateth the light… lest his deeds should be reproved.” Reproof here is not only external judgment; it is the collapse of the story one has been telling oneself. That collapse produces shame—the specifically social and self-regarding pain of being seen as one actually is, rather than as one needs to appear. Shame is more about identity and belonging than about isolated acts; it therefore motivates hiding more powerfully than guilt often does.

### Truth, shame, and the desire not to be seen clearly
Wanting not to be seen clearly is often a desire to keep incompatible parts of the self from colliding. A person can hold a high self-concept (“I am decent, competent, justified”) while acting in ways that contradict it, provided the contradiction stays in shadow. Full illumination forces a choice: revise the self-concept, revise the behavior, or endure chronic dissonance. Many choose neither revision; they choose reduced light.

This is not limited to dramatic “evil.” Everyday versions include:
- Avoiding feedback that would show incompetence or hypocrisy.
- Curating what others (and oneself) are allowed to notice.
- Preferring narratives that flatter or excuse over those that constrain.
- Treating certain topics, memories, or data as radioactive because they would require costly change.

The opposite movement in the passage—“he that doeth truth cometh to the light, that his deeds may be made manifest”—describes someone whose actions can survive inspection, or who is willing to let inspection change them. Visibility becomes confirmation rather than threat. The deeds are “wrought in God” in the sense that they do not depend on remaining hidden.

### Why the preference for darkness is stubborn
Darkness is not merely absence of information; it is a managed environment. It lets people keep both the benefits of the hidden behavior and the benefits of an unthreatened identity. Light is therefore experienced as aggression even when it is simply accurate. Philosophically this tracks older observations (Plato’s cave-dwellers preferring shadows; later analyses of motivated reasoning and self-deception): people often love the comfort of the unexamined more than they love being right.

The passage does not claim that everyone who avoids light is cartoonishly wicked. It claims a structural relationship: the more one’s life depends on not being fully seen, the more threatening illumination becomes, and the more one will resent or flee the source of that illumination. Coming to the light, conversely, is portrayed as alignment—actions that no longer require concealment.

The dynamic is recognizable outside any particular theology: wherever truth functions as exposure, those with something to lose by being seen will treat the light as the enemy.
