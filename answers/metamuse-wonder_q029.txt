In Galatians 6:1, correction is not an act of superiority. It is an act of **humble, restorative solidarity**.

Paul binds three things together because each protects the others:

### 1. Restoration is the *goal*

> “...restore such an one...”

The Greek word, *katartizete*, was used for mending torn nets and setting a dislocated bone back into place. It does not mean punish, expose, humiliate, or discard. It means **repair and reintegrate**.

The person is described as “overtaken in a fault” — *paraptoma*, a slip or trespass, as of someone caught or surprised by sin, not necessarily a hardened rebel enthroned in it. That language itself invites compassion: this is a brother overtaken, not an enemy defeated.

So the aim is medicinal, not retributive: to bring a fellow believer back to wholeness and back into fellowship.

### 2. Meekness is the *manner*

> “...in the spirit of meekness...”

Meekness here is not softness about sin. It is strength without harshness — no contempt, no gloating, no need to dominate, no pleasure in being right.

This connects directly to Galatians 5:22-23, where meekness/gentleness is fruit of the Spirit. That is why Paul says “ye which are spiritual” should do this. He does not mean an elite class of Christians. He means those actually walking by the Spirit (5:16, 25) will correct in the Spirit’s manner, rather than biting and devouring one another (5:15) or provoking one another out of vainglory (5:26).

Without meekness, “restoration” becomes coercion and shaming. It may force outward compliance while breaking the person inwardly.

### 3. Self-suspicion is the *guard*

> “...considering thyself, lest thou also be tempted.”

*Considering* — *skopōn* — means keep watching, examine continuously. The corrector must remember: *I am capable of this, or of worse. I am not correcting from above the human condition.*

The temptation is double:
- temptation to the **same sin** that overtook the other person, and
- temptation, in the very act of correcting, to **pride, contempt, self-righteousness, and harshness**.

That is why the next verses continue: “Bear ye one another’s burdens” (6:2), and “if a man think himself to be something, when he is nothing, he deceiveth himself” (6:3).

Without self-suspicion, meekness becomes a performance and restoration becomes moral theater — I “restore” you mainly to prove I am not like you.

**Together, the three form one posture:**

- **Restoration** without meekness becomes cruelty.
- **Meekness** without restoration becomes indifference that abandons the sinner.
- **Both** without self-suspicion become Pharisaism.

Paul’s required posture is therefore that of a fellow sinner-physician: clear-eyed about the fault, determined to heal rather than punish, gentle in method, and deeply aware that the one correcting stands in the same need of grace.
