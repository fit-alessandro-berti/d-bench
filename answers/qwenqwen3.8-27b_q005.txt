

Enemy-love is difficult because it asks us to break one of the strongest human instincts: **the instinct to return evil for evil**. When someone hurts, insults, betrays, or persecutes us, our first moral response is often outrage. That response can be understandable and even necessary for justice. But Jesus’ command in Matthew 5:44–45 goes deeper than merely saying “do not hate” or “be tolerant.” It says: *love, bless, do good, and pray* for those who mistreat you.

That is why it sounds so hard. It challenges not only our anger, but our self-image. It asks us to become “children of the Father in heaven,” whose care extends even to the evil and the unjust: the sun rises and rain falls on both the good and the bad.

Is enemy-love **sentimental**?  
Only if we reduce “love” to a pleasant feeling. Jesus is not commanding a soft emotional glow toward enemies. The words are active: bless, do good, pray. Enemy-love is not about feeling affection toward someone who harms you. It is a deliberate moral orientation: refusing to mirror evil with evil, refusing to dehumanize the enemy, and maintaining the enemy’s status as a person made in God’s image, even when they act as if they do not share that image.

In that sense, it is not sentimental at all. It is austere. It demands self-control, compassion, and endurance. It can coexist with grief, anger, sorrow, and moral condemnation of the enemy’s actions. One can hate the sin, resist the evil, and still refuse to become like the enemy.

Is it **strategic**?  
It can have strategic effects, but that is not its primary purpose. There is a real social and spiritual power in non-retaliation: it can disarm cycles of vengeance, expose the ugliness of hatred, preserve relationships, open the possibility of reconciliation, and prevent minor conflicts from becoming total war. In that sense, enemy-love can be politically and socially wise.

But if we treat it mainly as strategy, we corrupt it. A strategy says, “I love you because it will make you behave better.” Enemy-love as Jesus presents it says, “I love you because I am imitating the Father, whose mercy is not limited to those who deserve it.” If love becomes a tool for control, it becomes manipulation. If it is only a peace-keeping technique, it will fail when the enemy does not respond.

Is it a **deeper form of moral strength**?  
Yes. That is the best answer.

Enemy-love is difficult precisely because it is powerful. It requires more moral strength than revenge. Revenge is natural; it satisfies pride, protects the ego, and gives a feeling of control. Enemy-love is counter-natural. It requires a person to say, “I will not be governed by your evil.” It requires refusing to be turned into the very person one despises.

This is why Jesus calls it the mark of being “children of the Father in heaven.” The Father’s goodness is not earned by human behavior. The sun and rain are given to the just and unjust alike. In the same way, God’s love is not limited to the lovable, the innocent, or the righteous. It extends beyond moral desert.

So enemy-love is not a sentimental ideal. It is not merely a social strategy. It is a deeper moral strength: the strength to break the chain of retaliation, to preserve one’s own humanity under attack, and to trust God with justice while refusing to become a destroyer.

In practice, this does not mean ignoring sin or submitting to abuse. Enemy-love does not necessarily require staying in a harmful relationship, excusing injustice, or allowing someone to continue hurting others. It means responding to the enemy without becoming the enemy. It means maintaining moral clarity: the enemy’s actions may be evil, but the enemy is not to be hated, cursed, or treated as less than human.

That is why it is so hard. It asks us to be merciful in a world that often says mercy is weakness. But in Jesus’ teaching, it is the opposite: it is the strength of those who belong to a Father whose love is wider than justice’s circle and deeper than human pride.
