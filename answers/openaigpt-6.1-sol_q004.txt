The trust asked for is **wholehearted reliance on God rather than treating one’s own judgment as the final authority**. It is not a command to stop thinking; it is a command not to make one’s thinking self-sufficient.

### Humble self-distrust

“Lean not unto thine own understanding” challenges the assumption that *what makes sense to me must therefore be right*. Our judgment is limited—not only by lack of information, but also by fear, pride, appetite, and self-interest. We can reason carefully and still rationalize what we want.

Humble self-distrust therefore says:

> “I should use my judgment, but I should not regard it as infallible. I need instruction, correction, and wisdom beyond myself.”

That is different from self-contempt. The point is not that we are incapable of knowing anything, but that we are neither omniscient nor morally neutral.

### Not anti-intellectualism

Proverbs itself repeatedly commends wisdom, knowledge, understanding, and attentiveness to counsel. Indeed, Proverbs 3:13 calls the person who gains understanding blessed. So this passage cannot plausibly mean, “Understanding is bad.”

The distinction is between **using understanding** and **leaning on it as one’s ultimate support**. Reason remains a gift and a responsibility; it simply does not occupy God’s place. Nor does the verse justify treating an impulse, a religious leader’s assertion, or a supposed private revelation as beyond examination.

### “In all thy ways acknowledge him”

This asks for more than occasionally remembering that God exists. It means recognizing God’s authority throughout life: allowing one’s plans, motives, and conduct to be answerable to him. Trust becomes practical in obedience, prayer, receptiveness to correction, and willingness to do what is right even when its benefits are not immediately apparent.

“He shall direct thy paths”—also translated “make straight your paths”—promises divine guidance, not an effortless life or advance certainty about every decision.

In short, **anti-intellectualism says, “Do not examine.” Humble trust says, “Examine honestly, but do not assume that your perspective is the whole truth.”** The passage asks for confidence in God’s wisdom joined to humility about our own.
