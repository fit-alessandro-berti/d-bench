Luke 6:37 is not a command to switch off moral perception. It's a command to relinquish a particular *use* of moral perception: the eager, final, self-exempting verdict we pass on other people.

That distinction matters because the verse itself assumes morality is real. Jesus says, "forgive, and ye shall be forgiven." Forgiveness only makes sense if something genuinely wrong has happened. If nothing can be judged wrong, there is nothing to forgive — only things to excuse, ignore, or redefine. Mercy is not indifference. Mercy sees the wrong clearly and refuses vengeance anyway.

It is also significant that Jesus says this in Luke 6, the same chapter where he pronounces "woe" on the rich, the full, and the praised (6:24-26), and later says a tree is known by its fruit (6:43-45). Jesus is not forbidding discernment. He is practicing it.

The difference is between **discernment** and **the appetite to condemn**.

### Discernment judges acts in order to love truth and people

Discernment asks:

- What actually happened?
- Is it true? Is it harmful? Is it unjust?
- What is my responsibility here?
- How can there be protection, repentance, repair, or restoration?

It can be firm. Discernment can say: *That was a lie. That was abuse. That teaching is false. That person is not safe to trust with this.* It can set boundaries, confront, warn, and even separate. Scripture elsewhere commands this kind of judgment: "Judge not according to the appearance, but judge righteous judgment" (John 7:24), and "by their fruits ye shall know them" (Matthew 7:20).

But discernment remains specific, provisional, and self-inclusive. It judges an act or pattern without pretending to know the final worth of a soul. It is open to new facts. It applies the same standard to itself first — which is why Jesus immediately follows with the log and the speck (Luke 6:41-42).

Discernment is often reluctant. It grieves over wrong because it wants the person back.

### Condemnation judges persons in order to feel righteous

Condemnation asks a different question:

- What *kind* of person is this?
- How am I better than them?
- Can I place them outside the circle of hope?

The second verb in Luke 6:37 is stronger: "condemn not." Condemnation is not just noticing fault; it is passing sentence. It totalizes: not "you did something deceitful," but "you *are* a liar, that's all you are, and there's no road back." It treats our limited knowledge as if it were God's final judgment.

The appetite to condemn has telltale signs:

1. **Eagerness.** It enjoys finding fault. It feels energized by outrage rather than sobered by it.
2. **Selectivity.** It magnifies others' sins while explaining away its own.
3. **Finality.** It leaves no room for repentance, growth, or being wrong.
4. **Contempt.** The goal quietly shifts from helping or protecting to dismissing.
5. **Identity.** It needs an enemy in order to feel clean, orthodox, safe, or superior.

Discernment is perception in the service of love. Condemnation is perception in the service of the ego.

### A useful test

When you make a moral judgment, ask:

- Would I be glad to learn I was mistaken?
- Have I judged myself by this same standard?
- Does this lead me toward prayer, truth-telling, protection, or repair — or mainly toward contempt?
- Am I judging something specific and knowable, or pronouncing on someone's ultimate worth?
- Does my judgment leave a door open for grace?

If the judgment can survive those questions, it may be discernment, even if it's painful. If it cannot, it may be the appetite Jesus warns against.

So Luke 6:37 does not mean moral judgments never matter. It means they matter so much that we may not use them as weapons of self-righteousness. We are to tell the truth about good and evil without seizing God's seat, and to meet real wrongs — in others and in ourselves — with the mercy we hope to receive.
