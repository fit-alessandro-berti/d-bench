**His deepest problem is pride that turns service into a claim on his father’s affection—and makes him unable to celebrate grace given to his brother.** Anger, envy, and wounded fairness are expressions of that deeper problem, not complete explanations of it.

### His grievance is real, but it is not the whole problem

Jesus does not portray every accusation as false. The elder son has served for many years, and the younger son really has squandered the inheritance. There is genuine loss and injustice to acknowledge.

But the elder moves from a legitimate complaint to an untenable conclusion: **because his record is better, he is owed something different, and his family’s joy must be governed by desert.** He can acknowledge that the younger son has harmed the family while still assuming that restoration and celebration are rewards he has effectively purchased through obedience.

### His accounting reveals the pride

Notice the elder’s language:

> “These many years do I serve thee… I never transgressed… yet thou never gavest me a kid.”

He is effectively presenting a balance sheet. He does not primarily say, “I love my father and share his grief”; he says, in effect, **“Look at what I have contributed and what I am owed.”**

That does not mean his service was worthless. It means he has reduced his relationship with his father to an exchange: service for compensation. In theological terms, he is not merely proud of his obedience; he is relying on it to establish his standing.

### The father’s reply exposes his misunderstanding

The father answers:

> “Son, thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine.”

The decisive point is that **belonging is already his**. His sonship is not conditional upon a better record than his brother’s. The feast, therefore, cannot be a wage paid for the elder son’s merit; it is a family celebration of the younger son’s return.

The elder’s repeated language—“this thy son,” then “this thy brother”—shows his relational withdrawal. He will not simply call the younger man “my brother” and share the father’s joy. He must remind everyone of the younger son’s guilt, as though the celebration could be justified only by maintaining a hierarchy of guilt and merit.

### How the proposed answers relate

- **Anger** is the overt response, but it does not explain the heart beneath it.
- **Envy** supplies the comparison: the younger son’s restoration seems to diminish the elder’s distinction.
- **Wounded fairness** is the elder’s stated grievance. It may contain truth, but it becomes a claim that others must be measured by their deserts.
- **Pride in obedience** is the root: he treats service as a purchase of love and significance rather than the life of a son who already belongs.
- **Discomfort with mercy** is the clearest expression of that pride. He is not troubled by love shown to himself; he is troubled by love shown to someone who, in his accounting, does not deserve it.

**So the best summary is this: the elder son cannot bear unmerited mercy because he has made grace into a wage.** His deepest problem is not simply that he is angry, but that he cannot enter a celebration of restoration without insisting on his own moral superiority. He must relinquish the ledger before he can share the feast.
