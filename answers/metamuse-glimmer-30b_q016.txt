The anger is real, but it is not the root. It is the fruit.

The elder brother is angry because he is envious, he is envious because his sense of fairness is wounded, and his sense of fairness is wounded because his whole identity is built on pride in obedience. All of that is true, but all of it is a symptom of one deeper disorder: he does not want a Father, he wants an employer.

Listen to his own self-description. He does not say “Father, these many years have I been with you.” He says “Lo, these many years do I serve thee, neither transgressed I at any time thy commandment.” It is the language of a hired servant, not a son. He has been in the house, but he has never been at home.

That is why he can say with perfect sincerity, “thou never gavest me a kid, that I might make merry with my friends.” The relationship has been transactional for him. He has kept the contract and he expects the payment. The father has never withheld anything from him, because “all that I have is thine,” but the elder brother has never experienced it as gift, only as wages due.

So when the father acts like a father — extravagantly, gratuitously, joyfully merciful to the prodigal — the elder brother experiences it as an injustice. Mercy is not a category he can tolerate, because mercy undoes his merit. If the son who squandered everything is embraced without earning, then the older son’s entire story of “I never transgressed” collapses. His righteousness no longer distinguishes him, it only isolates him.

Jesus tells this parable to the Pharisees and scribes who “murmured” because he receives sinners. The elder brother is the religious person who is present at the feast but cannot enter it. He is not angry that his brother came home; he is angry that the father is glad. He wants God to be a strict accountant of obedience, not a Father who rejoices over the lost being found.

In that sense he is in fact the more lost one. The prodigal knew he was dead and came home for life. The elder brother is alive in the house and dead in his heart, because he has never known the mercy that makes sonship possible. The father’s final invitation is not a rebuke of his anger but a call to re-enter sonship: you are ever with me, and all I have is yours. Come in and be glad.

That is why the deepest problem is not merely wounded fairness or even pride. It is a discomfort with mercy itself, and the pride in obedience that makes mercy feel like an insult.
