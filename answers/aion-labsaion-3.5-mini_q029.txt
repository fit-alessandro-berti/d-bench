## The posture: a physician who knows he is a patient

The key verb is *katartizete* ("restore") — a craftsman's and physician's word, used for mending torn nets (Matt 4:21), setting a dislocated limb, fitting equipment for use. Paul does not say "punish," "examine," or "pronounce on." He says *mend*. And the fallen man is described with the same tenderness: he has been "overtaken" (*prolēmphthē* — ambushed, caught before he could escape) in a "fault" (*paraptōma* — a misstep, a false step). This is a brother caught off guard by sin, not a rebel on trial. The posture required, then, is that of someone kneeling at a fracture site — careful hands, healing intent, no rough handling that would make the break worse. Correction here is surgery, not sentencing.

## Meekness is not merely the manner — it is the qualification

Notice the apparent irony: Paul addresses "ye which are spiritual." In Galatians, the "spiritual" are those who walk by the Spirit, and the Spirit's fruit is explicitly "longsuffering, gentleness (*praütēs*)... meekness" (5:22–23). So the very trait that qualifies a person to correct is the trait with which the correction must be carried out. Meekness is not timidity; it is strength under restraint — the disposition of one who *could* crush but chooses to heal. The corrector has real standing, and that is precisely why meekness is indispensable: power ungoverned by gentleness becomes violence in the name of righteousness.

## Self-suspicion levels the ground

"Considering thyself, lest thou *also* be tempted" — that little *also* (kai) places the corrector on the same ground as the fallen. The verb *skopōn* means to keep watching, fixing one's gaze. The command is to turn the gaze back on yourself at precisely the moment it most wants to fixate on another's failure. The context makes the danger plain: Paul has just forbidden "vainglory, provoking one another, envying one another" (5:26), and he continues, "if a man think himself to be something, when he is nothing, he deceiveth himself" (6:3). The temptation that accompanies correction is not only the risk of falling into the same sin — it is the subtler temptation to *enjoy* another's fall as a contrast that flatters us, to correct in a way that displays our discernment rather than heals the wounded.

## Why the three must be kept together

They are mutually guarding; remove one and the act corrupts:

- **Restoration without meekness** becomes domination — setting a bone with a hammer.
- **Restoration without self-suspicion** becomes presumption — correcting from a height one has no right to occupy.
- **Meekness without restoration** becomes cowardly silence — never touching the wound, leaving the brother broken.
- **Self-suspicion without restoration** becomes paralysis — "who am I to speak?" — which abandons the fallen to his fault.

Paul does not allow that last escape. The command stands: *restore*. Self-suspicion qualifies the act; it does not cancel it.

## The deeper logic

The three together reproduce in the corrector the very grace he is administering. He was himself restored — gently, by the Spirit — and now he extends the same treatment. This is the economy of the body: when a member is wounded, the body mends the member; it does not amputate for display. And it is the pattern of Christ himself, meek and lowly, who restored Peter after the denial and then charged him, "when thou art converted, strengthen thy brethren" (Luke 22:32) — the restored become restorers, forever correcting as the formerly fallen, in the spirit of the One who binds up the brokenhearted.
