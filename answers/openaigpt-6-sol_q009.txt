Romans 12:20–21 defines victory over evil as **refusing to let another person’s wrongdoing dictate your own conduct**. Instead of retaliating, you meet a real need—food for hunger, drink for thirst—and answer harm with good.

This is not weakness: feeding an enemy takes restraint and courage, and it does not require calling the wrong acceptable. Nor is it a technique for secretly hurting or controlling the enemy. The “coals of fire” may suggest the painful recognition that kindness can awaken; in the surrounding passage, judgment is left to God rather than made the giver’s aim.

The command is genuinely strong because it seeks to stop evil from reproducing itself in you. You may not control whether your enemy changes, but you can refuse to become like the harm you oppose.
