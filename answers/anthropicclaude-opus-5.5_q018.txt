## Why They're Set Against Each Other

The two verses are structured as a deliberate exchange, continuing the "put off / put on" pattern Paul established in 4:22–24. Verse 31 lists what is removed; verse 32 lists what replaces it. The replacements answer the vices point for point:

| Put away (v. 31) | Put on (v. 32) |
|---|---|
| **malice** (*kakia*, ill-will) | **kind** (*chrēstoi*, useful, good toward others) |
| **bitterness** (*pikria*) | **tenderhearted** (*eusplanchnoi*, literally "good-bowelled," inwardly soft) |
| wrath, anger, clamour, evil speaking | **forgiving** (*charizomenoi*) |

Bitterness and forgiveness sit at opposite ends because they are two ways of handling the same thing: **a wrong that has been done to you.** Bitterness keeps the account open, revisiting the debt and letting it compound. Forgiveness closes the account. The Greek *charizomai* comes from *charis* ("grace") and means to give freely or cancel a debt (compare Luke 7:42, where the creditor "frankly forgave" both debtors). So the opposition isn't arbitrary. Bitterness is precisely what an unforgiven debt turns into when it is kept.

## Is Bitterness a Feeling, Habit, or Posture?

The text suggests something more precise than "a feeling."

**1. It is distinguished from raw emotion.** Only five verses earlier Paul writes, "Be ye angry, and sin not: let not the sun go down upon your wrath" (4:26). The initial surge of anger is not itself condemned. What is condemned is anger that outlasts the day, anger that has been *kept*. Bitterness is anger with a long shelf life.

**2. The Greek word carried that meaning.** Aristotle, describing ill-tempered people, says the *pikroi* ("bitter ones") are "hard to appease and retain their anger long" because they hold it in rather than resolving it (*Nicomachean Ethics* 4.5). In ordinary Greek usage, bitterness was a settled temperament: resentment stored rather than discharged.

**3. The list reads as a progression from root to fruit.** Many commentators notice a movement outward:
- *bitterness*: the inward, settled resentment
- *wrath* (*thumos*): the flare-up, the boiling over
- *anger* (*orgē*): the sustained, directed hostility
- *clamour* (*kraugē*): shouting, raised voices
- *evil speaking* (*blasphēmia*): slander, speech that damages reputation
- "*with all malice*": the underlying ill-will that feeds the whole chain

Bitterness stands first because it is the soil the others grow in.

**4. It is treated as a posture one is responsible for.** The command is addressed to the will: "let it be put away." Paul does not command people to stop *feeling* things. He commands them to stop harboring, nursing, and acting out of something. The verb (*arthētō*, aorist passive) suggests a decisive removal. The passive voice may also hint that this removal is something one consents to rather than accomplishes alone, fitting the surrounding call not to "grieve the holy Spirit of God" (4:30).

So the best answer is that bitterness begins as a response, becomes a habit through rehearsal, and ends as a chosen posture toward the offender. The text addresses it at the posture level, where responsibility lies.

## Is It Contagious?

The context strongly suggests yes, and other Scripture makes it explicit.

**Within Ephesians:** The whole section is about the *body*. Just before this, Paul says "we are members one of another" (4:25), and earlier he describes the body growing as "every joint supplieth" (4:16). Every item in verse 31 affects other people: clamour and slander are social acts. Paul also warns, "Neither give place to the devil" (4:27). The word *topos* means a foothold or territory. Unresolved anger gives the adversary ground *within the community*.

**Hebrews 12:15** is the clearest statement: "lest any root of bitterness springing up trouble you, and thereby many be defiled." One person's bitterness defiles *many*. This echoes **Deuteronomy 29:18**, where a single person turning from God is "a root that beareth gall and wormwood" spreading through the tribe.

**Acts 8:23** describes Simon Magus as "in the gall of bitterness," as though bitterness were a medium one can be immersed in.

Bitterness spreads because it recruits. A resentful person rarely stays silent; the grievance gets retold ("evil speaking"), and others take sides and inherit a resentment over a wrong that was never done to them. That is why Paul treats it as a threat to the unity he spent chapter 4 building (4:3: "endeavouring to keep the unity of the Spirit").

## The Ground of the Command

The final clause, "even as God for Christ's sake hath forgiven you" (Greek: *en Christō*, "in Christ"), explains why forgiveness is the true antidote rather than mere suppression.

Bitterness quietly assumes a position: *I am the one owed.* Forgiveness is possible only when a person also remembers *I am the one who was forgiven.* This is the logic of the parable in Matthew 18:21–35, where the servant forgiven ten thousand talents chokes his fellow servant over a hundred pence. His unforgiveness exposes that he never grasped his own release.

So Paul doesn't simply say "stop being bitter." He relocates the believer's identity: from creditor to forgiven debtor. The verb tense in verse 32 also shifts: "be ye" (*ginesthe*, present) and "forgiving" (present participle) denote ongoing action. The removal of bitterness may be decisive, but the practice of forgiveness is continuous, as it must be in a community where people keep wronging each other.

## Summary

- **Why opposed:** Both are responses to being wronged. Bitterness preserves the debt; forgiveness cancels it.
- **What bitterness is:** Not the initial emotion (anger is permitted, 4:26) but anger retained, rehearsed, and adopted as a stance, the root from which wrath, clamour, and slander grow.
- **Contagious?** Yes. It spreads through speech and loyalty, gives the devil territory in the community, and "defiles many" (Heb. 12:15).
- **The cure:** Not willpower alone but remembering one's own forgiveness in Christ, which makes the believer a channel of the grace received rather than a collector of debts.
