

Repentance in Isaiah 55:6–7 involves both **“way”** and **“thoughts”** because sin is not only something a person *does*; it is also something a person *thinks, imagines, intends, and justifies*.

The verse says:

> “Let the wicked forsake his **way**, and the unrighteous man his **thoughts**…”

This pairing is deeply significant.

## 1. “His way” refers to outward conduct

A person’s “way” is their course of life: their habits, actions, relationships, practices, and visible pattern of behavior. In Scripture, a “way” often means more than one isolated act; it describes the direction in which someone is walking.

So the wicked person is not merely told to stop doing one bad thing. He is told to abandon his **way**—his chosen path, his manner of living, his settled course of action.

This means repentance is not only a private feeling. It involves a real turning away from sinful behavior. If someone claims to repent but continues deliberately in the same outward course of life, that claim is hollow. True repentance affects how one lives.

## 2. “His thoughts” refers to inward imagination and intention

But the verse does not stop with outward conduct. It also says the unrighteous man must forsake his **thoughts**.

The word translated “thoughts” can mean plans, devices, imaginations, purposes, or inner counsels. It points to the interior life: the mind’s reasoning, the secret desires, the private justifications, the fantasies, the intentions that may never become visible to others.

This is crucial because a person may abandon an outward sin without truly repenting inwardly. Someone may stop doing something because of fear, shame, social pressure, or self-interest, while still loving the sin in their heart. Their “way” may change, but their “thoughts” remain unchanged.

God therefore requires repentance at the root, not merely at the surface. The sinful life begins within: in imagination, desire, pride, self-will, and false reasoning. So repentance must reach those inward places.

## 3. Outward conduct and inward imagination are connected

The “way” and the “thoughts” are not separated in real life. Outward behavior usually grows out of inward thought. A person lives wrongly because they have been thinking wrongly. The mind gives direction to the feet.

So this verse exposes the wholeness of repentance. It involves:

- abandoning sinful actions,
- abandoning sinful intentions,
- abandoning sinful patterns,
- abandoning sinful imaginations,
- abandoning the inner reasoning that excuses sin.

Repentance is therefore not merely behavioral reform. It is a turning of the whole person. The hands must turn from evil, and the mind must turn from evil. The public life must change, and the private life must change.

In that sense, the verse leaves no hiding place. God is not satisfied with outward respectability if the inward imagination remains corrupt. Nor is He satisfied with inward remorse if the outward way remains unchanged.

## 4. This shows why mercy is so necessary

The demand is high: forsake your way and your thoughts. That is why the verse immediately turns to mercy.

It says:

> “…and let him return unto the Lord, and he will have mercy upon him; and to our God, for he will abundantly pardon.”

The call to repentance is severe, but the promise is tender. The sinner is not summoned to earn mercy by perfect self-reform. Rather, he is summoned to **return unto the Lord**. Repentance is not merely leaving something behind; it is turning to God.

The hope of mercy lies not in the depth of the sinner’s feelings or the strength of his moral effort, but in the character of God. The text says:

> “He will have mercy upon him.”

And then it intensifies:

> “He will abundantly pardon.”

The Hebrew idea behind “abundantly pardon” is something like multiplying forgiveness. God does not merely pardon reluctantly or sparingly. He forgives richly, freely, and completely.

So the implication is this: the sinner’s condition is serious because it includes both outward life and inward thought, but God’s mercy is greater. The person may be outwardly crooked and inwardly corrupt, yet there is still hope—if he returns to the Lord.

## 5. The invitation is urgent

The opening verse adds urgency:

> “Seek ye the Lord while he may be found, call ye upon him while he is near.”

This means the offer of mercy is real, but it should not be presumed upon. God may be found now. He is near now. The call is present. The invitation stands. But the sinner should not delay, because no one controls the future.

Repentance, then, is not merely a vague wish to be better someday. It is a present turning to God while He graciously reveals Himself.

## Summary

Repentance here involves both “way” and “thoughts” because true turning to God must include the whole person. The “way” represents outward conduct; the “thoughts” represent inward imagination, intention, and desire. God requires not only external reformation but internal transformation.

Yet this does not lead to despair. The very same passage announces mercy. The wicked and unrighteous are invited to return, because God is merciful and abundantly pardoning. The seriousness of sin—both outward and inward—is met by the greatness of divine forgiveness.
