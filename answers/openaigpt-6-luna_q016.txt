His anger is the **surface**; the deeper problem is that he cannot welcome mercy when it is given to someone he judges undeserving. Beneath that is a transactional view of his relationship with his father: *I have served and obeyed, so I should be rewarded.* He treats obedience as a claim on the father, not as life with him.

That is why he speaks of “this thy son,” rather than “my brother.” The father gently restores the relationship—“this thy brother”—and reminds him, “all that I have is thine.” The elder has not lost his place in the family; he has become unable to rejoice in the father’s generosity.

So wounded fairness and envy may be part of his anger, and pride in obedience helps fuel it. But the deepest issue is **resentment of mercy**: he measures the younger son by what he has done, while the father celebrates that the lost one has returned. The father’s invitation leaves the elder’s response open.
