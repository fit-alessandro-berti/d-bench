Both the grammar of the verse and its immediate context point to the second option: the danger lies in the craving—or more precisely, in the *orientation* of the act—not in visibility itself. But the distinction deserves careful work, because visibility is the near occasion of the vice, which is why Jesus prescribes concrete countermeasures.

**A grammatical clue**

The KJV's "alms" reflects a manuscript variant; most early witnesses read *dikaiosynēn*—"your righteousness"—which suits the structure of the chapter: verses 2–4 (almsgiving), 5–15 (prayer), and 16–18 (fasting) unpack one warning covering the whole pious life.

The decisive phrase is the purpose clause: *pros to theathēnai autois*—"in order to be beheld by them." Jesus does not forbid doing righteousness *coram hominibus*, before men—an unavoidable condition of embodied life, and one he explicitly commands elsewhere. He forbids constructing the act *for the sake of* its visibility. The verb (*theaomai*) even carries faint theatrical overtones: the audience *gazes* at a spectacle. Fittingly, the repeated vice-name in vv. 2, 5, 16 is *hypokritēs*—the Greek stage term for an actor, someone whose entire existence depends on spectators. A hypocrite, strictly speaking, cannot function in secret; strip away the audience and the motivation collapses. This tells us the condemned thing is essentially audience-dependent.

**The contextual proof**

The strongest internal evidence sits one column over. Matthew 5:16, moments earlier: *"Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father which is in heaven."* Being observed is commanded; engineering one's visibility is forbidden. What separates them is the trajectory of the seeing. In 5:16, human eyes terminate in God's glory—they "glorify your Father." In 6:1, the doer makes his own appearance the terminus: he does the act precisely so that the seeing happens. Same deed, different final court of appeal.

**Two treasuries**

Notice the economics. "They have their reward" (v. 2) uses commercial language—the verb *apodidōmi*, to pay out, settle an invoice. Applause functions as a wage. If human recognition is what you're collecting, you collect it; the transaction is consummated on delivery. Hence "no reward of your Father"—not divine stinginess, but the fact that nothing of the sort the Father rewards remains. An act performed to acquire regard was spent acquiring it. Jesus' hearers would recognize the social script instantly: Greco-Roman cities ran on public benefaction, donors competing for inscriptions, crowns, proclamations. "They have their reward in full" was practically civic accounting.

**Why the craving corrodes from within**

Three losses follow when visibility becomes the end:

1. **Misallocated goodness.** Actions get selected for photogenic quality rather than real need. Visible gifts flourish; invisible obediences—secret fidelity, unnoticed repair—atrophy. The appetite redirects the moral budget.
2. **Instrumentalized neighbors.** The recipient becomes backdrop for the giver's portrait. Charity that exists to display me stops existing *for* the one served.
3. **Hollowing of the self** (Jesus' own Matthean gloss, 23:27: whitened tombs, beautiful outside, death within). One acts in order to appear; eventually one appears instead of being. Identity migrates into the mirror.

**Two honest caveats**

First, the motto-driven route of condemning all visibility leads somewhere Jesus refuses to go—he healed and taught notoriously in public. The "closet" instructions (giving secretly, praying shut away, anointing the face when fasting) are not because being seen is intrinsically evil but because the heart needs training regimes. They work toward applause the way fasting works toward food: discipline of a legitimate appetite turned master. Call it training in invisibility.

Second, complete motive purity is beyond anyone. No gift arrives unmixed. The present imperative—*"keep taking heed"* (*prosechetē*)—prescribes vigilance, not achieved innocence. And there is a usable diagnostic: when nobody notices your sacrifice, where does the disappointment land? Its location reveals whose treasury you were drawing on.

So: not the eye that sees you, but the audience you appoint as paymaster. The craving is dangerous precisely because it leaves the outward act intact while transferring its price to the wrong ledger—converting worship into performance and saints into actors.
