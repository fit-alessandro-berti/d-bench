# The posture: stooping, not standing over

The verse prescribes a man bending under a burden, not standing over a fault. The grammar already carries this: the command is plural ("you who are spiritual" — the community together), the fallen man is singular, and the word for his condition (προληφθῇ, "overtaken") suggests ambush — caught before he saw it coming, not defiantly entrenched. Verse 2 then names what verse 1 performs: correction is *burden-bearing*.

The corrector is thus physician, fellow patient, and servant in a single gesture — and each of the three elements in the verse guards one of those roles.

## The aim: mending, not verdicts

Καταρτίζετε is a workshop and sickroom word — "mend," as of nets (Matt 4:21), "set," as of bones, "supply what is lacking" (1 Thess 3:10). Its home is not the courtroom. To choose this verb is already to have settled what correction is *for*: not the extraction of satisfaction, not quarantine, not example-making, but the return of the brother to wholeness and usefulness. And the aim disciplines the means. Only reparative instruments serve a reparative end. A blow may extract shame or compliance; it does not mend a net.

## The manner: meekness as credential

Πραΰτης is not softness — in its classical range it is strength gentled, the tamed horse. More decisively, it stands in the catalog Paul has *just* finished: "the fruit of the Spirit is… gentleness, self-control" (5:22–23). So when 6:1 addresses "you who are spiritual," the manner of correction is the credential of spirituality. A correction delivered in another spirit refutes the corrector's claim to be walking in the Spirit in the very act of asserting it.

There is also a pastoral logic. A man "overtaken" is a wounded man, and the wounded bite. He will only open his hand to one that does not also grind him. Meekness is the condition under which honesty becomes affordable to the fallen.

## The guard: watching yourself *while* you work

Σκοπῶν σεαυτόν is a present participle — self-watch simultaneous with the correction, not a prior examination completed once and for all. Hands on him; eyes on yourself. And "lest thou also be tempted" cuts two ways. The corrector may fall into the brother's fault — but more subtly, he may fall into the sins *peculiar to correcting*: superiority, the small pleasure in another's fall, indignation wearing the clothing of zeal, the flattery of being the strong one. Correction is the moment of maximal inequality between two people, and inequality intoxicates. This is why the temptation rarely arrives looking like temptation; it arrives looking like righteousness. Paul names the danger one verse earlier: "let us not become conceited" (5:26).

## Why the three are inseparable

Positively: correction places one sinner above another, and that position is inherently unstable. Love operating under inequality needs all three — an aim fixed on the beloved's good (restoration), a manner suited to his weakness (meekness), and a distrust of the agent precisely when his motives feel most righteous (self-suspicion). Each is the safeguard of the others:

- **Restoration without meekness** is punishment in a therapeutic mask. Right aim, wrong hand — it produces concealment, not confession.
- **Meekness without restoration** is sentimentality that leaves the brother in his fault. "You shall not hate your brother in your heart; you shall surely rebuke him" (Lev 19:17) — silence can be abandonment.
- **Both without self-suspicion** curdles into condescension — the meekness of a man certain he stands. The fallen brother is then not being healed; he is scenery for the corrector's piety (cf. 6:3).
- **Self-suspicion without restoration** is paralysis — "who am I to speak?" as an alibi for leaving him where he fell.

The three together create a deliberate instability that is the whole point: the corrector stands **above** his brother's sin in duty (he must act), **beside** him in condition (fellow-tempted), and **beneath** him in service (taking the weight, v. 2 — the "law of Christ"). Remove any element and the posture collapses into domination, indifference, or pride.

Self-suspicion, notably, is what converts gentleness from condescension into *solidarity*. The brother's fall is not news about him; it is news about the shared human heart. "There go I" is the ground of the corrector's meekness.

## The deeper reason

The three are bound together because the church participates in God's *restorative* work, never His judicial work. "Who are you to pass judgment on another's servant?… Indeed, he will be made to stand, for God is able to make him stand" (Rom 14:4). The corrector assists a standing that God performs. The plural verb and singular man say the same thing ecclesially: the body stoops to raise its own wounded member — one member's fall is a wound in the body, and restoration is the body healing itself.

The pattern is Christ's. He restores the fallen "by the meekness and gentleness of Christ" (2 Cor 10:1), tempted in every respect yet without sin (Heb 4:15). The corrector imitates him at two points — the aim and the manner — and differs at the third; that difference is exactly where self-suspicion is inserted. He is a fellow patient entrusted with a physician's task.

Note, finally, the discrimination Paul himself practices in this letter: he is scathing toward those *unsettling* the Galatians (3:1; 5:12) and gentle toward the one *trapped* (6:1). Severity is measured to hardness; the aim remains, always, mending. He stoops; he does not preside.
