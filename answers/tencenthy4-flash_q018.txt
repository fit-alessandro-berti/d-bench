Because Ephesians 4:31–32 is not mainly giving private advice about “managing emotions.” It is describing what the new, Christ-formed community must look like over against the old, fractured humanity. Bitterness and forgiveness stand directly opposite because one keeps the wound alive as a weapon; the other releases the wound into God’s hands and treats the offender as a neighbor still worth kindness.

A close reading helps.

**“Let all … be put away”** gathers a whole cluster: *bitterness, wrath, anger, clamour, evil speaking, malice*. The movement is from inner disposition to outward expression. Bitterness is not isolated; it feeds rage, sharp speech, shouting, slander, and “all malice.” Then **“be ye kind … tenderhearted, forgiving”** gives the positive replacement. The repetition of **“one to another”** makes the point communal: these are not merely vertical virtues before God, but the daily atmosphere of the body.

So to your options: it is not fully any one of them alone.

- **It is a feeling** — but not merely a feeling. Bitterness includes the affective residue of hurt: resentment, sourness, the aftertaste of being wronged. Ephesians allows anger under limits: “Be angry, and do not sin” (4:26). The initial surge of pain or anger is not automatically condemned. What is condemned is the anger that has been allowed to settle and soured into *pikria*, bitterness.

- **It is a habit** — because it is “put away,” not merely “stop feeling for a moment.” Bitterness becomes habitual when the mind keeps rehearsing the offense, keeping accounts, returning to the injury until resentment becomes familiar. “With all malice” suggests a settled orientation, not just a passing flash.

- **It is a chosen posture** — not usually chosen at the moment of being wounded, but chosen when we consent to nurse the wound, refuse release, and make the memory serve accusation. That is why forgiveness can be commanded. Scripture is not saying, “Choose to feel no pain.” It is saying, “Do not enthrone the pain as your judge and your advocate.” Forgiveness is not excuses, denial, or pretending evil is good; it is the disciplined act of giving up the right to keep the debt alive.

- **It is contagious** — in context, yes. “Clamour” and “evil speaking” are how private bitterness becomes public atmosphere. A resentful spirit justifies itself in speech; others join in; the group begins to share the same suspicion, the same sarcasm, the same readiness to think the worst. Bitterness spreads because it is narrated, repeated, and tolerated. It is not a disease in the mechanical sense, but it is profoundly social. One person’s unforgiveness can become a church’s climate.

That is why **“even as God for Christ’s sake hath forgiven you”** is placed right there. The imperative to forgive is grounded in the indicative that God has forgiven us in Christ. Many translations render the phrase “as God in Christ forgave you”; the KJV’s “for Christ’s sake” preserves the same essential point: Christian forgiveness is not generic niceness. It is Christ-shaped. We forgive because the God who judged sin truly has, in Christ, opened the way of mercy for us. Unforgiveness therefore contradicts the very gospel by which we stand.

So the text treats bitterness as a **root** that is felt, rehearsed, embraced, and spread. Forgiveness is set against it because forgiveness is the Spirit-formed way of preventing a wound from becoming a root of malice. Put simply: bitterness keeps the account open; forgiveness closes the account because Christ has closed it for us.
