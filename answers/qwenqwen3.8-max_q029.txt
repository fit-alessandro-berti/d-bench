Galatians 6:1 requires a posture of **humble, restorative love** — not detached judgment, not angry superiority, but the kind of concern a physician has for a wounded person.

Paul says that “ye which are spiritual” are to restore the one overtaken in a fault. The spiritual person is not someone above temptation, but someone led by the Spirit and therefore able to correct without cruelty. The goal is not to expose, shame, or dominate the offender, but to **restore** him. The Greek word for “restore” (*katartizete*) can mean to mend, repair, or set right — as one might mend a net or set a dislocated bone. Correction, then, is meant to heal, not merely punish.

This restoration must be done “in the spirit of meekness.” Meekness here is not weakness. It is strength under restraint — gentleness shaped by grace. A meek corrector does not act from irritation, contempt, or self-confidence, but from sorrow over sin and hope for the sinner’s recovery. Meekness keeps correction from becoming vengeance.

Then Paul adds, “considering thyself, lest thou also be tempted.” This self-suspicion is essential. The one who corrects must remember that he is not beyond the same sin, or beyond other sins just as serious. The awareness of one’s own vulnerability prevents correction from turning into pride. The corrector stands beside the fallen person as a fellow sinner saved by grace, not as a superior judge.

So these three things are kept together because each protects the others:

- **Restoration** gives correction its proper aim: healing, not humiliation.  
- **Meekness** gives correction its proper manner: gentleness, not harshness.  
- **Self-suspicion** gives correction its proper spirit: humility, not self-righteousness.

Without restoration, correction becomes punitive. Without meekness, it becomes brutal. Without self-suspicion, it becomes proud. Paul binds them together because biblical correction is never merely discipline; it is a work of grace aimed at reconciling the person to God and to the community.
