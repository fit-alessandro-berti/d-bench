**The verse treats love of the visible brother as the necessary, observable test of claimed love for the unseen God because genuine love is not an abstract inner state or verbal profession that can float free of action toward those actually present; the “seen vs. unseen” contrast exposes the self-deception of treating piety as a private spiritual transaction that excuses hatred or indifference toward people.**

John’s logic is straightforward and empirical rather than merely sentimental. You *can* see, hear, and interact with your brother; the opportunity (and the cost) of love is immediate and concrete. God, by contrast, is not seen. If someone fails at the nearer, more accessible object of love, the claim to love the distant, invisible One is empty—it has no corresponding reality. The person is “a liar” because the profession does not match the evidence of how they actually treat those made in God’s image or belonging to God’s family. Love that never reaches the visible neighbor is not love of God at all; it is a self-serving idea of God.

This exposes a particular, durable form of self-deception: the split between “spiritual” devotion and ordinary ethical life. People readily imagine they love God through feelings, rituals, correct beliefs, or private prayer while remaining cold, contemptuous, or actively hostile toward actual brothers and sisters. The verse treats that compartmentalization as impossible. Because God is love (1 John 4:8, 16) and because the commandment is to love one another (4:21), hatred of the visible brother falsifies the claim. It is the same pattern John attacks elsewhere: claiming to walk in the light while hating (2:9–11), or claiming to know God while disobeying His commands (2:4). The unseen God is not a refuge from the demands of the seen neighbor; the latter is how the former is verified.

In short, the test works because love is not primarily a feeling directed at an idea. If it does not show up where it can be seen and tested, the profession is self-deception—often the comfortable kind that lets someone feel righteous without the inconvenience of actually loving people.
