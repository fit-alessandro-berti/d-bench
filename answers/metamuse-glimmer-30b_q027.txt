Luke 18:9 sets the frame before the story is told: He spake this parable unto certain which trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and despised others. The parable is not about bad doctrine about fasting or tithes. Fasting twice a week and tithing everything are not condemned; they are in fact good works. The condemnation is of the heart that does them.

The Pharisee is condemned for self-trust, i.e. pride. Comparison, contempt and the use of virtue as self-display are the outward forms that pride takes here.

* Comparison: "God, I thank thee, that I am not as other men are..."
* Contempt: "...extortioners, unjust, adulterers, or even as this publican."
* Self-display: "I fast twice in the week, I give tithes of all that I possess."

The prayer is "with himself". *proseauton*. It is a monologue addressed to God but really spoken to himself. He thanks God not for mercy received but for superiority achieved. His righteousness is a possession to be audited, not a gift to be received. He trusts in himself that he is righteous and therefore needs no mercy, only acknowledgment.

That is why the story is about self-righteousness, not ritual error. The Pharisee has the right practices with the wrong telos: they become a resume before God.

The publican is treated as spiritually truer because his whole body tells the truth about the human condition before God. Standing afar off, not lifting his eyes to heaven, smiting his breast are the physical signs of shame, humility and contrition. He does not bring merits, he brings need.

"God be merciful to me a sinner" is not a statement of moral improvement, it is a confession of dependence. *eleēson me ton hamartōlon*. He does not say I am not as bad as I once was, he says I am a sinner now and I need mercy.

In Luke's theology justification is God's declarative act, not a human achievement. The Pharisee seeks to justify himself by comparison; the publican receives justification by acknowledging he cannot justify himself. Humility is not a virtue that earns mercy, it is the posture that can receive it. The one who knows he is a sinner is the one who can be met by grace; the one who thinks he is already righteous has no room for God.
