The passage itself names the moral failure plainly in verse 9: the Pharisee is the portrait of someone who **“trusted in himself that he was righteous, and despised others.”** Jesus’ diagnosis therefore is not primarily about theological error, but about the posture of the heart. Within the list you offer, the text condemns a cluster of attitudes that reinforce one another. None stands entirely alone; they form a single spiritual pathology.

**1. What condemns the Pharisee**
- **Pride / self-trust:** The parable opens by saying he trusted in himself. Righteousness is treated as a personal achievement rather than a gift received. The thanksgiving is directed inward more than upward: he “prayed with himself,” congratulating God on the Pharisee’s own merits.
- **Comparison:** His prayer is built on measuring himself against “other men.” Righteousness becomes relative and competitive rather than absolute before God.
- **Contempt:** The comparison is not neutral; it produces contempt for “this publican.” The Greek behind “despised” implies looking down on others, treating them as religiously or morally inferior.
- **Virtue as self-display:** Twice-weekly fasting and tithing of all he possesses (likely exaggerated in oral tradition, but symbolically clear) are recited as a résumé. The religious acts become evidence entered on his own behalf rather than worship offered in humility.
- **Not false doctrine in this context:** The Pharisee is not condemned here for holding incorrect theology. Pharisaic piety in the Gospels is often orthodox in its framework; the danger is a righteousness performed to secure social and spiritual status, and a heart that forgets its need for mercy.

In short, the condemnation falls on the **self-referential, comparative, contemptuous pride** that turns devotion into a claim on God’s favor. Jesus treats these as facets of the same idolatry: trusting the self rather than depending on God.

**2. Why the tax collector’s posture is treated as spiritually truer**
- **Distance and lowered eyes:** He “stood afar off” and would not lift his eyes to heaven. In Jewish prayer culture this signals unworthiness and reverence, not theatrical humility. He knows he cannot approach God on the strength of his own record.
- **The breast-smote:** Striking the breast was a conventional gesture of grief and repentance. It externalizes inward conviction: “I have fallen short.”
- **A single petition:** “God be merciful to me, a sinner.” He does not compare, excuse, or list merits. He names his need and asks for mercy. The movement is from self-exaltation to self-emptying, which in Luke’s theology is the posture that receives grace.
- **Justified:** Jesus says this man “went down to his house justified rather than the other.” Justification here follows the logic of Luke’s Gospel: God declares right those who acknowledge their need and rely on mercy, not those who attempt to establish their own standing. The tax collector’s posture is “truer” because it aligns with the reality of the human condition before God—debt, dependence, and the need for compassion.

The contrast is therefore not between two kinds of works or two theologies, but between two orientations: a heart that presents itself as already sufficient, and a heart that receives. In Luke’s framework, the one who prays for mercy is the one who actually leaves in right relationship with God; the one who prays to be seen as righteous leaves with nothing but his own evaluation.
