Jeremiah 17:9–10: pessimism, realism, and how to speak of motives

1. Reading the passage in its setting

The two verses are usually quoted alone, which makes them sound like a free-standing verdict on humanity. In Jeremiah 17 they are the hinge of an argument about trust. Verses 5–8 set out two ways of living: "Cursed be the man that trusteth in man, and maketh flesh his arm" (a shrub in the desert), against "Blessed is the man that trusteth in the LORD" (a tree planted by water that "shall not cease from yielding fruit"). Verse 9 then explains why trusting in "flesh" is so dangerous: the organ that does the trusting is itself unreliable. Verse 10 gives the alternative: there is a knower who is reliable. Verse 11 adds the partridge sitting on eggs it did not lay, an image of gains that will not stay. The passage, in other words, is about where confidence can safely be placed, not an abstract catalogue of human vices.

Two translation points matter for the question.

First, "heart" (lev) in Hebrew is not the seat of feeling as opposed to reason. It is the whole inner person: thinking, deciding, wanting, remembering. So the verse is not saying "your emotions mislead you"; it is saying that the instrument you would use to examine yourself is the very thing that is bent.

Second, the KJV's "desperately wicked" is stronger and more moralising than the Hebrew. The word is aqob for "deceitful" (crooked, uneven, tracking-ground that will not run straight, from the same root that names Jacob the heel-grabber), and anush for "desperately wicked". Anush elsewhere in Jeremiah means "incurable" of a wound or sickness (15:18; 30:12; and 17:16 in this very chapter), and that is how most modern translations take it: "desperately sick" (ESV), "beyond cure" (NIV). The verse is a diagnosis, not a curse: the heart is crooked and its condition is not one it can fix from the inside. "Who can know it?" is a real question, and verse 10 answers it. "I the LORD search the heart, I try the reins" (the kidneys, the innermost seat of feeling and conscience). And the criterion of that examination is stated: "according to his ways, and according to the fruit of his doings."

2. Pessimistic about human nature, or realistic about self-deception?

The honest answer is that the dichotomy is slightly mis-cut, because the verse is making a claim about human self-knowledge more than about human worth. Still, one can say where the weight falls.

It is genuinely dark in one respect: it denies that a person has trustworthy access to his own inner condition, and it denies that this can be repaired by more sincerity or more effort. That is a strong claim, and it cuts against the intuition most people live by, which is that whatever else is uncertain, I at least know what I meant. Jeremiah's hearers were not cynics; they were sincere people who believed the temple guaranteed their safety ("The temple of the LORD, the temple of the LORD," 7:4) and that their worship was acceptable. Their sincerity was not evidence of anything. That is the sting.

But three features keep it from being pessimism in the ordinary sense.

(a) It is not fatalism. The judgment in verse 10 is "according to his ways" and "the fruit of his doings." Ways and fruit are real, observable, and they differ from person to person. The verse expects that a life can be assessed and that assessments will come out differently. A truly pessimistic anthropology would say it does not matter what anyone does; this says the opposite.

(b) The incurability is incurability from within. The same prophet who wrote this also wrote "I will give them an heart to know me" (24:7), "I will put my law in their inward parts, and write it in their hearts" (31:33), and "I will give them one heart" (32:39). Jeremiah's realism about the heart is exactly what makes those promises necessary. The passage does not say the heart cannot be healed; it says the heart cannot heal or even accurately examine itself. In the immediate context, the tree by the water is a picture of a human life that does bear fruit, so the chapter plainly believes in a good human outcome under the right conditions.

(c) The rhetorical question has an answer. "Who can know it?" is not shrugged at; it is met with "I the LORD search." The passage places the missing knowledge somewhere rather than nowhere. Whether one calls this pessimistic depends almost entirely on whether one thinks that knower exists. For Jeremiah, the verse is finally consoling: there is someone who sees you as you are, and who judges by what you actually do rather than by the story you tell about it.

So: realistic about self-deception, and the realism is uncomfortable enough that people mistake it for pessimism. What it is pessimistic about is a specific thing, the reliability of the inner witness, and it is pessimistic about that in a way that ordinary experience and a good deal of modern psychology confirm. People routinely report reasons for their behaviour that demonstrably were not the causes of it; they explain away their own failures and moralise others'; they find the flattering account of their conduct more available than the accurate one, and find it faster than they could have reasoned to it. The Hebrew word "crooked" is apt: the path from motive to self-report does not run straight, and it bends in a predictable direction, toward self-justification. Proverbs states the same thing without any flourish: "All the ways of a man are clean in his own eyes: but the LORD weigheth the spirits" (16:2; cf. 21:2). This is not a doctrine that humans are as bad as they could be. It is a doctrine that humans are not in a position to certify their own goodness.

One further caution: the verse is often cited as a proof-text for total depravity, and the KJV wording invites that. Its own claim is narrower and, for practical purposes, more useful. It is about opacity and crookedness, not about the absence of anything good.

3. How to talk about motives if the heart is an unreliable witness

The temptation is to conclude that motive-talk should stop: if no one can know the heart, then let no one speak of it. That is not what the passage does. It goes on immediately to speak of hearts, ways, and fruit. What changes is the mode of speech. Verse 10 supplies the model: the heart is searched (not simply trusted), tested (not simply reported on), and judged by ways and fruit (not by declared intention). Several practices follow.

Speak of your own motives as hypotheses, not verdicts. "I think I did that because..." is honest; "I did that because..." claims an access the verse denies you. This is not false modesty; it is accuracy about the evidence. Paul, who knew this verse, put it exactly: "I know nothing by myself; yet am I not hereby justified: but he that judgeth me is the Lord" (1 Cor 4:4). A clear conscience is not the same as a clean one. The psalmist's "who can understand his errors? cleanse thou me from secret faults" (Ps 19:12) is the prayer of someone who has taken Jeremiah's point.

Move the weight of the conversation from intention to ways and fruit. When you want to know what you were really after, the surest evidence is not what you felt at the time but what you did, what you kept doing, and what it produced. Patterns over time are harder to fake than single reports. This is why the surrounding chapter uses trees and fruit: fruit is slow, seasonal, and visible to others. If a person says they acted out of love and the fruit is consistently control, the fruit is the better witness, and the honest thing is to let it testify. Jesus draws the same conclusion in the same image: "by their fruits ye shall know them" (Matt 7:16–20).

Assume mixed motives, in yourself first. The heart is crooked, not merely wrong, and one of its habits is to present a single clean reason where there were several tangled ones. The truthful account of almost any act is "partly this, and partly that, and partly something I would rather not name." Speech about motives that admits mixture is more likely to be true than speech that claims purity, and it also tends to be less defensive, because it has already conceded the point a critic would make.

Be most suspicious of your motives where your interest is served. The bend in the heart is not random. Where the flattering interpretation and the self-serving outcome coincide, treat your confidence as a warning rather than a comfort. Jeremiah's contemporaries were confident precisely where confidence cost them nothing and required nothing of them.

Borrow other people's eyes. If the heart cannot see itself straight, the natural remedy is a witness who stands outside it. Others frequently see the shape of our conduct more clearly than we do; that is the whole office of a prophet, and it is also the ordinary office of a truthful friend, a spouse, a confessor, a community that is permitted to say "this is what it looks like from here." Talking about motives, then, should often take the form of asking rather than announcing: "What did that look like to you?" is a better use of the conversation than "Let me tell you what I meant." Someone who will not allow any external testimony about his heart has decided to trust the one witness the verse says is unreliable.

Extend the same humility outward, which means slowness to assign motives to others. You have less access to another person's heart than to your own, and the verse says your access to your own is poor. So you can describe what someone did and what it did to you; you can say what you fear it meant; you should be reluctant to declare what it meant. This cuts in both directions. Other people are probably not as noble as their account of themselves, and they are probably not as villainous as your account of them. Paul again: "judge nothing before the time" (1 Cor 4:5). The person who is quick to see the crookedness in everyone else's heart while treating his own reading of them as straight has misapplied the verse.

Use confession rather than defence as the default register. When motives must be spoken of, the passage suggests the shape: not "here is why I was right," but "here is what I see, here is what I suspect I do not see, search me." The prayer at the end of Psalm 139 ("Search me, O God, and know my heart: try me, and know my thoughts") deliberately picks up Jeremiah's two verbs, search and try, and hands them back to God. That is what it looks like to accept that you are not the final authority on yourself without falling into the paralysis of saying nothing.

Let time do some of the work. Because fruit is slow, judgments about motives, one's own and others', should stay revisable longer than feels comfortable. A great deal of damage is done by settling the question of what someone meant on the day it happened.

4. Summary

The passage is realistic about self-deception, and sharply so: the inner witness is crooked, it bends toward self-justification, and it cannot straighten itself. It is not pessimistic in the sense of hopeless or fatalistic, because it names a knower outside the heart, it expects lives to be judged by real and differing fruit, and the same prophet promises a heart that is remade. The practical consequence is not silence about motives but a different way of speaking about them: provisionally about one's own, charitably and slowly about others', with more weight on ways and fruit than on declared intention, with other people's eyes admitted as evidence, and with the final verdict left where the verse leaves it. The verse's most useful gift is the loss of a false certainty. Once you stop treating "I know what I meant" as bedrock, you become both harder to fool and easier to correct.
