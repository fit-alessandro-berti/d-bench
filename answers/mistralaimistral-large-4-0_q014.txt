This text does both—it **reveals** the heart diagnostically, but it primarily **trains** the heart formatively. The grammar and rhetorical context make the formative direction primary: Jesus issues a command (“Lay up for yourselves treasures in heaven”), then gives the rationale (“*For* where your treasure is, there will your heart be also”). The “for” (γάρ, *gar*) introduces the consequence that motivates the imperative. In other words, **change where you store your treasure, and your heart will follow**. It is a theology of “outside-in” transformation: we are not first to feel our way into loving heaven, but to practice storing treasure there until our affections are reoriented.

Here is how possession, attention, and love become tied together in the passage:

**1. Possession (Treasure) determines Attention (Heart)**
In the biblical anthropology of Matthew’s Gospel, the “heart” (*kardia*) is not merely the seat of emotion but the center of focus, will, and allegiance. When Jesus says “where your treasure is, there will your heart be also,” he is describing a law of gravity: **what we hold, hold us**. Earthly treasure—property, security, status—captures our vigilance (“where moth and rust corrupt… thieves break through”). We check it, worry about it, plan for it. That preoccupation is the “heart” being *there*. Conversely, heavenly treasure (generosity, investment in people, eternal realities) trains the attention to look upward. The location of our assets becomes the location of our gaze.

**2. Attention shapes Love**
Love, in this context, is not a spontaneous feeling but a settled attachment that grows through sustained attention. The text implies that **love follows focus**. If your attention is tethered to perishable things, your love will atrophy around them; if your attention is trained on imperishable things, your love will be educated toward them. The “heart” is where love resides, and the heart migrates to wherever the treasure is stored. Thus, the passage is a form of spiritual discipline: by redirecting your possessions, you redirect your desires.

**3. The Triadic Bond**
The three elements form a single circuit:
- **Possession** (what you store up) →
- **Attention** (what occupies your “heart”/mind) →
- **Love** (what you ultimately cherish/serve).

Jesus’ preceding warning that “no one can serve two masters” (v. 24) confirms this: you cannot split your treasure between earth and heaven, because your attention and love will inevitably consolidate around one of them. The text is a call to **undivided investment**. Your treasure reveals your true master (diagnostic), but your choice of treasure also constitutes your master (formative).

**The Paradox**
The passage feels counterintuitive because we assume we must love first, then treasure. Jesus reverses the sequence. He knows that human desire is liturgically formed—we become what we repeatedly do, what we invest in, what we handle. Therefore, to love heaven, you must first **act** as if heaven is your treasure. The heart is not the starting point; it is the destination that follows the treasure.

In short: **Treasure reveals the heart, but more importantly, treasure trains the heart.** Possession captures attention; attention cultivates love. Store up what is eternal, and your heart—the very center of who you are—will be trained to dwell there.
