In Luke 6:37, Jesus is asking not only whether we can recognize a wrong, but what we want for the person who did it. The verse follows the call to “be ye therefore merciful, as your Father also is merciful” (6:36). Mercy is not indifference: to forgive a betrayal is already to acknowledge that someone owed you fidelity. If no wrong had been done, there would be nothing to forgive.

**Discernment** asks, “What happened, whom did it hurt, and what should be done now?” It may require a firm judgment. If a friend betrays a confidence, you can name the betrayal, ask them to repair the harm, and decide not to trust them with another secret. Forgiveness does not require pretending that trust has been restored.

**The appetite to condemn** appears when a true judgment about the betrayal becomes a verdict on the whole person: “Now I know all I need to know about you.” The distinction lies less in how strongly you speak than in what would satisfy you. Would you welcome an honest apology and a change of conduct, even if you still needed distance? Or would some part of you miss the right to hold the failure over them?

A few verses later, Jesus tells the person who sees a speck in a brother’s eye to remove the beam from their own eye *first*, so that they can see clearly enough to help (6:41–42). The speck is real; the point is to see it without the distortion of self-righteousness. Moral judgment matters precisely because it can serve the person harmed and call the wrongdoer to account. What Jesus challenges is the pleasure of occupying the judge’s seat.
