Ephesians 4:31-32 is not a list of unrelated virtues. It is the end of Paul's argument about the new self that the Ephesians have already put on in 4:22-24. Put off the old man, put on the new man created in righteousness and true holiness. 

The first imperative is a single command: *pasa pikria... apothēthētō* - "let all bitterness be put away." The verb is an aorist imperative, a decisive act, not a gradual fading. Bitterness is then linked in a chain with thumos wrath, orgē anger, kraugē clamour/noise, blasphemia evil speaking, and kakia malice. Paul is tracing a movement from heart to mouth to community.

*Pikria* is bitterness. It is the root. The word is used for bitter herbs and for a bitter heart, and in the LXX it is the bitterness that poisons. In Hebrews 12:15 it is a *rhiza pikrias* a root of bitterness that defiles many. It begins as a feeling of hurt, resentment, injury not processed. Left there it settles into a disposition, a habitual posture toward the person who wounded you, and then it expresses itself. That is why Paul links it immediately to wrath, anger, clamour and evil speaking. Bitterness is not contained inside; it becomes speech that tears down and a tone that fills the house.

It is therefore all three things you name, and it is contagious because it is social. It is a feeling that hardens into a chosen habit. It is a chosen posture, because Paul commands it to be put away, not merely managed. And it is contagious because in a body like the church it moves from one person to another through the very things Paul names: noise, slander, malice. Bitterness keeps the wound open and makes you rehearse the injury.

That is why forgiveness is placed directly opposite it. Bitterness is clinging to a wrong; forgiveness is releasing it. Bitterness hardens the heart; *eisalloi* tenderhearted, is the softening of it. Bitterness is self-protective and self-justifying; *chrēstoi* kind, one to another, is the outward orientation of the new self. The positive command is not simply "feel nice." It is *charizomenoi allēlois* forgiving one another, in the same way, *kathōs kai ho theos en Christō eucharēsen hymin* as God in Christ forgave you.

Forgiveness is the practical antidote to bitterness because bitterness is, at its core, a refusal to accept the logic of divine forgiveness. You have been forgiven for Christ’s sake, not because you were first worthy of it. To keep bitterness is to live as if that forgiveness had limits for you while it applies to others. To forgive is to take off the old man’s poison and put on the new man’s character: kindness, compassion, and a forgiving disposition that breaks the chain from root to speech to community.

In short Paul treats bitterness as an interior root that becomes a habitual, chosen stance and inevitably becomes communal. Forgiveness is the commanded, Christ-shaped habit that uproots it.
