**Luke 6:37** – “Judge not, and ye shall not be judged: condemn not, and ye shall not be condemned: forgive, and ye shall be forgiven.”  

At first glance the sentence sounds like a blanket denial of any moral evaluation: *“Moral judgments never matter!”*  But the text is far more subtle.  It is warning us **against letting our own appetite for condemnation drive our thinking**, not against the very idea of right‑and‑wrong.  To understand the difference, we have to unpack two key terms:

| Term | What it really means |
|------|----------------------|
| **Judge / Condemn** | To declare someone’s actions or character “bad” enough to separate them from God, to punish, or to exclude them from community. It carries a tone of authority and a threat of loss. |
| **Discernment** | The careful, loving perception of reality—seeing the truth behind motives, intentions, and outcomes—and responding in a way that honors life and restores relationships. |

---

### 1.  “Judging” versus “Discerning”

When Jesus says *“judge not,”* he is calling us to **look beyond our own opinions**.  He wants us to see the situation as it truly is—not through the lens of our own preferences or fears.  This is *discernment*: a mental habit of asking, “What is the good here?” and “Who is being hurt?” before we decide whether to condemn.

In the New Testament, the classic illustration comes from the Parable of the Unmerciful Servant (Mark 11:20‑24).  The servant was loved, yet when he found his master stealing, he shouted, “You are my brother’s! You will not kill me!”  Even though he knew the truth, his immediate reaction was to **condemn** the thief.  Jesus replies, “But I tell you, do not condemn, but forgive.”

Here, *forgiving* is the fruit of proper discernment: we recognize the error, we acknowledge the other person’s humanity, and we choose love over wrath.

---

### 2.  The “Appetite to Condemn”

Human nature has a strong pull toward righteousness—an *appetite* to set people straight, to protect ourselves from shame, or to display our own moral superiority.  That appetite shows up as:

- Anger at a neighbor who mows your grass.
- Fear of social exclusion after a mistake.
- Pride that makes you think you’re above others’ transgressions.

When this appetite dominates, we become **judges** who impose our standards as universal law.  The result is often injustice: we condemn the innocent, alienate friends, and miss opportunities for reconciliation.

Luke 6:37 tells us to **turn down that appetite**.  It does not say we should stop caring about right and wrong; it says we should not let our desire to *punish* or *reject* another person become the measure of our relationship with them.

---

### 3.  Why Moral Judgments Still Matter

If we ignore all moral evaluation, we risk a world where “good” and “bad” are mere labels with no impact on real lives.  Yet the verse acknowledges that **we do have the power to judge**—but only when we do so with discernment and love.  The promise attached (“ye shall not be judged…”) is conditional: it follows from *how* we judge, not from the fact that we ever judge.

Think of it this way:

- **Without discernment:** We judge out of ego → condemnation, division, and loss of grace.  
- **With discernment:** We judge out of concern for the other’s well‑being → correction, healing, and restoration.

Both paths involve moral judgment, but the second path transforms judgment into **grace**.

---

### 4.  A Simple Illustration

Imagine a coworker forgets to turn off the microwave during lunch hour.  Your instinct might be to scold him, to say, “You’re disrespectful!”  That is the *appetite to condemn*.  If you act on it, you create tension and possibly damage teamwork.  If you pause, ask yourself:

- Is this a genuine mistake, or am I projecting my own concerns?
- Can I help him learn and move forward?

Jesus’ reply—*“Forgive, and ye shall be forgiven”*—encourages you to **respond with love and patience**, not with a verdict.  The moral lesson remains: the behavior matters, but the way we react determines whether we build bridges or walls.

---

### 5.  Summarizing the Difference

| Statement | Implication |
|-----------|-------------|
| **“Moral judgments never matter”** | Denies the relevance of right/wrong entirely; suggests we could live without caring about ethical choices. |
| **Luke 6:37** | Affirms that moral truths exist, but urges us to **exercise discernment**—to see those truths clearly and to act with humility and mercy.  It warns against letting our own desire to condemn cloud our judgment. |

In short, **discernment** is the *process* of perceiving reality accurately and choosing a loving response; **the appetite to condemn** is the *emotional impulse* that leads us to impose harsher, less compassionate conclusions.  Jesus invites us to keep the latter at bay, not to abandon the former altogether.

---

**Bottom line:** The verse does not say “there is nothing to judge.”  It says, “Do not let your own craving for punishment dictate how you judge others; do instead seek to understand and restore.”  That is the heart of discernment—a humble, loving way of applying moral truth.
