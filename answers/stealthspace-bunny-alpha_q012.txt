**Mainly realistic about self-deception—but the diagnosis is severe.** The passage does not simply say that people are worthless or beyond redemption. It says something more alarming in its own way: we cannot reliably understand our own deepest motives, even when we sincerely believe we do.

### What Jeremiah is diagnosing

The **heart** in biblical usage includes mind, will, desire, loyalty, and moral priorities—not just feelings. Jeremiah is describing how thoroughly these can be hidden from us.

Self-deception is not always deliberate dishonesty. A person can sincerely convince themselves that:

- wanting recognition is “being helpful”;
- resentment is “concern for truth”;
- self-protection is “loyalty to friends”;
- avoidance of responsibility is “choosing peace.”

We can select evidence to support a preferred story about ourselves without recognizing what we are doing. That makes the passage realistic, not merely cynical.

Its setting matters: Jeremiah is addressing Judah’s tendency to rely on outward religion while neglecting moral faithfulness. The question, “Who can know it?” exposes the limits of human self-examination. **The answer is not that motives are unknowable and therefore irrelevant.** The Lord searches the heart and tests the inner person. People cannot hide behind appearances, and they cannot make their self-account of themselves the final standard.

Some translations render “desperately wicked” as **“desperately sick,”** bringing out the Hebrew’s image of moral and spiritual sickness. That imagery allows for healing rather than inevitable hopelessness. The Bible’s remedy is not simply better self-analysis, but repentance, grace, and transformation.

### How should we talk about motives?

The heart’s unreliability does not make us unable to discern anything. Rather, it means **we should not mistake discernment for omniscience**.

**1. Describe what can be observed, rather than claiming to see someone’s heart.**

It is fair to say:

> “Over several months, you have dismissed others’ feedback and taken credit when they succeeded.”

It is much less fair to announce:

> “You are fundamentally arrogant and self-centered.”

The first describes a pattern that can be examined. The second claims ownership of an inner reality that we do not fully know.

**2. Mark uncertainty and invite explanation instead of dictating motives.**

Rather than “You did that only to humiliate him,” try:

> “How did you understand the situation at the time? What were you hoping would happen? What did you think this choice would accomplish?”

The other person’s account is important, but it too should be tested against their actual words, actions, and patterns.

**3. Expect mixed motives without making excuses.**

Someone can genuinely care about another person and also be driven by jealousy. They can sincerely want to serve while enjoying control. Both things may be true.

Good intentions do not make harmful conduct irrelevant, and an ugly motive does not erase every genuine good in a person. Maturity involves asking for the **whole truth**, not selecting whichever explanation is most flattering or most condemning.

**4. Be especially honest about your own motives.**

This is where the passage becomes personal. Instead of defending yourself entirely or condemning yourself entirely, specific confession is better:

> “I wanted to help, but I also wanted to be admired. I thought I was being patient, but part of me was resentful.”

That kind of honesty neither excuses the conduct nor reduces you to your worst failure. It makes you accountable to the One who searches the heart rather than depending on your own favorable interpretation.

**5. Leave final judgment to God while taking present action seriously.**

Recognizing our limited self-knowledge should produce humility, not passivity. We can confront wrongdoing, forgive, set boundaries, and refuse to trust someone whose repeated conduct is harmful—even without knowing every motive behind it.

**The appropriate posture is neither “I know exactly what you intended” nor “motives do not matter.”** It is: “Here is what I can observe; here is what I am uncertain about; here is what needs accountability and healing.” Jeremiah’s realism ultimately points beyond our ability to see ourselves perfectly: we need a God who both searches us honestly and brings us out of self-deception.
