The elder brother’s deepest problem is not simply anger, envy, wounded fairness, or pride in obedience taken separately—it is **a religion of service and reward that has quietly made himself the rightful heir to God’s love**. He can forgive the younger brother’s sins, but he cannot rejoice in the father’s mercy toward one who deserves none.

Let’s separate the options:

- **Anger** is his visible symptom.  
- **Envy** is the emotional core of the older brother’s complaint: “You never gave me a kid… but when this your son came… you killed the fatted calf.”  
- **Wounded fairness** is the grievance: “These many years I have served you… yet never gave me anything.”  
- **Pride in obedience** is the framing he brings: he sees himself as a paid servant, not a beloved son.  
- **Discomfort with mercy itself** is the deepest issue: he can accept that a sinner is forgiven only if it is not for the brother; he cannot accept the father’s unconditional welcome.

The key line is his self-description: “These many years do I serve thee.” A son does not “serve” to earn gifts; a servant does. He has turned the family relationship into a contract. His obedience is real, but it is not trust. It is a ledger. And in the father’s response—“Son, thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine”—he is told what he seems to deny: the inheritance is not a prize to be won at the end, but a relationship already given.

So the elder brother’s deepest problem is **unbelief expressed as resentment**: he believes he has been cheated, and so he must either make the younger brother’s restoration hurt or refuse to come in. He is not really defending the law; he is defending his own score. His inability to rejoice in the father’s goodness reveals that he has confused **grace with inequality**. He is angry less at the younger brother’s sin than at the father’s freedom.

The elder brother is not evil in the sense of being a deliberate murderer; he is a serious, obedient, self-justifying man who cannot bear that mercy is for everyone who repents. His deepest problem is **pride hardened into a grudge**—and Jesus tells this parable to both the disciples and the “righteous” who are near God by performance rather than by relationship.
