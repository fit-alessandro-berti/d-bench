## The comedy of the thing

The image works because it is not merely an argument but a small piece of physical theater. A man leans in, solicitous, tweezers ready, to extract a splinter from his brother's eye — while a roof joist projects from his own socket. The beam would knock the brother over before the fingers got near him. It is impossible, not just improbable, and that impossibility is the point: Jesus is not proposing a scale on which faults can be measured, but a picture that makes an ordinary posture look absurd from the outside.

Notice that both objects are wood, and both are in the same organ. *Karphos* is a chip, a bit of chaff; *dokos* is a load-bearing timber. Same material, wildly different magnitude — so the comparison isn't between his vice and my different vice, but between the same kind of fault at two scales. And the organ chosen is the most sensitive in the body. A speck in the eye is genuinely disabling; the brother's problem is real, and the desire to relieve it is not itself wrong. The satire doesn't depend on minimizing his fault. It depends on the grotesque disproportion of the helper's.

There is also a spatial joke. The speck is at the farthest remove at which one can inspect anything closely; the beam is at the nearest possible point — touching you, in you — and it is the one thing you cannot see. Everyone in the room can see it. That is why the narrator has to interject: *"and, behold, a beam is in thine own eye."* The *behold* is addressed over the corrector's shoulder to us. We are invited to look at the man who is busy looking.

## The failure is one of attention, not eyesight

The verbs are precise. "Why *beholdest* thou the mote… but *considerest* not the beam?" The second verb (*katanoieō*) means to fix the mind on something, to attend deliberately. He perceives the speck instantly; the beam he never turns his attention toward. This is not blindness in the raw sense but a habit of directed looking — scrutiny that runs outward by default. Which is exactly how moral perception actually malfunctions: we know our own faults from the inside, surrounded by intentions, pressures, and excuses, and other people's from the outside, as bare acts. The image simply inverts the arrangement, making your fault the enormous, external, publicly visible one.

The deeper sting is that the obstruction sits in the very faculty required for the task. Earlier in the same sermon the eye is the lamp of the body: if the eye is bad, the whole body is full of darkness. A beam in the eye is not an incidental blemish; it is the ruin of the instrument of judgment. And one may reasonably suspect the beam *is* the fault-finding habit itself — the censorious spirit is the timber, and every speck-hunt is an exercise of it.

## Why "hypocrite" is the right word

*Hypokritēs* is an actor, someone playing a part. The charge is not simply "you sin too." It is that he has cast himself as the physician while being far sicker than the patient. The gentleness of the offer — *"Let me pull out the mote"* — is what makes it theatrical: the tenderness is real as a performance and false as a claim about who is standing where. This is a subtler hypocrisy than open wickedness, because the hypocrite is doing something that looks like love.

Note also that the word shifts to the singular. Two general questions, then a verdict aimed at one man. The proverb turns and points.

## Why correction survives

Everything hangs on **"first… then."** Not *never*, but an order of operations. The final clause preserves the whole enterprise: *"then shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote out of thy brother's eye."* The mote still comes out. The brother is still helped. The sentence ends not with the corrector's humiliation but with two people who can see.

Three things keep this from collapsing into "no one may ever judge":

- **The same verb is used for both extractions.** *Cast out* the beam; *cast out* the mote. Self-surgery is not gentler than the surgery you proposed for him — it is the same operation performed on a larger object. The passage demands more severity toward the self, not less toward anyone.
- **"Brother" appears three times.** This is inside the household. The obligation of mutual care is presupposed, not suspended; what is being corrected is the manner and the sequence of its exercise.
- **The surrounding sermon requires discernment.** Do not give what is holy to dogs; beware false prophets; know them by their fruits. A gospel that forbade all evaluation would contradict itself two verses later. "Judge not" is being disciplined, not absolutized.

The genuine misuse of this text is to make the beam a permanent disqualification — since no one is ever finished with self-examination, no one may ever speak. But the text says the beam *can* come out, and promises clear sight afterward. The standard is honest self-scrutiny, not sinlessness. The other misuse is to turn it into a silencing tactic: *"you have a beam too"* — which is precisely the outward-pointing gesture the passage condemns, now wearing its clothes.

What remains is a demanding but usable rule: you may go to your brother, but you must go as someone who has already been under the knife, and who therefore knows what the operation costs.
