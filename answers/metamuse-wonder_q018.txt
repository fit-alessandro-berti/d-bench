Bitterness and forgiveness are placed against each other because, in Ephesians, they are two opposite ways of handling injury: **bitterness keeps the debt alive; forgiveness releases it.** They cannot easily coexist in the same heart or community.

The contrast is deliberate in the flow of the passage:

- v. 31: “Let all bitterness… be **put away** from you”
- v. 32: “And **be ye** kind… tenderhearted, **forgiving one another**”

It is the “old life / new life” pattern of Ephesians 4: put off corruption, put on the new humanity created in righteousness.

### 1. Bitterness is more than a feeling here

The Greek word is *pikria* — bitterness, sharp resentment, a sourness of spirit. Paul is not merely describing the first sting of being hurt. Ephesians has already allowed for anger as an emotion: “Be ye angry, and sin not: let not the sun go down upon your wrath” (4:26). The problem is anger **kept overnight** — nursed, rehearsed, fermented.

So bitterness in this text is best understood as:

- a **posture**: I will hold this against you.
- a **habit**: I replay the injury, interpret motives, keep accounts.
- a **disposition**: resentment becomes the lens through which I see the person.

Feelings may rise uninvited. Bitterness is what happens when those feelings are fed until they settle.

That is why Paul can command, “let it be put away.” He is not commanding people never to feel pain. He is commanding them not to cultivate pain into resentment.

### 2. The list shows bitterness spreading outward

Verse 31 is not a random pile of sins. It moves from inward root to outward damage:

> “bitterness, and wrath, and anger, and clamour, and evil speaking… with all malice”

- **Bitterness** — the hidden root  
- **Wrath / anger** — heated emotion and settled hostility  
- **Clamour** — loud contention, shouting  
- **Evil speaking** — slander, cutting speech  
- **Malice** — a general ill-will toward others  

Bitterness rarely stays private. It looks for expression, then for allies. It becomes tone, sarcasm, gossip, coldness, public argument, reputation damage. In that sense, yes — Paul treats it as **contagious**. Hebrews uses similar imagery: “lest any root of bitterness springing up trouble you, and thereby many be defiled” (Hebrews 12:15). A root is underground, but its fruit spreads.

This matters in Ephesians because the whole chapter is about unity: “keep the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace” (4:3). Bitterness grieves the Spirit (4:30) because it breaks the body from the inside.

### 3. Forgiveness is the exact reversal

Forgiveness in v. 32 is *charizomenoi* — from *charis*, grace. It means graciously giving, freely releasing. It is not pretending the wrong did not matter. It is refusing to keep collecting on the debt.

So the opposition is exact:

- Bitterness says: **You owe me, and I will remember.**
- Forgiveness says: **You wronged me, but I will not make repayment the law of our relationship.**
- Bitterness hardens the inner life.
- Being “tenderhearted” softens it — literally, having healthy inward parts, a compassionate gut-response rather than a clenched one.
- Bitterness tastes sour; kindness is what others can actually live with, benefit from, even “taste” as good.

### 4. The ground is not willpower but received grace

Paul does not say, “Forgive because it will make you feel better,” though it may. He says:

> “even as God for Christ’s sake hath forgiven you.”

The logic is: **people who have been released from an unpayable debt become people who learn to release others.** Forgiveness flows downward before it flows sideways. Bitterness forgets mercy received; forgiveness remembers it.

That also keeps forgiveness from being cheap. God’s forgiveness in Christ is not denial — it is costly grace. Human forgiveness likewise does not say, “It was nothing.” It says, “I will not let this debt define what I do to you.”

### So which is it: feeling, habit, posture, or contagion?

Paul treats bitterness as **a chosen and cultivated posture that becomes a habit, and then a communal contagion**.

The initial hurt may be involuntary. The resentment is not merely involuntary. Bitterness is anger given lodging, speech given poison, memory given a grudge to guard. Forgiveness is its opposite practice: tenderheartedness learned from Christ, repeated “one to another,” until grace becomes the atmosphere of the community.
