Isaiah 55:7 is built on Hebrew parallelism:

> “Let the wicked **forsake his way**,  
> and the unrighteous man **his thoughts**;  
> and let him **return** to the Lord…”

The two lines are not asking for two unrelated reforms. They describe **one whole-person turning** from two angles: the outward life and the inward life.

## 1. “Way” = outward conduct, the direction of life

“Way” (Hebrew *derek*) means road, path, journey, or habitual manner of life. It is the visible trajectory of a person’s choices: what he does, what he worships, how he treats others, the habits he has built.

To “forsake his way” is therefore not merely to feel sorry about isolated sins. It is to **abandon a whole direction of life**—to stop walking down the road of rebellion. In Isaiah, the “way” of the wicked includes idolatry, injustice, empty religious ritual, and trust in human power instead of God (cf. Isa 1:16–17; 59:7–8).

So true repentance has an **ethical, bodily, public** dimension. Grace does not leave a person on the same road.

## 2. “Thoughts” = inward imagination, plans, and purposes

“Thoughts” (Hebrew *maḥăšābôt*) means more than passing ideas. It refers to **plans, designs, intentions, purposes, mental schemes**—what we might call the “imagination” of the heart. In Scripture, the heart’s thoughts are the workshop from which behavior comes:

- “Every imagination of the thoughts of his heart was only evil continually” (Gen 6:5).
- “Out of the heart proceed evil thoughts…” (Matt 15:19).
- “As he thinks in his heart, so is he” (Prov 23:7).

The “unrighteous man” is called a man of *’āwen*—“iniquity” or “vanity/idolatry.” His thoughts are not neutral; they are the inner counsels by which he suppresses truth, constructs idols, justifies sin, and devises a life apart from God.

So to “forsake his thoughts” is to repent of the **hidden mind**: the false beliefs, fantasies, rationalizations, desires, and self-ruling plans that fuel the sinful “way.”

## 3. Why both?

Because sin is not merely external behavior, and it is not merely private feelings. Sin has taken over the **whole person**—mind and body, imagination and action. Biblical repentance (*šûb* / “return,” and later *metanoia*) is therefore a U-turn of the entire self.

If only the “way” changes but the thoughts remain, you have external morality without heart-change—hypocrisy (cf. Isa 29:13; Matt 23:25–27).

If only the “thoughts” change but the way remains, you have sentiment without obedience—faith without fruit (cf. Matt 3:8; James 2:17).

This is made even clearer by the next verses:

> “For my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways…  
> For as the heavens are higher than the earth, so are my ways higher than your ways, and my thoughts than your thoughts” (Isa 55:8–9).

The human problem is that **our thoughts and ways** are low, crooked, and self-directed. Salvation means abandoning *our* way and *our* thoughts and returning to the God whose thoughts are merciful and whose ways redeem.

## 4. What this implies about mercy

The verse ends with a staggering promise:

> “…and let him return unto the Lord, and he will have mercy upon him; and to our God, for he will abundantly pardon.”

Several things follow:

### a. Mercy is lavish, not grudging
“Abundantly pardon” literally means “multiply to forgive.” God does not pardon minimally. His grace is greater than the depth and number of sins (cf. Isa 1:18; Mic 7:18–19; Rom 5:20). No one is beyond the reach of mercy whose sin is truly forsaken.

### b. Mercy is not permission to stay unchanged
The promise is given to the one who **forsakes** and **returns**. Pardon is not a blanket amnesty for those who cling to the old way or cherish the old thoughts. Grace and repentance belong together: grace creates the turn, and the turn receives the grace.

### c. Mercy is transformative
The God who pardons also sends His word “like rain” to make the barren life fruitful (Isa 55:10–11). He promises a new heart and a new spirit (Ezek 36:25–27). So the demand to forsake way *and* thoughts is not a ladder we climb to earn forgiveness; it describes the shape of the life that forgiveness creates.

### d. Mercy is urgent
“Seek the Lord **while he may be found**; call upon him **while he is near**” (v. 6). There is a season of grace. The door is open now, but the call implies that it will not always remain open (cf. Prov 1:24–28; 2 Cor 6:2).

## In short

Repentance in Isaiah 55:6–7 involves both “way” and “thoughts” because God saves **whole persons**. Outward conduct must be abandoned, because sin is lived in the body and in the world. Inward imagination must be surrendered, because sin is conceived in the heart. And the possibility of mercy is real and abundant—but it is mercy for those who **turn from** their own way and thoughts and **return to the Lord**, who “will abundantly pardon.”
