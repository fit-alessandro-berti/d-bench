The passage is sharp because it turns an abstract moral failing into a literal, bodily picture: the hypocrite is a person offering to remove a tiny “mote,” or speck, from another’s eye while ignoring a whole “beam,” or plank, stuck in his own. The scale is absurd and physical: a splinter versus a plank, both supposedly lodged in the eye. So the hypocrite is not merely “biased” or “insensitive”; he is comically self-deluded, diagnosing another’s minor blindness while walking around with a giant obstruction in his own vision.

It criticizes hypocrisy by exposing this disproportion: you cannot correct another’s small fault while pretending not to see your own larger fault. The problem is not that judgment is bad, but that judgment from a compromised position is false, presumptuous, and morally dishonest.

Yet the passage does not forbid correction. It explicitly prescribes an order: remove the beam from your own eye first; then you may see clearly to remove the mote from your brother’s eye. Moral correction becomes legitimate once the criticizer has examined himself and corrected his own obstructing fault.
