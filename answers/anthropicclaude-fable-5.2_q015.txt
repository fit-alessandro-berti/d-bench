## Why the image is so sharp

**The absurd disproportion.** A mote is a speck of sawdust or chaff; a beam is a roof timber. The picture of a man with a rafter jutting from his face, squinting to find a splinter in someone else's eye, is deliberately comic. The humor lets the listener laugh before realizing the joke is about them.

**It's the eye.** The fault sits in the organ of perception itself. The beam is not just a bigger sin but a blinding one, damaging the very faculty needed to judge. And since eyes are delicate, the image asks whether you would let a half-blind man operate on yours.

**Same material, different scale.** Mote and beam are both wood. This suggests that the critic's fault is often the same kind as the one he condemns, only larger. We tend to be sharpest at spotting in others the failings we know from the inside.

**The verbs.** You "behold" your brother's speck but do not "consider" your own beam. The critic's attention to others is keen, and his attention to himself is absent. He may even use the first to avoid the second.

**"Hypocrite."** The Greek word means stage actor. The critic is playing the role of the eye doctor, a performance of moral concern rather than the real thing.

## Why correction is not forbidden

**The grammar is "first... then."** The passage does not end with "leave your brother's eye alone." It ends with "then shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote." This sets an order of operations, not a prohibition.

**The mote is real.** Jesus never says the brother's fault is imaginary or unimportant. A speck in the eye hurts and should come out. The repeated word "brother" assumes a relationship in which helping is the natural thing to do.

**Self-examination is what qualifies the helper.** Removing your own beam does two things. It restores your sight, so you can judge accurately. It also changes your posture: someone who has gone through that painful extraction approaches another's eye gently, as a fellow patient rather than a superior. The correction becomes care instead of condemnation.

So the target is not moral judgment but judgment used as an escape from self-knowledge. The passage does not teach "never correct." It teaches that honest correction begins with oneself, and that only that kind is likely to help.
