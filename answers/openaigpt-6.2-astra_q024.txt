External compliance means doing what a command requires, perhaps from fear of consequences, while inwardly resisting it. Receiving a “new heart” changes not just what a person does but how that person responds to God. The statutes still matter: the promise in verse 27 is precisely that the people will walk in them. The difference is that obedience will grow out of a willing response to God rather than being merely an outward act.

In Ezekiel, the “heart” is the center of understanding, desire, and decision, not just the seat of emotion. Stone makes hardness almost tangible: the obstacle is not that God’s people lack instructions, but that they have become unresponsive to them. Flesh, by contrast, is living and capable of response. A “heart of flesh” is not a weaker heart; it is one that can be moved.

The image of replacement also tells us who acts. In this promise to restore Israel, God does not say, “Soften your hearts and then I will help you.” He says, “I will take away,” “I will give,” and “I will put my spirit within you.” The people themselves will walk in God’s ways, but that walk begins with what God gives them.
