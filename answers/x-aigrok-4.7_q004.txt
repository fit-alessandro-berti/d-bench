The trust asked for is whole-person reliance on God, not a rejection of thinking.

In Proverbs, “heart” is the inner person—mind, will, and desire together—not a feeling set against reason. “Trust in the Lord with all thine heart” means making God the one you finally rest on in every part of life. “Lean not unto thine own understanding” names the alternative: treating your own insight as a sufficient support. The next line makes the posture practical: know and acknowledge him in all your ways, and he will make the path straight. The issue is where you put your weight, not whether you use your mind.

That is humble self-distrust, and it fits the rest of Proverbs. The book commands people to get wisdom, treasure understanding, listen to instruction, and seek counsel. Human discernment is real and necessary; it is also limited, biased, and easily bent by pride or desire. The wise person therefore does not abolish reason. They refuse to make their own understanding the last court of appeal, especially where it collides with God’s character and word. Fear of the Lord is the beginning of knowledge here, not a substitute for it.

Mere anti-intellectualism is different. It treats inquiry, learning, or careful thought as unfaithful in themselves, as if ignorance were humility and “God will guide me” excused refusing to think. Nothing in this verse requires that. The warning is against autonomous self-reliance—“thine own understanding” as the thing you lean on—not against understanding as such. Faith, on this reading, submits the mind; it does not switch it off.
