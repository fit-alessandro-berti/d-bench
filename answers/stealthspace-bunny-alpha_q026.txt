**All three—but they are interconnected.** Jesus presents speech as the visible fruit of a hidden moral orientation. That makes speech a **symptom** of the heart, a **moral choice** of the person, and a **revelation** that others—and ultimately God—may confront. The text’s main emphasis, however, is on what speech reveals about its source.

### The heart as a moral “treasure”

The “heart” here is the inner moral person: the desires, affections, loyalties, and commitments from which a person acts. Jesus’ language of “good treasure” and “evil treasure” is figurative, not a claim that the heart literally stores sentences until they are released. A person *treasures* certain ways of living and treats them as the resources from which life draws.

Immediately before this passage, Jesus compares a tree to its fruit: “a good tree cannot bring forth evil fruit” (12:33). Speech, then, is one kind of fruit. The claim is that **what comes out of the mouth cannot be detached from what a person fundamentally loves and honors**.

### Speech as all three

| Dimension | How the passage connects speech and the heart |
|---|---|
| **Symptom** | Words disclose a person’s moral condition. Someone’s speech may reveal priorities and allegiances that a polite exterior conceals. “Out of the abundance of the heart” suggests that speech draws upon the heart’s underlying resources, not merely upon the speaker’s latest intention. |
| **Choice** | Speaking is itself morally accountable conduct. A person can speak truthfully, mercifully, and honestly, or speak falsely, cruelly, and hypocritically. Those are not merely mechanical emissions; they are expressions of the speaker’s commitments. But words alone do not guarantee a good heart, nor does a good word necessarily demonstrate a wholly good character. |
| **Revelation** | Speech exposes what the speaker is trying to conceal or has not yet recognized. That is especially important in the context: the Pharisees’ accusation that Jesus casts out demons “by Beelzebub” itself expresses the evil alignment Jesus is diagnosing. Their words reveal the fruit of their supposed tree. |

### Why “revelation” matters most here

Jesus is not offering a neutral theory about verbal habits. He is exposing a **fundamental moral allegiance**, and he is doing so in response to religious leaders who claim to recognize good while attributing Jesus’ good work to Satan.

The “good treasure” and “evil treasure” therefore connect speech with **integrity**: what someone says cannot permanently be separated from what they actually trust and serve. Speech may imitate goodness, but its underlying source remains morally significant.

Two qualifications are important:

- **Speech is revealing, but not an infallible shortcut into another person’s soul.** The passage weighs a person’s fundamental direction, not every isolated sentence in isolation.
- **The diagnosis is serious, not necessarily a claim of inescapable determinism.** The Bible elsewhere speaks of the heart being renewed by God’s Spirit. Evil does not define a person’s final destiny.

In short, the passage depicts a tightly connected movement:

**What is treasured in the heart shapes what is said; what is said reveals what is treasured; and how a person chooses to speak both expresses and strengthens that moral orientation.** The words are not meaningless symptoms—but neither are they the whole person. They are visible actions that disclose the hidden heart.
