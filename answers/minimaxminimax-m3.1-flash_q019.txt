## Setting the verse in its paragraph

James 3:1–12 is a whole anatomy of the tongue: the teacher's accountability (3:1–2), the tongue compared to a bit, a ship, and a fire (vv. 3–6), then this contradiction (vv. 9–10), and then the origin of the fire itself (vv. 11–12). So the question "Why does the tongue do this?" was already being asked before verse 9. James's answer is that the tongue is *unusually diagnostic*.

One note on the KJV's "Therewith": it means "with the tongue" — the same fire-and-iniquity member of v. 8. The contradiction James describes is not occurring in two different people; it is happening inside **one instrument**.

---

## 1. The obvious contradiction: one instrument, two opposite acts

> "With it we bless God the Father; and with it we curse men made in the image of God."

Blessing and cursing are not two styles of speech. In the Old Testament they are **covenant invocations**: to bless is to invoke God's favour (Gen 12:2–3; 22:17), to curse is to invoke God's judgement (Gen 27:11; Num 24:9). Isaac's blessing literally *becomes* a curse when it is redirected. So a mouth that blesses and curses is not being "balanced" — it is invoking the God of heaven in two mutually exclusive directions at once, in the same breath.

That is why James does not say only "that is unpleasant." He says these things "ought not so to be" (οὐκ οὕτως — "how can it be so?" or "how shameful!"). His verdict is that the double speech is a **disorder**, an incoherence in the person producing it — not merely bad manners.

## 2. The deeper contradiction: cursing the image is an attack on God

This is where the phrase "made after the similitude of God" carries the weight of the whole argument. The image *is* of God. Therefore:

- To curse a human being is, knowingly or not, to strike at God's own likeness, because the image stands in God's place (Gen 9:5–6: "the image of God hath God avenged").
- The Third Commandment's logic now applies: since God's name and God's image cannot be separated, a tongue that blesses God and curses the image has effectively **cursed God with the same mouth it praised Him**.

So the contradiction is not between two loyalties (God and my enemy). It is between **God and God** — the God invoked in blessing and the God whose likeness is being trampled in cursing. That is why the passage moves so quickly to "Out of the same mouth proceedeth blessing and cursing": the source of both is identified, and the source cannot be two.

James also puts it pastorally: "My brethren." The brother you are about to curse is *of the family* you have just addressed as Father.

## 3. The contradiction in the person: a divided soul

James's diagnosis runs backward from v. 6: the tongue is a fire, ridden by powers "set on fire by Gehenna." A person cannot curse without having already passed a verdict — someone has been judged contemptible, and judgement has been called down on them. Meanwhile the same person confesses that God is good. The double mouth is therefore the **audible shape of a divided heart**: "double minded" (1:8; 4:8), wanting both (1:14–15) and refusing to repent.

The tongue is not the *cause* of the disorder here; it is the place where the disorder becomes visible. Like a crack running through a bell, the incoherence in the person shows up first in the note.

---

## Why speech reveals moral disorder so quickly

1. **It is voluntary.** The tongue is the member most directly under the command of the will. Cursing is not a reflex like blushing — it is an act, an endorsement. So the tongue furnishes an unusually reliable report on what a person is *willing to affirm*.

2. **It is involuntary in its disclosure.** Even the guarded tongue betrays: which words are chosen, what is emphasised, to whom it is turned. Jesus uses exactly this: "the mouth… out of the abundance of the heart" (Matt 12:34), "for you have spoken plainly" (John 8:6).

3. **It reveals settled judgement, not passing mood.** You cannot curse without an opinion of the person cursed. Speech is the organ by which evaluation is externalised — "the heart of the righteous studieth to answer" (Prov 10:28); "the foolish man walketh notoriously in the streets" (12:18).

4. **It is public and consequential.** Private disorder becomes social damage. James's measure of the community's health is its speech; a tongue that curses the brother dissolves the brotherhood the same sentence claims.

5. **It is fast and irreversible.** The tongue outruns deliberation (James 1:19: "quick to hear, slow to speak"). A word cannot be unsaid, and it cannot be recalled to its speaker — it stays with the hearer. James's phrase for the tongue's sphere is a "world of iniquity" (3:6): the tongue builds its own environment.

6. **It is the deciding member in Scripture and in the tradition James inherits.** "The heart of the righteous studieth to answer" (Prov 10:28); "the mouth of the righteous is the root of life" (10:31); the two-ways tradition — "with the accord of his mouth do men go to the house of truth or of wickedness" (Avot 1:2). James takes up the old question **"Is it possible for one mouth to bless and curse?"** and answers it in terms of the image of God: no, not legitimately; the tongue's dual use is a mark of a creation gone wrong, not a live option for the redeemed.

7. **It is the organ of the image.** A human tongue is a speaking instrument built for confession — the most God-like member we have. So it is precisely where the image is either honoured or mocked. The tongue is the fastest place the soul's disorder becomes *legible to others*.

---

## What James is *not* saying

- Not that the believer never offends: "we all stumble in many things" (3:2). The tongue is where imperfection is most visible, not where perfection is most expected.
- Not that the cure is suppression. James's counsel is not grim self-control but a humble, gentle, forgiving heart (3:13–16) and a tongue like a "peaceable olive tree" that gives the good fruit of wisdom (3:12).
- Not that the contradiction is harmless. The same fire that curses sets the whole body ablaze (3:6, 8).

## The last word

The section ends with the hunted man and the peaceable olive tree, and James's letter moves straight into chapter 4, where the tongue's disorder is traced to "the lusts that are at strife in your members." The tongue sets itself on fire *from Gehenna* — which means the resolution is not technique but judgement and grace. Scripture's own picture of the redeemed tongue is a seraphic one: threefold "Holy, holy, holy" (Isa 6:3), and a God who asks the man who joins himself to the devourers to lift up his head (Ps 50:20). The tongue that once blessed and cursed is meant to become wholly one thing.

And at the centre of it stands the One who took our contradictory speech into Himself — the cry that sounded like a curse and the prayer that surrendered at the same time — so that "we might be silent" and speak as those made after the similitude of God (Heb 12:2).
