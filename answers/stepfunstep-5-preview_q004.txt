The trust Proverbs asks for is **whole-person, covenantal reliance on the Lord**—not a blind rejection of the mind.

“Trust in the Lord with all thine heart” means to place your confidence, affections, will, and thinking under God’s authority. In biblical terms, the “heart” is the whole inner person, not just emotion. So the verse is not saying, “Don’t think”; it is saying, **do not make your own finite understanding the final authority**.

The phrase “lean not unto thine own understanding” gives the key image: don’t put the full weight of your life on your own point of view. Your understanding is real, useful, and God-given, but it is limited, biased, and capable of rationalizing. Humble self-distrust says:

> “I may be wrong. I cannot see everything. My motives are mixed. I need God’s wisdom beyond my own cleverness.”

That is not anti-intellectualism. It is the realism of a creature who knows he is not God.

Mere anti-intellectualism, by contrast, despises learning, avoids serious questions, treats doubt as failure, or uses “just trust God” to escape responsibility for thinking. But the book of Proverbs itself repeatedly commands us to seek wisdom and understanding. So the point is not to stop using the mind, but to keep the mind from becoming an idol. Think hard; seek counsel; study the evidence; then submit the outcome to God. Use understanding as a lamp, not as a sun.

“In all thy ways acknowledge him” means every area of life—work, relationships, money, decisions, suffering—is brought under God’s authority. Then, and only then, can the promise make sense: “he shall direct thy paths.” His direction is not a shortcut around reality, but the wise, moral, and loving ordering of a life entrusted to him.
