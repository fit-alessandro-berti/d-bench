This passage presents speech as a **convergence of symptom, choice, and revelation**, but it does so in a specific order that gives each dimension a distinct role.

**1. Words as symptoms of the heart's hidden state**  
Jesus opens with a diagnostic statement: “out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaketh.” Here, speech is not primarily a moral failure of the moment; it is the outward overflow of an inward reality. The “hidden moral stockpile” is the heart’s stored disposition of values, desires, and character. The words we speak are not just isolated decisions; they are the audible trace of what has long been accumulating inside us. In this sense, speech *symptomatically* reveals the hidden moral reality.

**2. Words as the natural outworking of the heart’s “treasure”**  
The metaphor of “treasure” gives the heart a moral economy. A good heart, filled with good treasures, “bringeth forth good things”; an evil heart, filled with evil treasures, “bringeth forth evil things.” The Greek word behind “treasure” (*thesauros*) implies something stored up over time. So speech is not random; it is the consistent expression of what has been stored up internally. This elevates speech from mere symptom to *active consequence* of one’s moral inventory. The connection is organic, not mechanical: the heart’s hidden stockpiles naturally produce words that align with their character.

**3. Words as deliberate choices that are also judged**  
The second half of the passage describes human agents: “A good man... bringeth forth good things” and “an evil man... bringeth forth evil things.” The verbs “bring forth” imply agency and choice. While the heart’s abundance is the source, the speaker is not a passive victim; they actively emit from what they possess. This means words are also *choices* in the sense that a person exercises moral responsibility for what they say and are consequently accountable. The passage does not present words as merely impulsive symptoms or automatic revelations; it holds the speaker accountable for exercising their stored moral goods or vices through speech.

**4. The integrated answer: speech as symptom, choice, and revelation**  
The passage does suggest all three, but it frames them in a specific hierarchy:
- **Revelation** is primary and implicit: by speaking, a person unintentionally discloses “the abundance of the heart.” Speech reveals what is hidden.
- **Symptom** is the immediate effect: words are the visible expression of an internal moral condition.
- **Choice** is the active dimension: because the heart’s treasure shapes what is brought forth, the speaker is morally responsible for the words they choose.

However, the passage does *not* treat these as equal or interchangeable. It does not say “people choose words which create their hearts” (a behaviorist or purely voluntarist view). Rather, it says the heart’s hidden stockpiles naturally overflow into speech, and that speech in turn discloses and embodies what is already there, drawing out the moral character of the speaker. In other words, speech is a **revealing symptom** of the heart’s hidden moral treasure, **chooseable within that condition**, and therefore **acting as a revelation of the soul’s true moral composition**.

**In short:** The passage connects speech to the heart’s hidden moral “stockpiles” by describing them as the source from which words flow. It suggests that words are **symptoms** (the observable overflow of an internal state), **choices** (moral acts accountable to the speaker), and **revelations** (disclosures of what is actually stored in the heart). But the foundational logic is one of organic alignment: the heart’s hidden treasure determines the kind of speech that flows, and speech, in turn, publicly and responsibly reveals the true moral nature of the person.
