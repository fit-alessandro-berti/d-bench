Luke 6:37 is not a declaration that moral distinctions are unimportant. It is a warning about **what we do with moral knowledge—and what posture we take toward other people when we use it**.

The context matters. Jesus has just commanded love of enemies and concluded, “Be ye therefore merciful, as your Father also is merciful” (Luke 6:36). Yet the following verses do not abandon evaluation: trees are known by their fruits, and the person with a beam in his own eye must first remove it, “and then shalt thou see clearly” to address the speck in another’s eye (vv. 41–44). Jesus does **not** say that the speck is imaginary or must forever be ignored. The problem is distorted, self-exonerating sight.

Indeed, the command to forgive would lose its meaning if moral judgments never mattered. If nothing could truly be wrong, there would be nothing to forgive.

### Discernment and condemnation

**Discernment** asks: *What is true, and what do truth, justice, and love require of me?* It can name an action as wrong, distinguish harm from excuse, protect the vulnerable, and support firm consequences. It is willing to say, “This must stop,” or, “You cannot presently be trusted with this responsibility.”

The **appetite to condemn** goes further—or often starts somewhere else entirely. It asks, consciously or not: *How does this person’s guilt establish my superiority?* Its characteristic signs include:

- eagerness to reach a verdict, rather than patience to understand;
- reducing a whole person to a worst act or accusation;
- confidence about others’ motives combined with little examination of one’s own;
- pleasure in exposure, humiliation, or punishment;
- resistance to repentance, because restoration would deprive us of a satisfying enemy.

Discernment may be severe, but it need not be contemptuous. Condemnation, in the sense Jesus warns against, wants not merely to identify wrong but to deliver a final verdict on another person’s worth—and often to enjoy the authority of doing so.

This distinction also protects against a sentimental misuse of the passage. “Judge not” does not require silence about abuse, indifference to victims, or the abandonment of justice. Forgiveness is not the same as pretending nothing happened, restoring trust automatically, reconciling under every circumstance, or removing every consequence. One can truthfully testify against wrongdoing, maintain boundaries, and still refuse to make vengeance or humiliation one’s satisfaction.

The warning “ye shall not be judged” places the would-be judge under judgment as well. I am not invited to become morally blind; I am reminded that I, too, need truthful judgment and mercy.

So the difference might be summarized this way: **discernment seeks truth even when it implicates me; the appetite to condemn seeks a verdict that acquits me.** Jesus calls for clear sight governed by mercy—truth without contempt, accountability without vengeance, and self-examination before accusation.
