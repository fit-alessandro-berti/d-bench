The danger is not visibility itself, but the **craving to be seen**—the deliberate use of public goodness as a means to win human approval.

Look at the syntax of the verse: Jesus warns against doing alms “**before men, to be seen of them**” (Greek: *pros to theathēnai autois*—“for the purpose of being seen by them”). The visibility is the scene; the **purpose clause** is the sin. The act is not condemned because it is public, but because it is **performative**: it is done not for God or for neighbor in need, but to harvest praise.

Jesus makes this explicit in the next clause: “**otherwise ye have no reward of your Father**.” Those who act “to be seen” do receive a reward—human applause (v. 2, “they have their reward”)—but they have already been paid in full by men, leaving nothing left from God. The moral danger, then, is **misplaced reward-seeking**: the heart trades filial communion with the Father for the cheaper coin of social status.

This is why Jesus does not say “hide all your good works” (contrast Matt. 5:16—“let your light so shine before men”). Public goodness is not forbidden; **self-advertising righteousness** is. The problem is interior: the ego’s hunger for recognition turns charity into theater, and the actor ends up worshipping the audience instead of God.

So: **visibility is innocent; the craving is lethal.** The moment “being seen” becomes the goal, the deed is morally hollow—done not in secret with God, but in public for self.
