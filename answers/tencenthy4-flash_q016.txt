The elder brother in Luke 15 is a masterful study of a heart that is outwardly religious but inwardly alienated from the Father’s character. While all the options you list are present in the text, they exist in layers. The **deepest problem is discomfort with mercy itself**—a resentment of grace that flows from a transactional, self-righteous heart.

Here is how the layers stack up, from the surface down to the root:

### 1. Anger (The Symptom)
The text opens with the surface emotion: *"And he was angry, and would not go in"* (v. 28). Anger is the visible eruption, but Jesus is careful to show us that it is not the disease itself—it is the fever.

### 2. Envy and Wounded Fairness (The Immediate Grievance)
The elder brother’s complaint is couched in the language of comparison and scorekeeping: *"thou never gavest me a kid... but as soon as this thy son was come... thou hast killed for him the fatted calf"* (vv. 29–30). He feels shortchanged. He believes he has been unfairly treated because the "prodigal" receives a celebration he never earned. His sense of justice is retributive: the sinner should get the rod, and the faithful servant should get the party. When the Father violates that calculus, the elder brother cries "Unfair!"

### 3. Pride in Obedience (The Foundation of the Grievance)
Beneath the envy is a deep trust in his own merit: *"Lo, these many years do I serve thee, neither transgressed I at any time thy commandment"* (v. 29). He views his relationship with the Father as a contract: *I obey, You pay.* He has reduced sonship to wages. This pride makes it impossible for him to accept that the Father’s love is a gift rather than a reward. Because he believes he has *earned* his standing, he cannot stand the idea that the prodigal has *received* his standing freely.

### 4. Discomfort with Mercy Itself (The Deepest Problem)
This is the core issue, and it is exposed by the Father’s response. The Father does not argue with the elder brother’s accounting; he reveals the brother’s fundamental misunderstanding of who the Father is and what the relationship is:

*   **"Son, thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine" (v. 31):** The Father gently corrects the elder brother’s sense of lack. He is telling him, *“You aren’t a hired hand who has been underpaid; you are a son who already owns everything. You have been living in grace all along, but you couldn’t see it because you were too busy comparing your ledger to his.”*
*   **"It was meet that we should make merry... for this thy brother was dead, and is alive again" (v. 32):** Notice the shift in language. The elder brother called the prodigal *"this thy son"* (disowning him). The Father calls him *"this thy brother"* (reclaiming him). The Father is saying, *“The reason I celebrated is not because I overlooked his sin, but because I rejoice in redemption. Your problem is that you cannot rejoice when I show mercy.”*

The elder brother is angry not merely because his brother got a party, but because **his Father is merciful**. He is offended by the very idea of unmerited favor. He would rather the Father be "just" (by his definition) than be loving. His refusal to enter the feast is a refusal to endorse the Father’s grace—he would rather stand outside in the cold and be right than go inside and celebrate mercy.

### Summary
The elder brother’s deepest problem is a **heart that prefers the economy of merit to the economy of grace**. His pride in his own obedience makes him resent the Father’s mercy toward others. He is a mirror held up to the self-righteous: he kept the rules, but he missed the point. He was so busy serving the Father that he forgot to *enjoy* the Father—and he could not bear it when someone else was welcomed home purely on the basis of the Father’s compassionate love.

As the Father’s words imply, the healing for the elder brother is the same as for the prodigal: he needs to realize that he, too, lives entirely on mercy, and learn to say with the Father, *"It was meet to make merry and be glad."*
