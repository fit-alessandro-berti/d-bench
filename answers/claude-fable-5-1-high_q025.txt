Matthew 6:1 — Public Goodness and the Craving to Be Seen

Short answer: the verse does not condemn visibility. It condemns a purpose. The danger lies in the clause "to be seen of them," and everything else in the passage is built to expose how a good deed is hollowed out when being watched becomes the reason for doing it.

1. What the verse actually says

Two details of the text fix the target of the warning.

First, the Greek behind "to be seen of them" is pros to theathēnai autois, a purpose construction: "with a view to being gazed at by them." Jesus does not say "do not do your alms where men can see"; he says "do not do them in order to be seen." The circumstance (people are present) and the intention (their gaze is the goal) are grammatically separated, and only the intention is forbidden. The verb theaomai is also loaded: it is the root of theatron, "theater." In the next verse the people who give this way are called hypokritai, which in ordinary Greek meant stage actors. The vocabulary of the whole section is theatrical. The problem is not that goodness is visible but that it has become a performance, with an audience, a script, and applause as the pay.

Second, the earliest manuscripts read dikaiosynē, "righteousness," rather than eleēmosynē, "alms" (the KJV follows the later reading). Verse 1 is therefore a heading for all three examples that follow: almsgiving (6:2-4), prayer (6:5-15), fasting (6:16-18). Jesus is not making a narrow point about charity; he is diagnosing a disease that can infect any form of righteousness whatsoever.

2. Why visibility itself cannot be the problem

The decisive evidence is a few verses earlier. In 5:16 Jesus commands: "Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father which is in heaven." The phrase "before men" (emprosthen tōn anthrōpōn) is word-for-word identical in 5:16 and 6:1. In one place it is commanded, in the other warned against. What changes is the purpose clause attached to it:

- 5:16: that they may see your good works and glorify your Father.
- 6:1-2: that you may be seen by them and be glorified by men.

Even the verb "glorify" (doxazō) is the same in both places. The only variable is the direction of the glory: toward the Father or toward the self. In 5:16 what is seen is the works; in 6:1 what is seen is you. Visibility is a constant in both; it is the object of the seeing that flips.

Jesus' own practice confirms this. He healed in synagogues, taught in public, fed crowds, and told the healed leper to show himself to the priest. Chrysostom drew the obvious conclusion in his homily on this passage: it is possible to give alms before men and not do it to be seen, and possible to give alms in secret and still do it to be seen. Christ, he says, is not looking at the act but at the intention. A recluse can be a peacock; a public benefactor can be genuinely self-forgetful. Location does not settle the matter.

So the answer to the second question is clear: the problem is the craving, not the visibility. But that answer is too easy on its own. The verse calls this craving a danger serious enough to void the deed ("otherwise ye have no reward"), and the more interesting question is why.

3. What the craving does to goodness

a) It reverses the direction of the act.

Alms are, by definition, a gift outward: something leaves me and goes to the poor. When the deed is done to be seen, the direction inverts. The poor person becomes a prop, the coin becomes a purchase, and what flows back to me (esteem) is the real object of the transaction. The deed keeps the outward shape of generosity while its inner motion is acquisition. That is what makes it worse than simple selfishness: it uses the form of self-giving as the vehicle for self-getting. The recipient is instrumentalized in the very act that pretends to honor him.

b) It installs the wrong judge.

The verse sets two audiences opposite each other: "before men" and "your Father which is in heaven." The person who acts to be seen has decided, in practice, that the crowd's verdict is the one that counts. That is a quiet form of idolatry. Human opinion has been seated on the throne where God's judgment belongs, and the whole moral life is being conducted before a tribunal that is partial, fickle, and easily fooled. The Father "seeth in secret" (6:4); he sees the whole act, including its motive. The crowd sees only surfaces. To crave the crowd's gaze is to trade a complete gaze for a fragmentary one, and to prefer the fragment.

c) It gets paid, and the payment closes the account.

"They have their reward" (6:2) uses apechousin, a word found on ancient receipts meaning "paid in full." Jesus does not say the hypocrites go unrewarded; he says they have been paid, by the audience they were performing for. This is the sharpest edge of the passage. You get what you aimed at. If you aimed at being seen, being seen is what you receive, and once received it exhausts the deed. Nothing remains to be given by the Father, because nothing was directed to him. The craving to be seen is dangerous because it is satisfiable: the applause really does come, and it really is the end of the matter.

Notice that Jesus does not attack the desire for reward as such. He speaks of reward three times in three verses and promises that the Father "shall reward thee openly." The point is not to want nothing for your goodness but to want the right thing from the right person. The Father's approval is the proper completion of a good deed; the crowd's approval is a substitute that arrives sooner and pays less.

d) It divides the self.

A hypocrite, in the original sense, is someone whose face and mask do not match. The person who does good to be seen wills two things at once: the good, and being admired for the good. Kierkegaard's formula applies: purity of heart is to will one thing. Two aims cannot both be ultimate; one becomes the master and the other its servant. In the vainglorious person, admiration is the master and the good deed its servant, which means that when the two come apart (when goodness would cost admiration, or admiration could be had without goodness) admiration wins. This is why Jesus opens with "take heed" (prosechete), a call to vigilance. The two givers may drop identical coins into the same box. The difference between them is invisible to every observer, and therefore observers cannot be trusted to catch it. Only the doer, and the Father who sees in secret, can.

e) It hides inside virtue.

Most vices announce themselves. This one grows in the soil of good deeds and feeds on them. Augustine, in Confessions X, admits that the love of praise is the temptation he could least escape, because it returned precisely when he did something right, and even when he rebuked it he found himself pleased with the rebuke. The better you become, the more raw material the craving has. That is what makes public goodness specifically dangerous rather than merely neutral: it is the one place where a person doing right is most exposed to a corruption that feels like doing right.

f) It makes goodness fragile.

Goodness that lives on an audience dies without one. If the reason for the deed is the gaze, then in the dark, in private, with no one keeping score, the reason evaporates. A virtue that requires spectators is not a stable disposition but a response to stimulus. Jesus' remedy in 6:3-4 is, among other things, a test: do the thing where no one can see, and find out whether you would still do it.

4. The deeper twist: the self as spectator

The instruction in 6:3, "let not thy left hand know what thy right hand doeth," pushes the diagnosis one step further. Even when the crowd is removed, an audience remains: oneself. It is possible to give in perfect secrecy and still perform, watching oneself give, admiring one's own discretion, enjoying the private theater of one's humility. Bonhoeffer called this the last and hardest form of the problem and read 6:3 as a command to hide our righteousness from ourselves: to act with a kind of self-forgetfulness in which the deed is done and released rather than done and savored.

This confirms that visibility was never the issue. The most private deed can be fully corrupted by the reflexive gaze. What the verse targets is the posture of watching oneself be good, whether the watching is done through others' eyes or one's own. The opposite of that posture is not concealment but attention fixed elsewhere: on the neighbor who needs the gift, and on the Father before whom the gift is made.

5. Why, then, does Jesus prescribe secrecy?

If the fault is intention rather than location, why does the passage lean so hard on hiddenness: give in secret, pray behind a shut door, fast with a washed face? Because secrecy is prescribed as a discipline, not as a principle. The craving to be seen feeds on being seen; removing the audience starves it. Concealment is the therapy that fits the disease, the way a person prone to drink stays out of taverns. It is not that taverns are evil but that this person, in this condition, cannot handle them. Visibility is a legitimate but hazardous occasion for a fallen heart, and Jesus counsels the ordinary believer to avoid the hazard where possible.

Held together with 5:16, the rule becomes: let your goodness be visible when visibility serves the neighbor or points to God; keep it hidden when visibility would serve only you; and in either case act as though the only spectator who matters is the one who sees in secret. Bonhoeffer's summary is apt: our good works must be visible, but never done for the sake of being visible.

6. Conclusion

What makes public goodness morally dangerous in Matthew 6:1 is not the public. It is the purpose clause: "to be seen of them." When being seen becomes the reason for a good deed, the deed reverses direction (from gift to acquisition), submits to the wrong judge (the crowd rather than the Father), collects a payment that closes the account (human praise as the reward, in full), splits the doer into actor and mask, hides its corruption inside the appearance of virtue, and leaves behind a goodness that cannot survive the dark. Visibility is merely the occasion on which this craving is most easily fed, which is why Jesus recommends hiddenness as a remedy. But the verse's own logic, and 5:16 beside it, make the answer to the question plain: the problem is the craving to be seen, and the cure is not to be unseen but to want, above every other gaze, the gaze of the Father who sees in secret.
