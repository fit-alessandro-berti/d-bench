The elder brother’s **deepest** problem is not the anger or the envy, but that he is **fundamentally uncomfortable with mercy itself**—and that discomfort is rooted in a **pride that turns obedience into a transaction**.

Here is how the symptoms layer:

| What you see | What it is | Evidence from the text |
|---|---|---|
| **Anger** | The outward refusal to enter the joy of the father. | “He was angry, and would not go in.” |
| **Envy / Wounded fairness** | His complaint that he “served” and never transgressed, yet got no reward, while the reckless brother gets a feast. | “Lo, these many years do I serve thee… thou never gavest me a kid… but as soon as this thy son was come… thou hast killed for him the fatted calf.” |
| **Pride in obedience** | He measures his relationship to the father by his performance. | “Neither transgressed I at any time thy commandment.” He is not a son asking to be with his father; he is a laborer billing for wages. |

But all of these are expressions of one deeper failure: **he does not understand grace, and therefore he cannot share the father’s heart.**

Notice the father’s response. He does not say, “You have earned everything; here is your wage.” He says: *“Son, thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine.”* The father is inviting him to see that he already lives in abundance—not because he served, but because he is **son**. The elder brother is outside the feast precisely because he thinks he should get a **kid** (a wage, a party he can control with his friends) rather than a **fatted calf** (the father’s unmerited celebration of a lost son).

He is “lost” (the father will say of his brother, “was lost, and is found,” but implicitly the elder is lost too) **not by rebellion, but by self-righteousness**. He wants the father’s *goods* (the inheritance), but he resents the father’s *grace* (the welcome). He wants justice for himself and punishment for his brother; he wants a moral ledger, not a merciful heart.

So to choose among your options:

- **Anger, envy, and wounded fairness** are all real, but they are the *symptoms*.
- **Pride in obedience** is the *mechanism*.
- **Discomfort with mercy itself** is the deepest problem: he cannot rejoice that a sinner is restored because he believes his own “service” makes him more deserving of celebration. He has turned the father’s house into a workplace, and therefore he cannot enter the feast.

The elder brother is lost in the very place he thinks is safest: inside the father’s rules, but outside the father’s joy.
