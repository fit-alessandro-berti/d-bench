The image is sharp because it is physically absurd, and the absurdity is the argument.

A mote is a speck of dust or splinter; a beam is a structural timber. Putting a rafter in an eye is not a mild exaggeration of a real fault. It is a cartoon of blindness: the person offering eye surgery cannot see the patient’s face, let alone the speck. The disproportion does the moral work that a sermon on sincerity would do more slowly. You do not need a definition of hypocrisy once you have pictured the surgeon with a log in his own eye.

The criticism is aimed at the order and the posture of correction, not at correction itself. The last line is the giveaway: “then shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote.” Removal of the brother’s speck is still the goal. What is forbidden is the pretense of clear sight while the larger obstruction remains. Three moves make that precise:

1. **The fault named is not “having a fault.”** Everyone in the picture has something in the eye. The hypocrite’s distinctive act is noticing the smaller fault and offering to extract it while ignoring the larger one that ruins his vision.
2. **“First” is a sequence, not a ban.** Self-examination comes before fraternal correction so that the correction can actually be accurate. A beam does not merely make you guilty; it makes you a bad diagnostician. You mis-see the speck, overstate it, or enjoy the extraction.
3. **Clarity is the condition of help.** Once the beam is out, sight is restored and the mote can be addressed. The passage assumes that moral speech between people remains possible and sometimes necessary. What it will not license is correction used as a way of not looking at oneself.

So the saying is not “judge not, ever.” It is “do not pretend to be the oculist while you are the patient.” Hypocrisy here is not the mere fact of standards; it is standards applied outward as a substitute for applying them inward, with the result that the help offered is blind.
