In Galatians 6:1, the moral posture required for correction is **not moral superiority, but humble, Spirit-directed responsibility**. The “spiritual” ones are those whose lives are governed by the fruit of the Spirit—love, patience, kindness, gentleness, self-control (Gal. 5:22–23). Therefore, when someone is “overtaken in a fault,” the church is not to respond with shock, contempt, or self-righteous judgment, but with a disposition aimed at **restoration**.

The key is that correction here is not primarily about exposing sin, punishing failure, or asserting spiritual authority. It is about **helping a fallen person return to wholeness**. The Greek word translated “restore,” *katartizō*, is used elsewhere of mending fishing nets (Matt. 4:21) or setting a dislocated bone (cf. Heb. 13:21). It suggests putting something back into proper order. The goal is not merely to point out the wrong, but to mend the damaged condition.

So why are **restoration, meekness, and self-suspicion** kept together?

Because each one checks the others and prevents correction from becoming a weapon.

### 1. Restoration sets the aim

Without restoration, correction becomes accusation.

If the purpose of addressing a fault is not the healing of the person, then the process has already departed from the gospel. Paul assumes that the church’s task is not simply to identify wrongdoing, but to recover the wrongdoer. The posture required is therefore constructive: “What would help this person return to fellowship, obedience, and spiritual health?”

Restoration means we do not treat the fallen person as an enemy to be defeated, but as a brother or sister to be helped.

### 2. Meekness sets the manner

If restoration is the aim, meekness is the atmosphere in which restoration can happen.

Meekness is not weakness. Biblical meekness is strength under control. It is power not lashing out in pride, anger, or humiliation. Paul says to restore “in the spirit of meekness.” That phrase tells us that correction cannot be done harshly and still be done truly. A proud rebuke may be accurate in content but ruinous in effect. Meekness preserves the dignity of the one being corrected. It opens the way for repentance rather than resistance.

This is especially important because sin is often met not merely with shame, but with fear of exposure. If correction is done with condescension or severity, the person may feel attacked rather than loved. Meekness creates trust.

### 3. Self-suspicion protects the corrector

Paul then adds: “considering thyself, lest thou also be tempted.”

This is the crucial safeguard. The person correcting another must not imagine that he or she is immune to the same kind of failure. The moral posture required here is not “I am the one who has fallen,” but “I too am fallen.” Paul warns the spiritual not to see themselves as beyond temptation. The one who corrects must look with honesty at his or her own vulnerability.

That self-suspicion prevents pride. It stops correction from becoming a platform for self-exaltation. It keeps the corrector from thinking, “I would never do that,” or “I am spiritually secure and therefore qualified to judge.” Paul says: consider yourself. You too could be tempted. You too could be overtaken.

Without this self-awareness, meekness fades quickly. And without meekness, restoration becomes impossible.

### Why they belong together

Paul binds these three realities together because the failure of any one corrupts the whole act of correction.

- **Restoration without meekness** becomes harsh and humiliating.
- **Meekness without self-suspicion** can still be self-righteous: “I am gentle, but I am above this.”
- **Self-suspicion without restoration** can devolve into sentimental tolerance: “I may fail too, so why address your failure?”

But together they form a complete moral posture:

> We correct because we want to restore; we restore gently; and we stay gentle because we remember our own vulnerability.

In other words, the person correcting another must carry three convictions at once:

1. **The other person matters enough to be restored.**
2. **My manner matters because pride will wound rather than heal.**
3. **My own heart matters because I am not safe from the same temptations.**

Galatians 6:1 therefore presents correction as an act of love under the Spirit, not an exercise of moral power. The right posture is not “I stand over you,” but “I stand beside you, in humility, helping you back up, while remembering that I too could fall.”
