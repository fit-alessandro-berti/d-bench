# Enemy-Love: Sentimental, Strategic, or Moral Strength?

## The Instinctive Rejection

When Jesus commands enemy-love, the most common modern reaction is not outright dismissal but a quiet assumption that he *couldn't really mean it literally*. Critics tend to file it into one of three reductive categories:

---

## 1. The Charge of Sentimentality

**The objection:** Enemy-love is naïve, a pious wish dressed up as a command.

This charge has some surface plausibility — especially when "love your enemies" is heard as mere *warm feeling* toward those who harm you. Taken this way, it would indeed be both impossible and arguably *irresponsible*. No one can command an emotional response the way one commands a moral action.

**But the text resists this reading.** Notice the four concrete, action-based verbs:

| Command | Nature of Action |
|---|---|
| **Love** your enemies | Sustained posture of will |
| **Bless** them that curse you | Active words of goodwill |
| **Do good** to them that hate you | Tangible benefaction |
| **Pray** for them who persecute you | Intercession before God |

These are not feelings; they are **deliberate choices**. Jesus is not asking you to *feel fondly* for someone who betrayed you. He is asking you to *act* in a way that refuses to let their hostility define the terms of your engagement. This distinction is crucial — it moves the command out of the category of sentimentality entirely.

---

## 2. The Charge of Strategy

**The objection:** Enemy-love is just a smart long game — destroy your enemy's hostility by not reciprocating it. It's leverage disguised as virtue.

There is a grain of truth here. Psychological research on conflict de-escalation, and examples like Gandhian satyagraha or King's nonviolent resistance, do show that refusing to meet hatred with hatred can often work tactically. One could argue that enemy-love is simply *enlightened self-interest*.

**But the text again resists this reduction.** The stated purpose is not strategic advantage:

> *"That ye may be the children of your Father which is in heaven"*

The reason given is **ontological identity**, not tactical gain. You love your enemies not because it *works* but because it is **what God himself does**. The Creator sends rain and sunlight indiscriminately — on the just and the unjust — not as a strategy to win anyone over, but because his generosity is simply *his nature*. The command, then, is participation in the **character of God**, not a maneuver.

If enemy-love were merely strategic, the moment it stopped being effective one would be justified in abandoning it. Jesus grounds it in something that has nothing to do with whether it works.

---

## 3. The Deeper Reading: Moral Strength

**This is where the text most powerfully lands.** Enemy-love is neither sentiment nor strategy but arguably the **highest expression of moral strength** — for several reasons:

### a. It requires the greatest self-command
It is far easier to hit back, to curse in return, to withhold good. Enemy-love demands that a person **override one of the deepest reflexes in human nature**: retaliation. This is not weakness dressed as piety — it is *discipline over primal instinct*.

### b. It breaks the cycle of reactive identity
Without enemy-love, your moral identity is always **determined by your enemy's behavior**. They insult you → you insult back. They withdraw → you withdraw. In this model, the aggressor controls the moral landscape. Jesus' command severs this chain: **you choose your response independent of their provocation**. This is a radical reassertion of moral autonomy.

### c. It refuses to instrumentalize persons
Neither sentimentality nor strategy fully captures this. Sentimentality still centers *your* emotional comfort. Strategy still centers *your* advantage. Enemy-love, as Jesus frames it, treats the enemy as a **moral subject worthy of genuine regard** — not as an obstacle to be overcome or a heart to be melted, but as a fellow creature under the same rain and sun.

### d. It mirrors divine vulnerability
The image of God sending rain on the unjust carries a profound implication: **God himself is perpetually "wasted" on people who never respond**. His generosity is not conditional, not protected, not calculated. Enemy-love, then, mirrors not a human strategy but a **divine self-emptying** — a willingness to remain open even at the cost of being used.

---

## Why It Feels So Hard to Take Seriously

The real reason enemy-love sounds absurd to modern ears is **cultural and psychological, not logical**:

- We live in a **retaliatory moral economy** — justice = proportionate punishment
- We confuse **love with approval** — loving an enemy sounds like *endorsing* what they do
- We assume **emotional authenticity is automatic** — we can't imagine willing love for someone we resent
- We've absorbed the idea that **moral seriousness requires hardness** — compassion feels soft

Jesus' command systematically dismantles each of these assumptions.

---

## Conclusion

Enemy-love is:

- ❌ **Not sentimental** — it is action-based, demanding, and costly
- ❌ **Not merely strategic** — its ground is God's character, not its effectiveness
- ✅ **A deeper form of moral strength** — because it requires self-command over instinct, severs reactive identity, treats the enemy as a genuine person, and mirrors the vulnerability of God himself

The command is not easy. But its difficulty is precisely the point: **it names the hardest moral thing a human being can be asked to do**, and Jesus does not soften it. He simply grounds it in the nature of a Father who lets the sun rise on those who would rather he didn't.
