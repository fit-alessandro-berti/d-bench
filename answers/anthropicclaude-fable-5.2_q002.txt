David does ask for pardon. Earlier in the psalm he prays, "blot out my transgressions" and "wash me throughly from mine iniquity." But he doesn't stop there, and I think the reason is that pardon and a clean heart address different problems.

**Pardon deals with what I did; a clean heart deals with what I am.**
Forgiveness clears the record, but it leaves the person who made the record. A pardoned David with the same heart is the same man who will be back on the rooftop. He sees that adultery and murder were not accidents but came from something in him (v. 5–6: "thou desirest truth in the inward parts"). He wants the source cleansed, not just the stain removed.

**"Create" admits he cannot fix himself.**
The Hebrew verb is *bara*, the word used in Genesis 1, which in Scripture has only God as its subject. David isn't asking for help in turning over a new leaf. He is asking for something to be made that isn't there.

**Wanting escape from guilt is self-directed; wanting change is God-directed.**
A person can want relief from guilt for self-interested reasons: fear of consequences, damaged reputation, the discomfort of a bad conscience. Saul is the contrast. When confronted, he said, "I have sinned: yet honour me now... before the elders" (1 Sam. 15:30). He wanted the fallout managed. David says, "Against thee, thee only, have I sinned." He has come to hate the sin itself, not just its cost. Paul later calls this the difference between "worldly sorrow" and "godly sorrow" (2 Cor. 7:10).

One test of the difference is whether you would still want to change if you were guaranteed no consequences. If not, you want escape. If so, you want a clean heart.

**What David fears losing is presence, not comfort.**
His great dread in verse 11 is not punishment but distance: "Cast me not away from thy presence." What he wants back is "the joy of thy salvation." Someone who only wants pardon treats God as a judge to get past. Someone who wants a clean heart treats God as the one he wants to be near, and knows that an unchanged heart will keep pulling him away.

**He wants to want what is right.**
The "free spirit" of verse 12 can be rendered "willing spirit." David isn't asking only for restraint. He asks that his desires be changed, so that obedience flows from desire rather than from fear.

The two requests aren't rivals. Pardon comes first, and fear of consequences is often where repentance begins. But the psalm suggests that repentance isn't complete until "get me out of this" becomes "make me different."
