### 1. The Kind of Power Being Praised

The power praised in this passage—often called *kenotic* or "self-emptying" power—is the power of **sovereign restraint and absolute security**. 

In standard human economies, power is measured by leverage, invulnerability, and the capacity to compel others. It is anxious by nature; because it can be lost, it must be guarded, performed, and asserted ("reputation"). 

The hymn in Philippians praises the exact inverse: a greatness so secure in its own reality that it has nothing to prove and nothing to hoard. When the text says Christ "thought it not robbery to be equal with God" (often translated from the Greek *harpagmos* as "not something to be grasped or exploited"), it redefines divine status not as a privilege to extract tribute, but as an inexhaustible fullness free to give itself away. 

This is **downwardly mobile power**. It demonstrates its strength not by how much weight it can crush, but by how much humiliation and suffering it can absorb without ceasing to be love. In the ancient Roman world, crucifixion was the ultimate state-sponsored erasure of a person’s dignity—a death reserved for rebels and slaves to prove their total powerlessness. By placing God at the bottom of that gallows voluntarily ("obedient unto death"), the passage claims that true omnipotence is the freedom to become utterly vulnerable.

### 2. Why Greatness Joined to Humility Is More Unsettling Than Domination

At first glance, open domination seems far worse than humility. Yet psychologically and spiritually, a tyrant is much easier to make sense of than a self-emptying God. Humility joined to supreme greatness is deeply unsettling for several reasons:

* **Domination plays by rules we already understand and secretly endorse.** 
  Brute force is terrifying, yet completely familiar. When confronted by a dominating power, our worldview remains intact: *might makes right, the strong rule the weak.* Even if we are losing that game, we usually agree with its premise—we just wish *we* held the whip instead. Humble greatness doesn't just defeat us within the game of status; it flips the board over and reveals the entire game to be a delusion.

* **Domination leaves our moral pride intact; humble greatness exposes our pettiness.**
  When someone dominates you, you can submit externally while remaining defiant internally. You can hate the tyrant, nurse your grievance, and feel morally superior to the one crushing you. Your ego survives. But when the highest imaginable authority relinquishes all status to become a servant, resentment loses its footing. If the Creator of the universe makes himself "of no reputation," what happens to all our anxious grasping after prestige, credit, and superiority? Our insistence on our own status suddenly looks not heroic, but absurd.

* **Force demands compliance; self-emptying love demands transformation.**
  Open domination only asks you to bend your knee out of self-preservation. Greatness clothed in humility asks for something much scarier: the surrender of the ego out of love. Notice how the passage begins: *"Let this mind be in you..."* You cannot merely admire this kind of power from a safe distance without realizing it is an indictment of how you live. To accept it means giving up the right to dominate others in your own small spheres of influence.

* **It strips away our defenses.**
  We build armor—cynicism, pride, self-sufficiency—designed to withstand attacks, coercion, and judgment. Those defenses work well against aggression. They are completely useless against someone who holds all the power, refuses to use it against us, and instead steps into our vulnerability. As Kierkegaard observed in his parable of the King and the Maiden, a king who appears in dazzling glory crushes the peasant girl's freedom, making genuine love impossible; only by actually becoming a peasant—taking the risk of being rejected or overlooked—does he treat her as a free moral agent. That kind of terrifying gentleness leaves us nowhere to hide.
