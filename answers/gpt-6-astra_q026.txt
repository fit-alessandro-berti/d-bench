Matthew 12:34–35 presents speech as the outward expression of an inward moral storehouse. The “heart” here is not merely the seat of emotion; in biblical usage, it encompasses thought, desire, intention, and will. Its “treasure” is what a person holds and cultivates within—and therefore has available to bring forth.

Two images explain the connection:

- **“Out of the abundance of the heart”**: speech overflows from what fills a person inwardly.
- **“Out of the … treasure of the heart”**: speech draws upon stored moral resources, whether good or evil.

The point is not simply that people occasionally say what they secretly think. It is that their words have roots in the kind of inward life they cultivate.

So **words are symptoms, choices, and revelations—all three**, though each term highlights something different:

**1. Symptoms:** Words indicate an underlying moral condition. In the preceding verse, Jesus connects a tree’s fruit with the tree’s character. Speech likewise does not arise in isolation; habitual malice, deceit, or generosity in speech points toward something deeper.

**2. Choices:** This is not a picture of involuntary discharge that excuses the speaker. Immediately afterward, Jesus says people will give account for their words and be judged by them (Matthew 12:36–37). Speech is morally accountable action: people do things with words, including slandering, deceiving, blessing, and telling the truth.

**3. Revelations:** Words make the otherwise hidden heart perceptible. In context, the Pharisees have attributed Jesus’ healing work to demonic power (12:24). Jesus treats that accusation as more than a mistaken description: it exposes a moral disposition willing to call good evil.

The question “How can ye, being evil, speak good things?” therefore presses the inconsistency of expecting genuinely good fruit from a corrupt source. It is not primarily a claim that an evil person can never utter a pleasant or factually correct sentence.

**The central connection is that speech both expresses character and exercises it.** Words reveal what has been stored within, while remaining acts for which the speaker is responsible. The passage consequently calls for more than verbal polish: good speech requires the renewal of the heart from which it comes.
