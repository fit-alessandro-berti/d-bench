The context sharpens the question. Isaiah 1 is a covenant lawsuit: heaven and earth are summoned as witnesses (v. 2), and the charge is not irreligion but religion severed from justice. God is "full" of their sacrifices, weary of their feasts, and will not hear their prayers—"your hands are full of blood" (v. 15). The hands raised in worship are the hands doing violence. So when v. 16 commands "Wash you, make you clean," the irony is deliberate: the cleansing God demands is not cultic (they have plenty of cult) but moral. And moral purification turns out to have two distinct movements: removal, then construction.

Count the imperatives. Four concern removal—wash, make clean, put away, cease. Five concern construction—learn, seek, relieve, judge, plead. The negative gets less breath than the positive, and the positive gets the last word. That proportion is itself an argument: the text refuses to let "cease to do evil" be the terminus, because a merely subtractive morality leaves a vacuum, and vacuums do not stay empty. Restraint empties the hands; it does not tell them what to hold.

Then comes the surprising verb: *learn*. The Hebrew *limdu* is the root of "disciple" and "Talmud"—the vocabulary of apprenticeship, not resolution. You can cease in a moment; you cannot become just in a moment. Evil is abandoned; goodness is acquired, the way a craft is acquired, through instruction and repetition. The word choice quietly concedes that Judah has lost the skill. Jeremiah makes the diagnosis explicit in nearly identical terms: "They are wise to do evil, but to do good they have no knowledge" (Jer. 4:22). And it answers the chapter's opening charge, where Israel, unlike the ox that knows its owner, "doth not know" (v. 3). The book opens in lost knowledge; here is the curriculum.

Notice what the learning consists of. "Doing well" is not defined as intensified devotion or private virtue but as concrete, almost forensic competencies. *Mishpat*—"judgment"—is the covenant word for public justice. To "judge the fatherless" is to secure a fair hearing for those with no patron to speak for them; to "plead for" the widow (*rib*) is to take up her case in court. Orphans and widows are the paradigmatic defenseless—no legal leverage, no economic safety net, no way to reciprocate. Goodness, in Isaiah's definition, is skilled advocacy on behalf of people who cannot pay you back. It has a grammar, and it must be learned precisely because it is a practice.

So the movement from "cease" to "learn" answers your second question almost by itself. If goodness were mere restraint, the second half of the passage would be unnecessary—having told them to stop, God could simply say *and be done*. Instead, evil is treated as a habit to break and good as a discipline to acquire—an asymmetry the tradition later formalized as the difference between continence and virtue: the continent person holds back from wrong; the virtuous person has been so formed that justice is second nature. Restraint is real and necessary—hands must be emptied of blood before they can be put to work—but it is the doorway, not the house.

There is a final grace note. The same verb, *lamad*, returns in Isaiah's vision of the redeemed future: "neither shall they learn war any more" (2:4). Learning to do good and unlearning violence are, in this book, the same education conducted from both ends. Even Zion's restoration is keyed to it—"Zion shall be redeemed with judgment, and her converts with righteousness" (1:27). Goodness is not the absence of evil but its replacement: a practiced love, exercised where it costs something, on behalf of those who can offer nothing in return.
