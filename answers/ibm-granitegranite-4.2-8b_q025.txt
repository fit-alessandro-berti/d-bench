The morally dangerous element in Matthew 6:1 is **the craving to be seen** (i.e., the *motivation* of seeking human approval), **not visibility itself**. The verse does not condemn *all* public good deeds per se; it specifically targets good deeds done *“before men, to be seen of them.”* The danger lies in the *purpose* or *intention* behind the act—doing it primarily to gain the observers’ approval.

Here’s a precise, verse-grounded explanation:

1. **The exact wording**:  
   > “Take heed that ye do not your alms **before men, to be seen of them**…”  
   The phrase “to be seen of them” directly identifies the motive: the act (e.g., giving alms) is performed *in order that men see it*. Visibility alone (e.g., giving alms openly in a quiet, natural way without seeking praise) is not named as sinful. The trigger is *“to be seen of them”*—i.e., *seeking* to be seen *by others* for the sake of that recognition.

2. **Why the craving/motive is the moral danger**:  
   - Jesus contrasts this with private, unseen good deeds (e.g., giving to the poor, praying, fasting *in secret*—v. 6, 8). Doing good privately aligns with God’s character (who acts without need for applause) and receives God’s reward.  
   - The phrase “otherwise ye have no reward of your Father which is in heaven” indicates the *consequence*: if the primary motive is “to be seen,” God’s intended reward (divine recognition/blessing) is not received. The danger isn’t that public acts are inherently wrong; it’s that *doing them for the sake of human applause* severs the connection to God’s motive and reward.  
   - The phrase “take heed” (Greek: *ōreueσθε*) means “pay careful attention,” implying a warning against a habitual or conscious desire to impress others. It’s about the *intention* ("to be seen"), not merely the outward circumstance (e.g., giving alms in public could happen accidentally; doing it *intentionally* to be seen is the target).

3. **Why “visibility itself” is *not* the core problem**:  
   - If visibility *alone* were dangerous, the verse would say something like “Do not give alms publicly” or “Avoid being seen doing good.” But it doesn’t. It says “do not your alms *before men, to be seen of them*.” The qualifier “to be seen of them” is essential. One could give alms publicly in a quiet, unselfconscious way (e.g., helping someone on the street without announcing it), which would not violate “to be seen of them” if no desire for applause accompanies it. The sin is the *craving/purpose* (“to be seen”), not the public setting per se.

4. **Practical clarity**:  
   > ✅ *Safe (morally not dangerous)*: Give alms quietly to a neighbor in need, not thinking about who might see it or wanting praise. God sees and rewards it.  
   > ❌ *Dangerous (morally problematic per v.1)*: Give alms publicly *with the clear intention* “so that people see what I’m doing and admire me” (e.g., announcing it, doing it on display to get compliments or status). In this case, the reward from God (“no reward of your Father”) is effectively forfeited per Jesus’ statement.

**In short: The morally dangerous element is the *craving to be seen*—the conscious intention to perform good deeds “to be seen of men.” Visibility alone is not condemned; it’s the *motivation* of seeking human approval that disrupts the relationship with God’s reward.** The verse warns against the habitual desire to impress others with one’s goodness, not against doing good things publicly in a neutral or private-hearted way.

> *Thus, answer succinctly:* **The problem is the craving to be seen—the motive “to be seen of them.” Visibility itself is not intrinsically dangerous; it becomes morally dangerous only when the act is done *with the purpose* of gaining human approval.*
