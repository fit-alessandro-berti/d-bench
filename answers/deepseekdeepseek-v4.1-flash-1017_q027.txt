The parable is governed by Luke’s own summary in v. 9: the Pharisee is one who “trusted in himself that he was righteous, and despised others.” So the condemnation is not simply one item from your list; it is a whole spiritual orientation with several layers.

**What condemns him is not, narrowly, “false doctrine.”**  
Fasting, tithing, avoiding extortion, injustice, and adultery are not condemned as evil. Jesus does not say the Pharisee was secretly an adulterer or thief. His words may be externally accurate. What is wrong is his **practical theology of self-justification**: he relates to God as though his standing were based on his own performance and superiority. In that broader sense, his doctrine of justification is false, but the parable’s emphasis is on the heart.

**Pride is the root.**  
He “trusted in himself that he was righteous.” His prayer is addressed with God’s name in it, but the center of gravity is “I”: *I am not like others; I fast; I give tithes.* His “thanksgiving” is not gratitude for mercy but self-congratulation with God as audience.

**Comparison is the mechanism.**  
He defines himself by contrast: “not as other men are… or even as this publican.” He cannot say “I am righteous” without constructing an inferior. The tax collector becomes a prop in his self-congratulation.

**Contempt is the fruit and proof.**  
Luke explicitly says he “despised others.” His contempt is not an incidental bad attitude; it reveals that his righteousness has become a system of ranking rather than love.

**Self-display is the mode.**  
He uses virtue as evidence in his own favor. His good deeds are real, but they are turned into a platform for self-justification. The problem is not virtue; it is virtue turned into self-righteousness, comparison, and contempt.

The tax collector’s posture is treated as spiritually truer not because bowing, beating the breast, or feeling bad earns salvation, but because his posture and prayer correspond to the truth. He stands afar off, will not lift his eyes, beats his breast, and says, **“God, be merciful to me, a sinner.”** He offers no resume, no comparison, no claim. He simply tells the truth about himself and casts himself on God’s mercy. The Greek word behind “be merciful” even carries an atonement/propitiation nuance: he is asking God to be gracious toward him on the basis of mercy, not merit.

So the Pharisee is condemned for **self-righteous self-trust**, expressed through **pride, comparison, contempt, and religious self-display**. The tax collector “went down to his house justified” not because low posture is a new work, but because he asked for mercy and had nothing to plead but grace. The parable does not condemn good works; it condemns good works used as a righteousness of one’s own and as grounds for despising others.
