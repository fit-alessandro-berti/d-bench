Luke 6:37 is one of the most frequently quoted verses in the Bible—and one of the most frequently flattened. Read in isolation, "Judge not" is often treated as a mandate for moral relativism, as if Jesus were saying, *Don’t evaluate anything, don’t call anything wrong, and mind your own business.*

Yet both the wording of the verse itself and the paragraphs immediately surrounding it make that reading impossible. Luke 6:37 does not abolish moral judgment; it confronts a specific pathology of the human heart.

---

### Why This Isn’t Saying Moral Judgments Never Matter

**1. Forgiveness presupposes moral judgment.**
Notice how the verse ends: *"forgive, and ye shall be forgiven."* Forgiveness is impossible without first making a moral judgment. To forgive someone is to look clearly at an action, name it as genuinely wrong, and choose to release the debt rather than exact vengeance. If moral judgments didn’t matter, forgiveness wouldn’t be necessary—you would simply shrug and say, "No harm done." Grace only makes sense in a world where right and wrong are real.

**2. The immediate context demands moral evaluation.**
Just a few lines after "Judge not," Jesus gives two metaphors that require careful moral assessment:
* **The speck and the plank (Luke 6:41–42):** Jesus does not say, *Ignore the speck in your neighbor’s eye because specks don't matter.* He says to deal with the plank in your own eye *first*, "and then you will see clearly to remove the speck." Moral clarity is the goal, not moral blindness.
* **The tree and its fruit (Luke 6:43–45):** Jesus teaches that "each tree is recognized by its own fruit." Recognizing good fruit from bad fruit is, by definition, an act of moral judgment.

**3. The structure of the verse defines what kind of "judging" is meant.**
Luke 6:37 uses Hebrew poetic parallelism—pairing related ideas to sharpen their meaning. "Judge not" is immediately clarified by "condemn not," and both are contrasted with "forgive" (literally, *release* or *pardon*). The target isn't the faculty of moral reasoning; it is the gavel of final condemnation.

---

### Discernment vs. The Appetite to Condemn

If Luke 6:37 isn't banning moral clarity, it is drawing a sharp line between **discernment** and **the appetite to condemn**. On the surface, they can look similar—both involve naming something as wrong. Underneath, they are opposites.

#### 1. The Target: Actions vs. Souls
* **Discernment** evaluates actions, patterns, ideas, and consequences. It says, *"What you did was dishonest and caused harm,"* or *"This person is not trustworthy right now, so I need boundaries."*
* **Condemnation** collapses a person into their worst moment and stamps a final verdict on their worth. It moves from *"You lied"* to *"You are a liar—irredeemable, beneath me, finished."* It assumes the seat of God by claiming total knowledge of another person's motives and ultimate value.

#### 2. The Posture: Humility vs. Superiority
* **Discernment** begins with self-awareness. Because it remembers the "plank in its own eye," it approaches another person’s failure with soberness. It recognizes a shared human frailty: *I am capable of blindness and harm, too.*
* **The appetite to condemn** feeds on moral contrast. Condemnation is deeply intoxicating because putting someone else on trial automatically elevates the judge. It turns another person’s sin into a pedestal for our own righteousness.

#### 3. The Emotional Flavor: Grief vs. Thrill
* **Discernment** is rarely enjoyable when it confronts evil. Naming a friend's betrayal or a community's injustice brings grief, heaviness, or solemn resolve.
* **The appetite to condemn** carries a subtle—or not-so-subtle—pleasure. You can feel it in gossip, outrage culture, and self-righteousness: a quiet thrill when someone stumbles, a hunger to catch people out, a reluctance to see them repent because their repentance would rob us of our indignation.

#### 4. The Goal: Restoration and Protection vs. Exile
* **Discernment** serves love and truth. It names harm in order to protect the vulnerable, establish honest boundaries, or—where possible—invite repair and healing. Even when it must firmly oppose someone, it leaves the door open to the possibility of change.
* **Condemnation** wants finality. Its goal isn't to heal the wound or mend the person, but to pronounce a sentence and close the book.

---

### The Logic of the Warning

Luke 6:37 ends each clause with a mirror: *"and ye shall not be judged... and ye shall not be condemned... and ye shall be forgiven."* 

This isn't just a threat of cosmic payback; it is a description of the moral atmosphere we create. When we cultivate an appetite to condemn, we train ourselves to live in a merciless world—one where every flaw is fatal, every mistake defines us, and grace has no room to breathe. Inevitably, the harsh standard we use to measure others becomes the very standard under which we suffocate (Luke 6:38).

True moral seriousness doesn't require an appetite to condemn. In fact, condemnation undermines moral seriousness by turning ethics into a status game. Discernment, by contrast, allows us to hold two things at once without flinching: an unflinching honesty about what is wrong, and an open-handed willingness to forgive.
