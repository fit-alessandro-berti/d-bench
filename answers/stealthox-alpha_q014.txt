## Short answer

The verse is often quoted as a piece of diagnosis — "your treasure *reveals* your heart" — but in its context it functions primarily as promise and motive: the heart *follows* the treasure. Both readings are true, but the grammar and rhetoric tilt decisively toward training.

## Why the text leans toward "trains"

**1. The tenses point forward.** In v. 21 the treasure *is* (ἐστιν, present), but the heart *will be* (ἔσται, future): "where your treasure is, *there will* your heart be also." The image is of a heart in transit — lagging behind, then arriving. Treasure is treated as settled fact; the heart as migratory, moving to catch up with what has been invested.

**2. The γάρ ("for") grounds commands, not observations.** Verses 19–20 are imperatives: stop storing earthward, start storing heavenward. Verse 21 supplies the reason such relocation is possible and effective — redirect the investment, and the affection will migrate after it. If treasure merely revealed a pre-existing heart, the logic would run backwards: you would have to renovate the heart *before* the treasure could move. Instead, Jesus treats treasure-relocation as the *means* of heart-relocation.

**3. The verb matters.** Θησαυρίζειν ("store up, treasure up") in vv. 19–20 names an ongoing practice, not a static snapshot of holdings. The issue is the *activity* of treasuring.

Revelation isn't absent, though. The saying still exposes the fiction of divided service (v. 24): whatever we profess, our treasury shows our actual liturgy. It diagnoses the present precisely in order to motivate redirection.

## The chain: possession → attention → love

- **To possess is to have sunk yourself into a thing** — cost, labor, identity. Ownership means part of you is bound up in the object.
- **Possession conscripts attention.** Notice Jesus' three hazards: moth, rust, thief. These are precisely the threats that demand vigilance. Earthly treasure doesn't merely decay; it recruits the heart as night-watchman. (This is the root of the anxiety Jesus addresses in 6:25–34.)
- **Repeated attention ripens into attachment.** We come to love what we habitually behold and guard. The heart settles where it keeps returning.

So the heart ends up where the treasure is because treasure is where the self has been spent — and must keep being spent.

The positive mirror completes the logic: heavenly treasure runs the same engine in reverse. Almsgiving and kingdom expenditure bind your interests to God's, so the heart follows Godward. Luke's parallel makes the mechanism explicit: "Sell… and give alms… a treasure in the heavens that faileth not… For where your treasure is…" (Luke 12:33–34). Chrysostom pressed exactly this point: spend on the poor, and your desire goes where your money went.

## One caveat

This is not a purchase mechanism or salvation-by-donation. It is a moral psychology of desire: loves are *trained* through practices of investment. Jesus treats the heart not as fixed bedrock but as a creature that follows what it feeds on — which is why the command reads as practical counsel rather than impossible idealism. If you want your heart in heaven, the instruction is: put your treasure there first, and the heart will arrive.
