The movement from **“cease to do evil” to “learn to do well”** suggests that goodness is more than the absence of wrongdoing. Stopping harm is necessary, but it is not the whole of repentance. There is also a good to be learned and actively done.

**“Cease” names a break with destructive habits; “learn” names the formation of new ones.** The latter implies that goodness requires instruction, attention, practice, and correction. We do not become just simply by deciding not to be cruel. We must learn to recognize another person’s need, understand our responsibilities, and act faithfully.

The commands that follow make this concrete:

- **“Seek judgment”**: pursue justice, not merely personal innocence.
- **“Relieve the oppressed”**: intervene where people are being harmed.
- **“Judge the fatherless”**: secure justice for those without protection.
- **“Plead for the widow”**: advocate for someone whose voice may otherwise go unheard.

These are outward-facing acts. They ask not only, *“What wrong must I stop doing?”* but also, *“Whose suffering must I notice, and what good am I responsible to do?”*

In the surrounding passage, Isaiah rebukes religious observance disconnected from justice. Washing and becoming clean therefore cannot remain merely ritual gestures: they must take shape in changed conduct toward others.

So yes: the passage presents goodness as **a practiced form of love and justice**, not mere restraint. Restraint prevents us from injuring our neighbor; learned goodness teaches us to defend, restore, and care for that neighbor. Repentance is both turning **away from evil** and turning **toward the good of others**.
