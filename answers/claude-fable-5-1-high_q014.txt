Short answer: the saying does both, but its weight falls on training. Verse 21 is usually quoted as a diagnostic ("show me your bank statement and I'll show you your heart"), and it can be used that way, but the sentence itself runs in the other direction. Treasure is the antecedent; the heart is what follows. "Where your treasure is, there will your heart be also" — the heart is the dependent variable. And the verse is offered as the reason (gar, "for") for two commands about what to do with your hands, not for an exhortation about what to feel. Jesus does not say, "Examine your heart and then store accordingly." He says, "Store here rather than there, because your heart will end up wherever you store." That is the logic of formation, not of disclosure.

1. What the grammar and structure say

The passage has three parts: a prohibition (do not treasure up treasures on earth), a command (treasure up treasures in heaven), and a ground for both (for where your treasure is, there your heart will be). Notice several things.

First, the verb is active and iterative: thesaurizete, "keep storing up." Treasure is not a static possession here but an ongoing practice of accumulation. What is being addressed is a habit, and habits are the things that form us.

Second, the ground clause is in the future tense: estai, "will be." Not "there your heart is," as a mirror, but "there your heart will be," as a consequence. The heart goes to the treasure the way a compass needle goes to iron. If the point were purely revelatory, the present tense would have done the job.

Third, the whole thing is prescription. You cannot command a heart directly; nobody ever changed what they loved by being told to love something else. What you can command is a hand — what it grasps, what it releases, where it puts things. Jesus addresses the one point in the chain that is under direct volitional control and promises that the rest of the person will be dragged along behind it. That is why the passage is practical rather than merely searching: it gives a lever. The Hebrew sense of "heart" (leb, kardia) matters here too. It is not primarily the seat of feeling but the seat of thought, intention, and will — the center of the person. To say the heart follows treasure is to say the whole orientation of a life follows what it accumulates.

2. Why the revelatory reading is still true

None of this cancels the diagnostic use. Precisely because treasure forms the heart, treasure also reliably indicates it. If your heart has been following your treasure for years, then the location of your treasure is the best evidence of where your heart now sits. The saying can be read backwards as a test exactly because it is true forwards as a law. But the order matters for what you do about the result. If treasure merely revealed the heart, the remedy for a misplaced heart would be interior — repent, feel differently, reorder your affections by introspection. Because treasure trains the heart, the remedy is exterior and concrete: move the treasure, and let the heart be re-trained by the new practice. The Sermon consistently works this way — it treats external acts (almsgiving, prayer, fasting, oaths, retaliation) as the places where the inner life is actually formed or deformed.

3. How possession, attention, and love become one thing

The tying together happens across 6:19–24, which is a single argument in three images: treasure (19–21), the eye (22–23), and the two masters (24). Each image adds a link.

Possession (19–21). To store up treasure is to bind yourself to an object. The two lists of threats explain why this binding is dangerous when the object is earthly. Moth and "eating" (brosis — rust, corrosion, vermin, decay) are the enemies from within: the treasure fails by its own nature. Thieves who "dig through" (the mud-brick walls of a Palestinian house) are the enemy from without: the treasure fails by violation. So earthly treasure is doubly insecure, and a heart bound to it inherits that insecurity. This is why the passage leads directly into "do not be anxious" (6:25–34). Anxiety is what a heart feels when it is fastened to something perishable. Possession, in other words, is never one-directional. You hold the treasure, and the treasure holds you; it sets the terms of your fear.

Attention (22–23). The eye is "the lamp of the body." In Jewish idiom a "good/single eye" is a generous eye and an "evil eye" is a grudging, envious one, so the image is not only about perception but about how you look at goods — as things to be given or as things to be clutched. Either way the point is that what you have stored determines where you look. You watch over what you have invested in. You check on it, calculate around it, notice threats to it. The treasure recruits the gaze, and the gaze floods the whole person with either light or darkness. Attention is the medium through which possession reaches the heart.

Love (24). The third image names the endpoint: "he will hate the one and love the other." Mammon is personified because accumulated wealth behaves like a master — it issues demands, requires service, and claims loyalty. What began as a thing you own becomes something you belong to. Love here is not sentiment but allegiance, the heart's settled orientation. The sequence, then, is: what you gather draws your attention; what holds your attention shapes your heart; where your heart is, there you love and there you serve. Possession trains attention, attention trains love, and love is what "heart" finally means.

4. Treasure in heaven is not the absence of treasure

It is important that Jesus does not tell his hearers to have no treasure or to detach the heart from everything, in the manner of a Stoic. The heart will attach; it cannot be neutral. The question is only what it attaches to. So he does not abolish the mechanism; he redirects it. "Treasure in heaven" is a well-known Jewish expression (Tobit 4:8–9; Sirach 29:10–12) for almsgiving and acts of mercy done before God — exactly the practices Matthew 6:1–18 has just described, done "in secret" with the Father as the one who rewards. To lay up treasure in heaven is, concretely, to give earthly treasure away. This is the hinge of the whole passage: the act of releasing possession is itself the act of storing, and the heart follows the new storage place. Giving is not merely the sign of a heart already in heaven; it is the means by which a heart gets there.

5. So which is it?

The text says that treasure trains the heart, and that because it does, treasure also reveals the heart. The revelatory reading is a true corollary; the formative reading is the thing being taught. And the practical wisdom of the passage lies in the direction it chooses. If you wait to give until your heart is in the right place, you will wait forever, because the heart is being pulled the whole time by wherever the treasure already sits. Move the treasure first. The heart, Jesus promises, will follow — and the whole self with it: what you hold becomes what you watch, what you watch becomes what you love, and what you love becomes what you serve.
