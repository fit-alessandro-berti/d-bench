The problem in Matthew 6:1 is **the craving to be seen**, not visibility itself. 

Public goodness becomes morally dangerous when human applause shifts from being an accidental byproduct of doing good to being the actual reason for doing it.

### 1. Visibility vs. Motive (Matthew 5:16 vs. 6:1)
The clearest proof that visibility itself is not the problem comes from just one chapter earlier in the same sermon. In **Matthew 5:16**, Jesus commands the exact opposite if taken purely on the surface:
> *“Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father which is in heaven.”*

The difference between the two verses lies entirely in the **purpose**:
* **Matthew 5:16:** Others see your good works, and the result is that they glorify **God**.
* **Matthew 6:1:** You do your alms *"to be seen of them,"* meaning the goal is that they admire **you**.

In the original Greek of Matthew 6:1, the phrase *"to be seen of them"* (*pros to theathenai autois*) comes from a root word related to "theater." It carries the sense of putting on a spectacle or performing for an audience. The danger isn't that people happen to see you being generous; it's that you are staging your generosity for an audience.

### 2. Why Public Goodness Is Morally Dangerous
In the verses that immediately follow (Matthew 6:2–4), Jesus explains *why* performing righteousness for human eyes is so spiritually hazardous:

* **It turns virtue into egoism:** Almsgiving is supposed to be an act of love toward someone in need and obedience to God. When done *"to be seen,"* the person in need is reduced to a prop in a performance meant to boost the giver’s reputation.
* **It creates a spiritual dead end ("paid in full"):** When Jesus warns that such people *"have no reward of your Father,"* he follows up in verse 2 by saying of hypocrites that *"they have their reward."* In the Greek, that phrase was a commercial term for a receipt stamped "paid in full." If your goal is human admiration, and you get it, the transaction is complete. You got what you worked for—praise from people—which leaves nothing coming from God.
* **It fosters hypocrisy:** If your motivation depends on being watched, your goodness will inevitably become selective. You will do the right thing when there is an audience and neglect it when no one is looking, hollowing out your actual character.

In short, public goodness is dangerous because it subtly replaces God with the crowd as the ultimate judge, audience, and source of validation.
