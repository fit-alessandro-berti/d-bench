The verse itself identifies the danger: not merely doing good “before men,” but doing it **“to be seen of them.”** So the problem is not visibility as such, but the **orientation of the heart toward human recognition**.

### 1. Visibility is the occasion, not the root problem

Jesus does not condemn public goodness everywhere. Earlier in the same sermon He says:

> “Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father which is in heaven.”  
> — Matthew 5:16

So good works may rightly be seen by others. The difference is the **end** or **purpose**:

- In Matthew 5:16, the purpose is that people **glorify God**.
- In Matthew 6:1, the danger is that the person acts so that people **will notice and approve the doer**.

Public goodness is dangerous because it introduces an audience, and an audience can quietly become the reward.

### 2. The moral danger is theatrical righteousness

Matthew 6:1 warns against turning charity into performance. The phrase “to be seen of them” suggests deliberate display. The almsgiver is not merely helping the poor; he is staging a visible act of virtue. The poor person becomes, in effect, a prop in the performer’s moral drama.

That is why Jesus later says not to sound a trumpet when giving alms, as the hypocrites do “that they may have glory of men” — Matthew 6:2. Their concern is not primarily the relief of the needy, but the admiration of observers.

### 3. The craving for human praise displaces God as the audience

The deeper issue is the heart’s desire. Public goodness becomes morally dangerous when the doer wants human applause more than divine approval — or when the human applause becomes the real satisfaction sought.

Jesus says such people “have their reward.” That is, if your reward is being seen and praised by people, then you have already been paid in full. There is no further reward from the Father, because the act was not ultimately done toward Him.

So the danger is not simply being noticed. It is **wanting to be noticed**, **resting in being noticed**, or **using the good deed as a means of self-exaltation**.

### 4. “Take heed” shows how subtle the danger is

Jesus begins with “Take heed” — “Be careful” or “Guard yourselves.” This implies that the corruption can happen quietly. One may begin by genuinely intending to help, but then become attached to the admiration that follows. Public goodness is morally dangerous because it exposes the heart to temptation: the temptation to seek reputation, to manage appearances, and to love human honor.

The problem is not that people see the good deed. The problem is when **being seen becomes the goal**, and the Father who sees in secret is forgotten.

### In short

Public goodness is morally dangerous because it can turn virtue into performance and charity into self-promotion. The problem is not visibility itself, but the craving to be seen — or, more broadly, any heart that seeks human glory instead of God’s approval.
