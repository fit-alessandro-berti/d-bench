The verse treats visible love as the test of invisible love because love that never meets a concrete person is too easy to fake. God is unseen, so a claim to love him can stay inside private feeling, worship language, or self-image. The brother is seen: he has a face, needs, faults, and a claim on you. If that nearer, harder demand produces hate rather than love, the claim about God has already failed. The argument is a fortiori: inability at the easier, public test undercuts the harder, invisible one.

What it exposes is a specific self-deception, not mere inconsistency. The person is lying—to others and to himself—by treating “I love God” as a spiritual status that can stand apart from how he actually treats the brother. In 1 John’s terms, that profession is empty: love for God is not an inward mood that might later show up in conduct; it is known by whether love is practiced toward the one God has placed in view (cf. 4:7–12, 19–21). The deceit is the split between piety and practice: using the unseen as a refuge from the seen.
