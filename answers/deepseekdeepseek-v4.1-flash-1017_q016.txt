The elder brother’s anger, envy, and sense of wounded fairness are symptoms; his deepest problem is **self-righteous entitlement**—a heart that treats sonship as a reward for obedience and therefore cannot receive or rejoice in grace.

His words expose this:

- **“These many years do I serve thee”** — he speaks like a hired servant, not a beloved son.
- **“Thou never gavest me a kid”** — he believes he is owed something for his record.
- **“This thy son”** — he refuses even to call him “my brother.”
- He will not go in to the feast — he rejects the father’s grace toward the returning son and, in effect, rejects the father’s invitation too.

So the deepest issue is not merely “anger” or “envy,” though those are real. Nor is it simply “discomfort with mercy itself,” though that is very close. Underneath it all is **pride in his own obedience and a false understanding of the father**. He wants a wage rather than communion; he wants fairness rather than fellowship; he does not share the father’s heart.

The father’s answer—“Son, thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine”—shows that the elder’s real poverty was not lack of a fatted calf. He already had the father and all his goods, but he did not know the father’s goodness. His deepest problem is therefore a **self-righteous, graceless heart** that cannot love the father or the brother as grace requires. He is lost not outside the house, but in the house.
