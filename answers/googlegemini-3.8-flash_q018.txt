In Ephesians 4:31–32, the juxtaposition of **bitterness** (*pikria*) and **forgiveness** (*charizomai*) is not accidental; it is the structural and theological hinge of the entire passage. 

To understand why they are placed in direct opposition, and what nature the text assigns to bitterness, we have to look at the theological framework and the psychological progression laid out in the Greek text.

---

### 1. Why Bitterness and Forgiveness Are Placed Against Each Other

Paul is operating within his broader motif of the **“Old Man” vs. “New Man”** (vv. 22–24). The Christian life is framed as an act of stripping off soiled garments and putting on clean ones. 

Bitterness and forgiveness are placed at opposite poles for two main reasons:

* **The Ledger vs. Grace:** 
  * Bitterness is the refusal to release a debt. It is the internal brooding that keeps accurate records of wrongs, savoring the offense. 
  * Forgiveness in the Greek (*charizomai*) shares its root with *charis* (grace). It literally means to extend unmerited favor, to pardon, or to cancel a debt freely. 
  * Therefore, bitterness and forgiveness represent two fundamentally incompatible economic systems of the heart: **retribution versus grace**.
* **The Theological Pattern (v. 32b):** 
  Forgiveness is explicitly tethered to a model: *“even as God for Christ’s sake hath forgiven you.”* Bitterness is ultimately an implicit denial of the cross. A Christian cannot simultaneously stand in awe of having an infinite debt cancelled by God while clutching an unpaid, finite debt against a neighbor. Bitterness and the Gospel cannot coexist in the same space.

---

### 2. Is Bitterness a Feeling, a Habit, a Posture, or a Contagion?

The text actually synthesizes these categories, treating bitterness as **a chosen posture that hardens into habits and acts as a communal contagion.**

#### It is more than a mere “feeling”
While modern readers often reduce bitterness to an involuntary emotional sensation (like a flash of sorrow or surprise), the biblical command undermines this view. Paul says, *“Let all bitterness... be put away from you.”* Scripture does not command people to stop having involuntary nervous-system responses. 

The Greek passive imperative (*arthētō* — “let it be taken away/removed”) implies that bitterness is something the believer is **harboring and nursing**. You cannot command someone to never feel the initial sting of being wronged; you *can* command them not to cultivate the sting into a lifestyle.

#### It is a chosen posture that spawns destructive habits
Notice the psychological and behavioral anatomy Paul maps out in verse 31:

1. **Bitterness** (*pikria*): The internal, smoldering root; a settled, sour disposition of resentment.
2. **Wrath** (*thymos*): The sudden, fiery flare-up of passion.
3. **Anger** (*orgē*): The sustained, settled hostility.
4. **Clamour** (*kraugē*): The loud, out-of-control shouting/brawling.
5. **Evil speaking** (*blasphēmia*): Slander and abusive speech aimed at destroying another’s reputation.
6. **Malice** (*kakia*): The active ill-will or desire to inflict injury.

Paul reveals that bitterness is the **incubator**. It begins as an internal, chosen posture (a refusal to pardon) and rapidly calcifies into verbal and relational *habits* (shouting, slandering, plotting harm).

#### It is a communal contagion
In Ephesians 4, Paul is not merely writing a manual for private, individual spirituality; he is writing to an *ekklesia*—a community called to “the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace” (v. 3). 

Just as the Old Testament warns of a “root of bitterness” that sprouts up and defiles the whole camp (Deuteronomy 29:18; echoed in Hebrews 12:15), bitterness here is treated as socially infectious. 
* Bitterness inside the heart inevitably spills out through the mouth (*clamour* and *evil speaking*). 
* Once spoken, it infects relationships, fractures the body, and grieves the Holy Spirit (v. 30).

### Summary
The text opposes bitterness and forgiveness because **bitterness is the root of relational rot, while forgiveness is the only soil in which the “new humanity” can grow.** 

The text treats bitterness not as an involuntary feeling to be managed, but as **a toxic spiritual orientation**—one that begins in the private cultivation of offense, metastasizes into public hostility, and must be decisively renounced and displaced by the radical, debt-canceling grace of Christ.
