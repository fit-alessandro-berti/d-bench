The problem is **not visibility itself, but visibility becoming the purpose**. The decisive phrase is *“to be seen of them.”* Jesus does not condemn every good deed that happens to have witnesses; he warns against practicing goodness **in order to secure an audience’s admiration**.

That distinction is confirmed within the same Sermon on the Mount. In Matthew 5:16, Jesus says, *“Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father which is in heaven.”* Public goodness can therefore be good—when it directs attention toward God and serves one’s neighbor. Matthew 6 addresses the opposite case: when the deed directs attention toward the doer.

Publicity is morally dangerous because it can quietly change the act’s inner logic:

- **The crowd replaces God as the audience whose approval matters.**
- **The person being helped can become a means of self-display**, rather than the object of genuine compassion.
- **The doer can deceive himself**, mistaking a successful performance of piety for love of God and neighbor.
- **Human applause becomes the reward.** As Jesus says of the hypocrites in 6:2, *“they have their reward”*: they receive the recognition they sought, but not the Father’s reward.

The outward deed may still benefit someone—a hungry person can be fed by a vain giver—but the giver’s devotion is compromised.

The secrecycommended in the following verses is therefore chiefly a **discipline of sincerity**, not an absolute rule that all generosity must be hidden. It trains a person to do good without depending on credit. A useful test is: *Would I still do this if no one ever knew? And if it must be public, can I do it without needing to be admired?*

In short: **being seen while doing good is not the same as doing good in order to be seen.**
