**Short answer:** not false doctrine, at least not formally. The other four are one fault seen from different angles, and Luke names its root in verse 9: they "trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and despised others."

## What condemns the Pharisee

**His doctrine is not the problem.** He thanks God for his virtue, which is formally correct. His claims are presumably true, and his fasting and tithing exceed what the law requires. The parable only works if he is really a good man, because the original hearers would have expected him to be the hero.

**The root is self-trust.** The form of his prayer is thanksgiving, but "I" is the subject of every verb, and he asks for nothing. A prayer with no petition comes from someone who believes he needs nothing. God is his audience rather than the source of his righteousness. The phrase "prayed thus with himself" may even hint that the prayer never leaves him. So his stated creed is orthodox, but the theology he actually operates by is not.

**Comparison is the method.** If righteousness is something you possess, it has to be measured, and the easiest measure is other people. He locates himself by looking sideways ("not as other men are") rather than upward.

**Contempt is the result.** "Or even as this publican" is not incidental. He needs the tax collector, because his standing depends on someone being beneath him. This is why Luke pairs self-trust with despising others: the second follows from the first.

**Virtue is the material.** His good works are real, but he has turned them into credentials. The fasting and tithing are no longer offered to God but presented as evidence.

## Why the tax collector's posture is truer

**It is not because he is secretly better.** A tax collector was a collaborator and probably an extortioner. His self-assessment is accurate, not modest.

**He measures himself against God, not against others.** The Greek is literally "to me, *the* sinner." He makes no comparison and offers no mitigation, not even "at least I'm not a hypocrite."

**He asks for something.** "Be merciful" (*hilasthēti*) is atonement language, roughly "be propitiated toward me," spoken in the temple where sacrifice is offered. He rests his whole case on what God provides.

**Only he can receive what is given.** "Justified" is passive: it is something done to him. The Pharisee leaves unjustified not because God refuses him but because he never asked. He came to report, not to receive.

His posture is truer in the literal sense that it corresponds to reality: the reality of his sin, and the reality that righteousness before God is a gift, not an achievement.

## The trap in the parable

The publican is not justified by his humility, as if it were a better work than fasting. The moment a reader thinks, "Thank God I'm not like that Pharisee," he has prayed the Pharisee's prayer. The parable is built to catch exactly that reflex, which suggests its target is not a particular class of people but the tendency to turn anything, even humility, into grounds for self-justification.
