What kind of power is being praised?

The power praised in Philippians 2:5–8 is not power as the world ordinarily measures it — the capacity to compel, to acquire, to defend one's position. It is the power of voluntary self-emptying: what the Greek text calls kenosis ("he emptied himself," rendered in the KJV as "made himself of no reputation"). The passage is careful to establish that Christ possessed the highest conceivable status — "being in the form of God," equality with God held not as something snatched or clung to ("thought it not robbery") — precisely so that the descent that follows is understood as freely chosen rather than imposed. This is crucial: humility is only praiseworthy in one who had something real to surrender. A slave's lowliness is a condition; Christ's lowliness is an act.

So the power on display is threefold:

1. Power over oneself. The passage describes a will so secure that it needs no external validation. Christ does not grasp at equality with God because he does not need to grasp; grasping is what insecure power does. The truly powerful can afford to descend. Every verb in the hymn is active and reflexive — he emptied himself, he took the form of a servant, he humbled himself. Nothing is done to him that he does not first do.

2. Power expressed as obedience and love rather than assertion. The descent is graduated and deliberate: from divine form, to servant's form, to human likeness, to death, to the most degrading death Rome could devise — "even the death of the cross," a punishment reserved for slaves and rebels, so shameful that polite Roman society avoided naming it. The hymn insists that this bottom rung of the social and cosmic ladder is where divine power most fully reveals itself. Strength is redefined: not immunity to suffering but the freedom to accept it for the sake of others.

3. Power as pattern rather than spectacle. Paul introduces the hymn with "Let this mind be in you" — the point is imitation. Domination cannot be shared without being diminished; there is only so much throne. But self-giving power is the one form of greatness that multiplies when copied. The passage praises a power whose very nature is to be communicable.

Why is humility joined to greatness more unsettling than open domination?

Open domination, however brutal, is legible. It confirms our existing map of the world: the strong take, the weak submit, and everyone knows where they stand. We can hate a tyrant while still understanding him; he plays the game we all recognize, only more ruthlessly. Domination can therefore be resisted, appeased, envied, or fled — but it never forces us to revise our idea of what power is.

Humility in the great does something more radical: it collapses the scale by which we rank ourselves. Several distinct discomforts follow.

First, it removes our excuses. As long as greatness means domination, my own refusal to serve can be dressed up as dignity — I am simply protecting my rightful status, as anyone would. But if the one with the highest status of all "took upon him the form of a servant," then my grasping is exposed as grasping. The condescension of the truly great is a silent judgment on the self-assertion of the small. Nietzsche saw this clearly and resented it: a God who dies a slave's death makes ordinary pride look ridiculous.

Second, it cannot be defended against. Force provokes counterforce; a threat mobilizes us. But voluntary lowliness slips past every defense, because there is nothing to fight. One can kill a man who refuses to dominate — the cross proves it — but one cannot defeat him, since defeat presupposes he was contending for what you are contending for. Domination seeks our submission, which we can withhold at least inwardly. Humble greatness seeks our transformation, and that is a far deeper claim on us.

Third, it inverts the hierarchy we secretly rely on. Most of us, even when we suffer under the strong, still believe in the ladder itself; we merely want a better rung. The kenosis hymn declares that the ladder runs the other way — that the summit of reality bends downward, that God is most God-like in washing feet and dying between thieves. This is unsettling the way a change in the direction of gravity would be unsettling. If it is true, then all our climbing has been descent, and everything we called success must be re-audited.

Fourth, it shames without accusing. A dominator who demands my obedience leaves my conscience intact — I can obey outwardly and despise him inwardly. But greatness that stoops for my sake creates a debt no force could impose. The unsettling emotion it produces is not fear but something closer to exposure: the sense of being loved from below by one who had every right to look down from above. Fear can be managed; being served by one's superior cannot.

Finally, it threatens because it invites. The tyrant asks only that I stay out of his way. The self-emptying Lord asks that I have "this mind" in me — that I make his descent my own pattern. Domination costs me my goods or my liberty; kenotic greatness asks for my self-understanding, my ambitions, my whole way of keeping score. That is why it feels more dangerous. Open domination is an assault on my circumstances. Humility joined to greatness is an assault on my ego's constitution — and unlike the tyrant, it attacks with nothing in its hands, which is exactly why nothing can stop it.

In sum: the passage praises power made perfect in self-surrender — the strength of one so secure in his greatness that he could spend it entirely on others, descending by free choice to the lowest place. And this unsettles us more than any conqueror could, because a conqueror only challenges our position, while the humble God challenges our definitions: of power, of greatness, and finally of ourselves.
