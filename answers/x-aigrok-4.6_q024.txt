**External compliance is outward rule-keeping (often partial, inconsistent, or motivated by fear, habit, or social pressure) without a changed inner disposition. A “new heart” is God’s promised internal replacement of that disposition so that obedience becomes possible from the inside rather than imposed from the outside.**

In the surrounding context of Ezekiel 36, Israel’s history is one of repeated covenant-breaking despite the Law, the temple, prophets, and exile. The people could (and sometimes did) perform rituals or avoid certain sins externally while remaining unresponsive—“stony.” The promise is not merely better enforcement or more teaching; it is that God Himself will remove the unresponsive core (“stony heart out of your flesh”) and implant a living, responsive one (“heart of flesh”) plus His Spirit, so that they actually walk in His statutes. This is the language of the new covenant (compare Jeremiah 31:31–34), not self-reformation.

The material/medical imagery is deliberate and common in the prophets. A stone heart is unfeeling, unyielding, and dead to God—incapable of the sensitivity or pliability needed for genuine relationship or obedience. Flesh here means living tissue that can respond, feel, and be shaped. The condition is treated as something that must be *replaced*, not merely sanded down or trained, because the problem is portrayed as constitutive: the old heart is fundamentally resistant. Human effort, extra rules, or external pressure cannot turn stone into living flesh; only God can perform the “surgery.” This underscores both human inability and divine initiative. The same contrast appears elsewhere (e.g., “uncircumcised heart,” Pharaoh’s hardened heart, “heart of stone” vs. a new spirit).

The result is not antinomianism but empowered, internalized keeping of God’s ways. External compliance can produce appearance or temporary restraint; the new heart produces actual walking in the statutes because the source of resistance has been removed and God’s Spirit now indwells. Different traditions emphasize corporate/national restoration for Israel, individual regeneration, or both; the text itself stresses that the change originates with God and reaches the core rather than the surface.
