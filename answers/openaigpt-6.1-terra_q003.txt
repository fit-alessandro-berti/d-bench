Micah’s summary is demanding because it asks for more than outward compliance. Justice, mercy, and humility are not three boxes to check; each shapes how the others are practiced. Together they call for a lifelong way of relating to God and to other people.

- **Justice corrects mercy** when mercy becomes favoritism or excuses harm. To “do justly” means taking wrongdoing and its victims seriously, seeking fair treatment and, where possible, repair.
- **Mercy corrects justice** when justice becomes harsh or merely punitive. To “love mercy” is not simply to grant occasional leniency, but to cherish steadfast kindness—even toward those who have failed.
- **Humility corrects both** when we assume that our judgment is flawless or that our kindness makes us superior. Walking humbly means remaining answerable to God, ready to listen, repent, and learn.

The correction also runs in the other direction: justice keeps humility from becoming an excuse for passivity, while mercy keeps it from collapsing into shame or distrust of others.

For example, when someone causes harm, justice asks what accountability and repair require; mercy asks how to seek these without denying the person’s dignity or possibility of change; humility asks what we may have misunderstood and whether we are applying the same standards to ourselves.

The verse is therefore demanding precisely because none of its commands can be fulfilled mechanically. Micah calls for **fairness without cruelty, compassion without indifference to harm, and humility without withdrawal from responsibility**.
