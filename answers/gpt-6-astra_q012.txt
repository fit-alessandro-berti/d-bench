The passage is **pessimistic about unaided human self-knowledge, but not nihilistic about human nature**. Its realism is severe: we can deceive not only others but ourselves, especially about why we do what we do. Yet it does not conclude that truth about us is inaccessible. The heart is opaque to us; it is not opaque to God.

### What the passage is saying

In Jeremiah, the **“heart”** is not merely the seat of feelings. It includes thought, intention, desire, and will—the inner life from which decisions come. “Reins,” an older word for kidneys, similarly refers to the deepest interior life.

The KJV’s “desperately wicked” translates a Hebrew word often rendered **“incurably sick.”** That does not remove the moral seriousness, but it changes the image somewhat: the heart is not simply a reliable faculty that occasionally tells lies; it is itself disordered and cannot straightforwardly diagnose or cure its own condition.

The surrounding verses contrast trusting in human strength with trusting in the Lord (17:5–8). So this is not just an isolated insult to humanity. It challenges **self-sufficiency—including the confidence that our own account of ourselves must be correct**.

Verse 10 supplies the answer to “Who can know it?”: God can. And God’s judgment encompasses both the inward person and the outward life—“ways” and “fruit.” Neither respectable appearances nor claims of good intentions settle the matter.

### How, then, should we talk about motives?

**As meaningful but fallible interpretations—not infallible verdicts.**

Instead of:
> “I know my heart. I was only trying to help.”

Try:
> “I wanted to help, but I may also have wanted approval or control. What I did still needs to be evaluated.”

Several practices follow:

- **Allow for mixed motives.** Love, fear, pride, and genuine concern can coexist in one action. Finding self-interest does not prove that every better motive was fraudulent.
- **Distinguish intention from justification.** A sincere intention matters, but it does not automatically excuse harmful conduct.
- **Test self-descriptions against patterns and consequences.** “I value honesty” gains credibility through truthful behavior, especially when truth costs something.
- **Welcome correction without treating other people as omniscient.** Others may see our blind spots, but their readings of our motives are fallible too.
- **Avoid claiming privileged access to someone else’s heart.** “That action was dishonest” may be well supported; “Everything you do is secretly selfish” usually goes beyond the evidence.

The goal is not endless suspicion of every impulse. That can become another form of self-absorption. It is **humility joined to responsibility**: act on the best understanding you have, remain open to correction, and repair what your actions damage.

In short: **the heart is a witness worth hearing, but not a judge whose verdict ends the case.**
