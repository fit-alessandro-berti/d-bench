## Why the negations?

**1. They are aimed at a specific community.** Paul is writing to Corinth, a church full of rivalry: factions around favorite teachers, competition over spiritual gifts, lawsuits between believers, and the wealthy humiliating the poor at shared meals. Paul's word for their attitude, *phusioō* ("puffed up"), recurs throughout the letter ("knowledge puffeth up, but charity edifieth," 8:1). Chapter 13 sits between two chapters on spiritual gifts, so the negations work as a diagnosis. The things the Corinthians were proud of, such as eloquence, knowledge, and miracles, are worthless without love.

**2. They show that these vices are counterfeits of strength.** Boasting, envy, touchiness, and suspicion all look like self-assertion, but they come from insecurity. The braggart needs an audience, the envious person needs to be ahead, the easily provoked person needs to be deferred to, and the suspicious person needs to be protected from being fooled. Each is a defensive posture of a fragile ego. By naming what love *doesn't* do, Paul exposes how much of ordinary "strength" is really anxiety.

**3. Love is easier to recognize in restraint than in sentiment.** The passage never says love is a feeling. It describes behavior under pressure: what you do when slighted, when someone else is praised, when you could assume the worst. Negations mark the moments when the self most wants to take over.

## The vision of strength

- **Strength as security, not display.** Someone who doesn't need to win, be noticed, or be vindicated is free to be patient and kind. Love here is power that has stopped trying to prove itself.

- **Strength as endurance.** The passage opens with "suffereth long" and ends with "beareth... endureth." The Greek for "beareth" (*stegei*) can also suggest "covers" or "shelters," like a roof. Love absorbs cost rather than passing it on, and it does not retaliate.

- **Strength that is not naïve.** "Thinketh no evil" literally means something like "does not keep accounts" of wrongs. It is not gullibility, since love "rejoiceth not in iniquity, but rejoiceth in the truth." Love refuses to be suspicious or vindictive without pretending that wrong is right. "Believeth all things" and "hopeth all things" describe a generous default, not a lack of discernment.

- **Strength that is cruciform.** This fits Paul's broader theology: "my strength is made perfect in weakness" (2 Cor. 12:9), and Christ's power is revealed in self-emptying. The model is not domination but costly, patient self-giving.

## The deeper point

The passage reverses the usual question. We tend to ask, "How do I protect myself and assert my worth?" Paul suggests that real maturity is being so little preoccupied with yourself that you can attend fully to others. The negations clear away the self-concern that blocks this, and the positive verbs describe what remains: patience, kindness, truthfulness, and perseverance.
