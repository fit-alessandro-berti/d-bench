Repentance involves both “way” and “thoughts” because Isaiah is calling for a change not only in what a person does, but also in what directs their life.

- **“Way”** is the path a person takes: their conduct, habits, and dealings with others. Repentance has an outward shape; it means leaving practices that do harm, not merely regretting them.
- **“Thoughts”** are the plans, desires, and judgments that guide that path. Repentance reaches into the inward imagination: what a person dwells on, excuses, or plots. The point is not that every passing thought is a chosen act, but that outward reform is incomplete if a person still inwardly cherishes or justifies the wrong.

Yet the heart of the passage is **“let him return unto the Lord.”** Forsaking a way and its underlying thoughts is not a price paid for mercy; it is what turning back looks like. The promise that God will “abundantly pardon” means that even wrongdoing rooted deep in a person’s habits and imagination is not beyond mercy. The call is searching, but it is not a demand to make oneself worthy before coming near.
