John makes the visible the test of the invisible on purpose.

A claim like "I love God" is easy to make because God is unseen. It can't be checked, it costs nothing, and it flatters us. No one can contradict it. You can sing to Him, feel moved by Him, argue correctly about Him, and call all of that "love" — while remaining entirely unchanged toward the people around you.

A brother — in 1 John that means a fellow Christian, and by extension any neighbor in need — *has been seen.* He is concrete, inconvenient, sometimes offensive, always demanding. He needs time, forgiveness, money, patience. As 1 John 3:17-18 puts it just before: if you see your brother in need and shut up your heart, how dwells the love of God in you? Don't love in word and tongue only, but in deed and truth.

That is the logic of 4:20: it is an argument from the testable to the untestable.

> If you fail where love can be seen, touched, and proved, your claim where love cannot be seen is empty. How can you love God whom you have not seen, if you do not love the brother whom you have seen?

John is not saying philanthropy replaces God. In the same chapter he insists all love originates in God: "We love him, because he first loved us" (4:19), and "God is love" (4:8). Real love for God must flow *through* us outward.

It's a test for three reasons:

**1. Visibility makes love honest.** Sight evokes compassion and responsibility. You can't pretend you didn't notice. Loving the invisible can stay abstract; loving the visible requires sacrifice.

**2. The brother bears God's stamp.** To despise what God loves and what God made in his image, and for whom Christ died, is to despise God himself. Compare Matthew 25:40, James 2:15-17.

**3. Indifference counts as hatred.** John uses stark black-and-white language: there is no neutral "I just don't love him." To not love is to hate. To pass by is to fail the test.

The self-deception John calls a "liar" is this:

Substituting *vertical piety for horizontal obedience.* It's the religious split that says: my relationship with God is private, spiritual, emotional, orthodox — so it doesn't have to show up in how I treat people. I love a God I control in my mind, not the God who commands: "he who loveth God love his brother also" (4:21).

That deception is comforting because an invisible God never interrupts your schedule, asks for forgiveness, or disagrees with you. A visible brother does. So we settle for cheap, wordy love upward to avoid costly, active love outward — and then call the feeling "love for God."

John won't let us. To know God who is love is to become loving. Hatred, contempt, cold indifference toward a seen brother proves we have not yet loved the unseen God at all.
