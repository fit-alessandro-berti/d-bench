**Why it's hard to take seriously**

Enemy-love cuts against reciprocity, the basis of nearly all our moral intuitions. We reward those who do good and resist those who do harm, and that feels like justice itself, not mere instinct. So the command can look like a betrayal of justice, of victims, and of the distinction between good and evil. Freud argued that love given to everyone is cheapened, and that an enemy has done nothing to earn it. Nietzsche suspected it was weakness dressed up as virtue: those who cannot retaliate rename their impotence "forgiveness." There is also a historical objection: the command has often been preached by the powerful to the people they were harming.

**Is it sentimental?**

I think the text resists that reading. The commands are verbs of action: bless, do good, pray. Nothing requires warm feelings. The passage also doesn't pretend enemies aren't real. It assumes you have them, that they curse and persecute, and it keeps the categories "evil" and "good," "just" and "unjust." Sentimentality denies enmity, while this passage looks directly at it.

**Is it strategic?**

Sometimes it works. It can break cycles of retaliation, shame an opponent, or win over onlookers, as Gandhi and King showed. But the passage doesn't argue from results. It never promises that your enemy will change. If enemy-love were only a tactic, you would abandon it when it failed, and you would still be treating the enemy as an object to be maneuvered. King himself distinguished nonviolence as a technique from nonviolence as a way of life.

**Is it a deeper strength?**

This seems closest to the text's own logic. The justification is the sun and the rain: God's goodness does not react to the worthiness of its recipients, but flows from what God is. The command asks for the same independence: "that ye may be the children of your Father." The focus is on who you become, not on what happens to the enemy.

Read this way, hatred is a kind of captivity, because it lets the enemy dictate your inner life and your conduct. Enemy-love refuses to let someone else's malice determine your character. The next verses make the point: loving those who love you is ordinary, and "even the publicans" do that. Reciprocity requires no moral strength at all.

**Two caveats**

First, Nietzsche's suspicion is a useful test. Enemy-love can be counterfeit, a mask for fear or conflict-avoidance. The real thing is recognizable because it comes from someone who sees the wrong clearly and could respond otherwise. It also doesn't mean abandoning justice. Willing an enemy's good can include wanting them stopped.

Second, the passage's grounding is openly theological: you love this way because God does. Whether the ethic holds up without that foundation, or becomes merely admirable but arbitrary, is an open question.
