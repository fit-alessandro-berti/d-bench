> **Why enemy-love is difficult to take seriously:** It is **neither primarily sentimental nor primarily strategic; it is a deeper form of moral strength—rooted in disciplined alignment with God’s demonstrated character and requiring consistent, costly choice.** Here’s why, step by step, using only the passage’s own wording and logic:

1. **It is *not* primarily sentimental:**  
   Sentimentality implies feeling *without action* or action driven mainly by emotional warmth ("I *feel* nice toward them"). The verse explicitly commands concrete actions: *"do good to them that hate you," "pray for them which despitefully use you, and persecute you."* Love is defined by *behavior* ("do good," "bless," "pray"), not merely feeling pleasant. The difficulty arises because these actions often *contradict natural human impulses* (e.g., wanting to retaliate, avoid, or despise the hater/persecutor). Sentimentality would skip the hard choice; the passage insists on the action *despite* the natural inclination. Thus, it’s not about feeling; it’s about *doing rightly when it costs*.

2. **It is *not* primarily strategic (in the sense of self-interest or manipulation):**  
   While God’s nature (sun/rain on "evil" and "good") *reveals consistency* (God cares for all, not just the useful), the passage does **not** frame loving enemies as a tactical move for personal gain, reputation, or even long-term social stability ("strategic" in the sense of "what works best for me"). Saying "I’ll love my enemy to make peace" or "to avoid conflict" reads *into* the text. The motivation given is theological identity: *"That ye may be the children of your Father which is in heaven"* and the description of God’s consistent care (*"he maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good, and sendeth rain on the just and on the unjust"*). The goal is *becoming* who God is (a child of God), not optimizing outcomes for oneself. God’s sun/rain example shows *objective reality* (God sustains all), not a strategic calculation. Therefore, framing it primarily as "strategic" misreads the text; the difficulty isn’t primarily about calculating benefit, but about aligning one’s *consistent action* with a reality (God’s character) that contradicts our instinctive self-protection.

3. **It *is* a deeper form of moral strength:**  
   The passage identifies the difficulty and its resolution together: *Loving enemies (doing good, blessing, praying despite hate/persecution) is the very means by which we "may be the children of your Father."* This reveals the core: **Enemy-love is serious moral strength because it requires consciously choosing actions (do good, bless, pray) that directly oppose our natural self-preservation instincts (avoid, retaliate, despise) in order to embody and reflect God’s demonstrated character (sun/rain on all).**  
   - *Why it’s hard:* Our instincts favor self-protection and avoidance ("hate you," "despitefully use you" trigger natural defensiveness). Doing the opposite ("do good," "pray") feels costly, unnatural, and risky (real-world persecution is possible). It requires vigilance against slipping into judgment or resentment.
   - *Why it’s strength (not sentiment):* It is *consistent action* ("do good," "pray") chosen *repeatedly* and *especially* when it hurts or seems pointless (when hated or persecuted). It is not optional or mood-dependent; it is defined by what a "child of God" *does*. The strength lies in *reliably aligning one’s behavior with the reality God has shown* (sun/rain on all), even when it feels counter-intuitive or costly. It transforms identity ("ye may be the children...") through practice. It acknowledges the difficulty ("praying for them which persecute you") without excusing it or reducing it to feeling.
   - *Why it’s not just strategic:* Calling it "strategic" would imply the primary reason is "this works best" for the lover (e.g., peace, safety, reputation). The text gives no such reason; the reason is identity formation ("that ye may be the children of your Father"). God’s sun/rain is descriptive of *what God does*, not a strategic move God makes for us. Thus, the seriousness comes from *faithfulness to God’s character*, not from calculating personal advantage.

**In plain terms (directly answering the question):**  
> Enemy-love is difficult to take seriously *not* because it feels nice (sentimental) or because it cleverly serves our interests (strategic), but because it asks us, in the midst of real hurt, hate, and persecution, to *consistently choose kind, forgiving, praying action*—even when it goes against our natural instinct to defend ourselves or retaliate. It is difficult because it requires moral strength: the disciplined, repeated choice to act as a "child of God" would act (sun on evil, rain on unjust), trusting that God’s character (not our strategy) defines the standard. The strength is in the *doing* (do good, bless, pray) amid the cost, not in feeling warm toward the enemy or calculating benefits.
