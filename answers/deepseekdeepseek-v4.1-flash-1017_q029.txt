The required posture is **restorative, gentle, humble, and self-vigilant**—what we might call a “penitent correction.” The one correcting is not a superior judge standing over a sinner but a fellow sinner and fellow recipient of grace standing alongside one who has been caught.

1. **Restoration sets the aim.**  
   The verb behind “restore” (*katartizete*) means to mend, set right, equip, put back in order—used for repairing nets or setting a broken bone. So correction is not primarily punishment, exposure, or victory over an opponent; it aims at healing, reintegration, and wholeness. The fault is real, but the goal is the person’s restoration.

2. **Meekness sets the manner.**  
   “Spirit of meekness” means gentleness that is not weakness but strength under control. It is a fruit of the Spirit (Gal 5:22–23), so it is the opposite of the fleshly “biting and devouring” of 5:15 and the pride of 6:3. Meekness refuses contempt. It treats the erring person as a brother still to be restored, not as an enemy to be crushed.

3. **Self-suspicion sets the corrector’s stance.**  
   “Considering thyself, lest thou also be tempted” means the corrector must look at his or her own vulnerability. It is not paralysis or endless self-doubt; it is sober self-distrust. The one who corrects must remember that he too is made of the same vulnerable flesh. This guards against pride, hypocrisy, harshness, and the illusion that “I could never do that.”

Why are these kept together? Because each prevents the others from being distorted:

- **Restoration without meekness or self-suspicion** becomes harsh, punitive, and self-righteous.
- **Meekness without restoration** becomes permissiveness, indifference, or conflict avoidance.
- **Self-suspicion without restoration or meekness** becomes paralyzed introspection or a way of avoiding responsibility.
- **Together**, they produce the only kind of correction that fits the gospel: firm about the fault, gentle toward the person, watchful over the self, and aimed at healing.

So the moral posture is not “I am better than you,” but “I am also vulnerable, forgiven, and in need of grace; therefore let me help restore you in the way I myself would need to be restored.” That is why restoration, meekness, and self-suspicion belong together: they make correction an act of burden-bearing love and thus a fulfillment of “the law of Christ” (Gal 6:2).
