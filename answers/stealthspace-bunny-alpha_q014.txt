**This text most directly says that treasure reveals the heart’s orientation. It also implies a training effect: what we treasure shapes—and gradually strengthens—the desires and commitments of the heart.** The verse is diagnostic, but it is not merely a detached observation about human psychology.

### “There will your heart be also”

The heart here is not simply a temporary feeling. It represents the whole inner person: desires, hopes, fears, loyalties, and what a person ultimately lives for.

Jesus’ claim is that **what we treasure discloses where our hearts are directed**. We may profess one allegiance while continually reserving our security, identity, and future for something else. Treasure makes those priorities visible. The context makes this clearer: Jesus is addressing rival sources of security and devotion, not simply whether someone owns possessions.

But “heart” is not a separate faculty that remains unaffected by what we treasure. To treasure something is to love it, rely on it, give it attention, and organize life around it. The verse therefore implies a reinforcing relationship:

> What we treasure reveals what we love; what we love receives our attention and trust; and that attention and trust strengthen its hold on us.

### Possession, attention, and love

**1. Possession identifies what we are staking life on.**  
“Lay up treasures” concerns more than owning property. It concerns what we hold onto as precious and what we make responsible for our security and fulfillment. Jesus does not say that possessing goods is itself sinful. The question is whether corruptible things have become our treasury—our final reliance—or whether God is.

Possession by itself therefore cannot reveal every motive. Someone may possess little and be deeply attached to security; someone else may possess much and remain generously devoted to God. The revealing issue is not the quantity of wealth, but the **attachment and priority** expressed in how it is held and used.

**2. Attention trains the heart by giving something continued importance.**  
The “heart’s” location involves more than conscious belief. It includes what we dwell upon, plan for, fear losing, and trust will sustain us. Repeatedly returning our concern to something makes it increasingly familiar and desirable.

This is where the text’s formative dimension appears: attachment to a treasure does not merely reveal a preference; it tends to deepen that preference. The things we consistently care about become the things our hopes, thoughts, and choices naturally pursue.

**3. Love gives treasure its emotional and spiritual weight.**  
To treasure something is not merely to appreciate it. It is to love it and depend upon it. When the following verses distinguish serving God from serving “mammon,” money is standing for a rival devotion: something trusted to provide what only God can properly supply.

The verse warns against a divided heart—not against having thoughts about money, but against claiming one treasure while giving our deepest love, trust, and allegiance to another.

### The relationship is neither mechanical nor incidental

The text does not say that objects automatically manufacture a heart’s desires. People are not rigidly determined by what they own. Nor does it reduce the heart to whatever happens to be in our possession.

Rather, Jesus presents a moral and spiritual dynamic: **our treasures disclose our deepest commitments, and our repeated attention to and dependence upon those treasures strengthen those commitments**. The verse calls for self-examination, while the command to “lay up” treasures in heaven calls for a different orientation—trusting and loving God rather than making earthly security our god.

So the answer is **both**: treasure reveals the heart, and attachment to treasure helps train the heart. Possession shows what we are valuing, attention shapes what we desire, and love determines what our hearts are ultimately bound to.
