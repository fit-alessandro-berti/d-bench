Enemy-love is hard to take seriously because it cuts against the instincts and social systems that usually keep people alive and groups coherent. It is not sentimental, and it is not mainly a clever strategy. In the Sermon on the Mount it is a demand for moral strength: refuse the cycle of retaliation and act as if the other person still belongs to the same moral world as you.

### Why it feels unreal
Several pressures make it sound naïve.

- **Retaliation feels like justice.** When someone curses, hates, or persecutes you, the natural reply is to match or exceed the harm. That reply restores a sense of order and self-respect. Enemy-love looks like surrender of that order.
- **Groups run on loyalty.** Enemies often mark the boundary of “us.” Loving them can look like betrayal of your own people, not virtue.
- **Feelings do not obey commands.** The verse does not say “feel warm toward them.” It says love, bless, do good, and pray. People hear “love” as an emotion they cannot manufacture, so they dismiss the whole teaching.
- **It has been sentimentalized.** Soft preaching turns it into niceness or passivity. Once it is reduced to “be sweet,” serious people stop listening—especially people under real threat.

Nietzsche’s charge that this ethic is slave morality captures one real risk: if enemy-love means only swallowing injury and calling weakness holy, it deserves contempt. The text, read carefully, does not require that.

### Not sentiment, and not mainly strategy
Matthew 5:44–45 ties the command to God’s conduct, not to a mood. God “maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good, and sendeth rain on the just and on the unjust.” The point is impartial generosity, not approval of evil. You are told to become a child of that Father by refusing to let the enemy’s malice set the terms of your action.

The verbs are practical: bless, do good, pray. That is discipline under provocation, not a feeling. It also does not cancel other moral duties in the wider biblical picture—truth-telling, protection of the vulnerable, judgment of wrong. Enemy-love limits how you treat the person; it does not require you to pretend the wrong was right or to leave people unprotected.

It can have strategic effects. Breaking the revenge cycle sometimes disarms an opponent, as in parts of the civil-rights practice of nonviolent resistance. But the verse does not justify the command by its success rate. Rain falls on the unjust whether or not they reform. If you love enemies only when it “works,” you have a tactic, not this ethic. When it fails—as it often does—the command still stands.

### A harder kind of strength
The strength here is negative and positive at once.

- **Negative:** you do not let the enemy conscript you. Hatred, curses, and persecution are attempts to pull you into their logic. Refusing to return them is a form of self-command. That is closer to courage than to softness.
- **Positive:** you keep treating the enemy as a person who can still receive good, blessing, and prayer. That is costly. It risks looking foolish, being exploited, and losing allies who wanted revenge.

Jesus places this next to the rejection of “eye for an eye” as a personal rule of life (Matthew 5:38–42). The old limit on vengeance—“only an eye, not a life”—is tightened further: do not use the injury as your measure at all. The measure becomes the Father’s undiscriminating care, not the enemy’s last blow.

So the difficulty is not an accident of wording. Enemy-love asks you to separate justice from payback, loyalty from destruction of the outsider, and strength from domination. Most moral systems praise the first half of each pair and quietly rely on the second. This saying refuses the bargain. That is why it remains easy to admire in a text and hard to enact when the curse, the hatred, or the persecution is aimed at you.
