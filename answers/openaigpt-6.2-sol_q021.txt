Pride comes before a fall because it makes the fall harder to see coming. The proud person is not merely guilty of thinking too highly of himself; he is less able to recognize his limits. A warning sounds like an insult, advice feels like a threat to his standing, and success looks like proof that he was right all along. The habits that could keep him from falling—listening, admitting error, accepting help—are the very habits pride makes hardest to practice.

The contrast with “the lowly” sharpens the point. The proud may actually have spoil to divide: arrogance can pay, at least for a time. Yet the proverb says it is better to stand with people of little status than to share in those gains. It distinguishes **being lowly in spirit** from **being lowly in social position**: humility is not thinking yourself worthless, but being willing to learn from and stand alongside people who cannot increase your prestige.

The two verses thus connect pride’s blindness to its social rewards. If you choose your companions for the status and gains they offer, you may end up surrounded by people who have as much reason as you do not to question how you got them.
