Luke 6:37 is often misread as a blanket endorsement of moral relativism—as if Jesus were saying, *"Nothing is really right or wrong, so don’t have opinions about anyone’s behavior."* 

In reality, the verse does the exact opposite: it assumes that moral reality matters deeply, while drawing a sharp boundary around **who gets to play the final executioner** and **what posture of heart** we bring to another person’s failures.

Here is why the verse is not a dismissal of moral judgment, followed by the crucial difference between discernment and the appetite to condemn.

---

### 1. Why Luke 6:37 Assumes Moral Judgments *Do* Matter

The structure of the verse itself refutes the idea that morality is irrelevant. Notice the progression of the three commands:
1. *Judge not*
2. *Condemn not*
3. *Forgive*

**Forgiveness only makes sense if a real wrong has occurred.** You cannot "forgive" a neutral preference or an imaginary offense; forgiveness presupposes a moral standard that was genuinely violated. When the verse tells us not to condemn and instead to forgive, it is not erasing the category of sin or harm—it is telling us how to respond when harm is real.

Furthermore, the immediate context in Luke 6 explicitly *requires* moral evaluation:
* **The Speck and the Plank (vv. 41–42):** Jesus does not say the speck in your neighbor's eye isn't there. He says you must first remove the plank from your own eye *so that you can see clearly* to help remove the speck. That is a call for moral clarity, not blindness.
* **Trees and Fruit (vv. 43–45):** Just a few verses later, Jesus says a good tree cannot bear bad fruit, nor a bad tree good fruit. Recognizing the difference between good and bad fruit is, by definition, an act of moral judgment.

What Luke 6:37 prohibits is not moral evaluation, but **condemnatory judgment**—stepping into God’s seat to pronounce final verdicts on another person’s soul, motives, and worth.

---

### 2. Discernment vs. The Appetite to Condemn

The real tension in human psychology—and the heart of what Jesus is addressing—is the difference between **discernment** and **the appetite to condemn**. On the surface, both involve noticing that something is wrong. Beneath the surface, they are opposites.

#### **Target: Actions vs. Ultimate Worth**
* **Discernment** evaluates actions, patterns, truth claims, and consequences. It asks: *Is this behavior honest or deceitful? Is this situation safe or destructive? Is this teaching true or false?* It names a wrong accurately without pretending to know the entirety of a person's heart or eternal standing.
* **Condemnation** moves seamlessly from *“You did something wrong”* to *“You are worthless / irredeemable.”* It reduces a complex human being to their worst failure and stamps a permanent verdict on them.

#### **Motive: Clarity vs. Ego**
* **Discernment** is motivated by truth, love, and protection. A parent discerning whether a teenager’s friend is leading them into danger, or a community discerning that a leader is abusing power, isn't indulging in moral superiority—they are protecting what is good and vulnerable.
* **The appetite to condemn** is fed by ego. There is a subtle, intoxicating pleasure in catching someone else in a moral failure: it lets us feel righteous by comparison, masks our own flaws, and grants us a temporary sense of power. Notice that Jesus pairs *"Judge not"* with the warning about the plank in our own eye—because condemnation almost always serves as a distraction from self-examination.

#### **Posture: Reluctance vs. Eagerness**
* **Discernment** tends to be careful, patient, and even sorrowful when confronting evil. It checks the facts, considers context, and resists jumping to conclusions because it cares about justice—and false accusations are an injustice.
* **The appetite to condemn** is impatient and hungry for a target. It prefers outrage over inquiry, rumors over nuance, and public shaming over quiet restoration. Where discernment asks, *"What actually happened, and how can this be made right?"*, the appetite to condemn asks, *"Who can we write off today?"*

#### **Horizon: Openness vs. Finality**
* **Discernment** holds the door open for repentance, repair, and growth. Because it recognizes that human beings are flawed and capable of change, it can say, *"This action is unacceptable,"* while still hoping for the person’s restoration. This is why it aligns naturally with *"forgive, and ye shall be forgiven."*
* **Condemnation** slams the door shut. It treats human failure as a finished story rather than a chapter. By refusing mercy to others, the condemning person locks themselves into a world where no mercy is possible—hence the reciprocal warning: *"and ye shall be condemned."*

---

### Summary

Luke 6:37 does not ask us to abandon our moral compass or pretend that cruelty, dishonesty, and injustice don't matter. Rather, it warns us against the human temptation to turn moral standards into weapons for our own pride. 

**Discernment** is the discipline of seeing clearly so we can love wisely and resist harm; **the appetite to condemn** is the desire to sit on a throne, look down on others, and declare them beyond grace. The verse calls us to keep the first and surrender the second.
