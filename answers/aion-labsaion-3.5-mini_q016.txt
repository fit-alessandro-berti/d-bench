The five options aren't really alternatives — they're layers of a single problem, arranged from surface to depth. Anger is the visible symptom; aversion to mercy is the theological name for it. But underneath both is something the parable states with startling precision.

**What the text actually shows**

1. **Anger, and refusal to enter (v. 28).** He *ōrgisthē* — was enraged. But the significant act is not the emotion; it's "would not go in." The feast is the father's joy made audible and edible. Standing outside it is a declaration: *I will not participate in this joy.* He is near the house but outside the father's heart — the exact inversion of the younger son, who was far away but came in.

2. **His speech betrays him (v. 29).** "These many years do I serve thee" — the verb is *douleuō*, *I have slaved for you*. He describes sonship in the language of a hired laborer: years rendered, wages withheld ("thou never gavest me a kid"). And "neither transgressed I at any time thy commandment" is self-salvation in the first person. Compare the younger son: "I have sinned against heaven, and before thee." The elder offers no confession — only a ledger and a grievance. (Note also that he denounces "harlots" — detail he could not know firsthand; his moral accounting includes decorating the other's sin.)

3. **"That I might make merry with my friends."** He can imagine celebration — but only a merit-funded party among peers. The father's feast is familial: "this thy brother." Watch the pronoun war: the elder says "this *thy son*," refusing brotherhood; the father answers, "this *thy brother*," restoring it.

4. **The father's reply (v. 31): "Son, thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine."** This is the devastating line. The elder was never actually deprived. Everything was already his. He never asked because he never understood the relationship — he assumed favor was rationed by merit. Grace is not zero-sum, but you can only see that from inside grace, and he has never been inside it.

5. **"It was meet" (v. 32)** — *edei*, "it was necessary." The same necessity-language Luke uses for divine compulsion ("I must be about my Father's business"; "the Son of man must suffer"). Joy over the recovered is not a mood the father happens to be in; it is the necessary grammar of grace. The elder resists a necessity.

**Weighing the options**

- **Anger** — real, but it's the smoke, not the fire.
- **Envy** — present but misdirected. He doesn't primarily want the fatted calf; he wants his merit *acknowledged*. That's why his complaint concerns a kid *for his friends*.
- **Wounded fairness** — the felt experience, and genuinely near the core. Grace feels to him like theft: if the undeserving is celebrated, the deserving has been robbed.
- **Pride in obedience** — the engine of the ledger. "I never transgressed" is the credential on which his whole identity rests.
- **Discomfort with mercy** — the theological name for the syndrome. But it needs one refinement: his problem is not that mercy *exists*, but that he has never received mercy *as mercy*. If the younger is welcomed home as a son, unpurchased, then what exactly has the elder been purchasing all these years? Mercy given freely to another indicts his entire life strategy.

**The synthesis**

The deepest problem is this: **the elder brother relates to the father as a wage-earner, not a son.** His obedience was transactional — service rendered for goods owed — and a transactional relationship to grace cannot rejoice in grace. He is lost without ever leaving home: morally upright, physically proximate, relationally estranged. Notice that the father goes out to him just as he ran to the younger (v. 20) — the same self-humbling condescension extended to the respectable sinner, because the elder is as truly outside the feast as the younger was outside the country.

That is why Jesus tells the parable at all (15:1–2): the Pharisees' murmuring, "This man receiveth sinners," is the elder brother's complaint in real time. And it's why the parable ends unresolved — no reply from the elder, no closing scene. The father is still outside, entreating. The invitation stands open to whoever is listening.

In one line: his deepest problem is not that mercy was shown to his brother, but that he has never let mercy be shown to him — and unreceived mercy always experiences another's forgiveness as an injustice.
