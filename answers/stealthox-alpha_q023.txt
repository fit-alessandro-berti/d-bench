## Two words, one whole person

The Hebrew makes the pairing deliberate rather than decorative:

> *yaʿazōv rāshāʿ darkô* — "let the wicked forsake his **way**" (*derekh*)
> *wəʾîsh ʾāwen maḥshəvōtāyw* — "and the man of iniquity his **thoughts**" (*maḥashavot*)

*Derekh* is a road — not isolated misdeeds but a habitual direction of travel, a life in motion toward somewhere. *Maḥashavot* is deeper still: plans, devices, schemes, the things a person *devises* (cf. Gen 6:5, "every imagination of the thoughts of his heart"). The verse insists that sin is both trajectory and engine — the road being walked *and* the map being consulted. To deal with only one is to deal with half a sinner.

## Why both must go

**Outward conduct alone is insufficient.** A person can abandon visible vices while the inner machinery that produced them keeps running. Scripture consistently locates sin's origin upstream of behavior — "out of the heart proceed evil thoughts, murders..." (Matt 15:19); "keep thy heart... for out of it are the issues of life" (Prov 4:23). Repentance that stops at conduct is moral renovation, not return; it whitewashes the tomb.

**Inward remorse alone is equally insufficient.** Sorrow over thoughts that never changes the walked road is sentimentality. Hence the prophets' refrain: "bring forth fruits worthy of repentance" (Matt 3:8); "repent and turn to God, *doing* deeds consistent with repentance" (Acts 26:20).

So the verse guards against two opposite frauds: the respectable life with a corrupt interior, and the pious feeling with an unchanged life. True repentance (*shuv*, "return") is a reversal of the whole self — feet and imagination together.

## The literary key: verses 8–9

Notice what comes immediately after:

> "My **thoughts** are not your thoughts, neither are your **ways** my **ways**... as the heavens are higher than the earth, so are my **ways** higher than your **ways**, and my **thoughts** than your **thoughts**."

The exact two nouns of v. 7 reappear as God's own. The structure is an exchange: lay down *your* ways and thoughts; receive *His*. The wicked man's *maḥashavot* are self-authored schemes; God's *maḥashavot* are purposes of mercy. Repentance is less a moral cleanup than a change of authorship — surrendering the plans we devise for the plans He has. A traveler can turn around on the road, but if his map and destination are unchanged, he will plot his way back. Both the steps *and* the navigation must be handed over.

## What this implies about mercy

Three things follow:

1. **No one is disqualified by their inner life.** Many assume they stand outside the offer because their worst sins are invisible — envy, contempt, fantasy, resentment, unbelief. Verse 7 explicitly names thoughts as things that can be *forsaken* and therefore forgiven. The invitation reaches into the room where shame usually hides deepest.

2. **Respectability is stripped as a refuge.** The "man of iniquity" may walk a blameless-looking road while his schemes churn. Outward decency without surrendered thoughts is precisely what this verse refuses to call repentance.

3. **The breadth of the pardon matches the depth of the surrender.** Because God asks for everything — down to the imagination — He gives everything: *ki-yarbeh lisloach*, literally "for he will **multiply** to forgive." The "abundantly" of the KJV is not exaggeration; it is arithmetic. And note the grounding: the offer rests not on the thoroughness of our repentance but on His character — "*for* he will abundantly pardon."

In short: the verse demands the whole person because it offers mercy to the whole person. Halfway repentance would secure only halfway pardon — so God, in His abundance, asks for the road *and* the map, and pardons accordingly.
