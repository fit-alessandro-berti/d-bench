## Why the visible is the test of the invisible

The verse compresses the argument of the whole letter, and the key to it sits eight verses earlier:

> "No man hath seen God at any time. If we love one another, God dwelleth in us, and his love is perfected in us." (4:12)

Several strands converge there:

1. **The epistemic point.** A claim to love the invisible God is, from the inside, incorrigible — nothing in the feeling or the words can falsify it. Love of the brother is not like that: it either feeds, forgives, endures, and shares, or it doesn't. John has already drawn the line sharply: "let us not love in word, neither in tongue; but in deed and in truth" (3:18); the man who sees his brother in need and shuts up his heart — "how dwelleth the love of God in him?" (3:17). The neighbor is the only place where the invisible claim becomes checkable.

2. **There is one love, not two.** For John, love is not a private sentiment aimed at God plus a separate one aimed at people; it is participation in God's own love — "God is love... we love him, because he first loved us" (4:8, 19). And God's love is by definition self-giving toward the *unlovely* (4:10). A love drawn from that spring cannot pool in the self. Hence 5:1 states the syllogism outright: "every one that loveth him that begat loveth him also that is begotten of him." Loving the Father while hating his child is not a partial love of God; it is a contradiction of it.

3. **The visible is God's chosen medium.** The entire Johannine claim is that the unseen God is known through the seen — "the only begotten Son... hath declared him" (John 1:18); "that which we have heard, which we have seen with our eyes, which we have looked upon, and our hands have handled" (1 John 1:1). Now that Christ is himself unseen, mutual love is where God becomes visible among us (4:12). To bypass the seen brother in order to reach the unseen God is to refuse God's own mode of self-giving.

4. **Hence the "cannot."** John does not say the man's love of God is merely unproven; he says it is impossible (*ou dynatai*). Given the above, this is not hyperbole. Loving the unseen God has no unmediated form in this life: its operational content is keeping his commandment, and the commandment is "love one another" (John 13:34; 14:15). A love of God that bypasses the neighbor can only be love of one's own idea of God.

## The self-deception it exposes

Notice the formula. The verse belongs to a pattern repeated throughout the letter: "If we say that we have fellowship with him, and walk in darkness, we lie" (1:6); "he that saith, I know him, and keepeth not his commandments, is a liar" (2:4); "he that saith he is in the light, and hateth his brother, is in darkness" (2:9). John is targeting a religious self-description that outruns the life — likely the secessionists of 2:19, who claimed intimate knowledge of God while breaking fellowship with the community. The deception has layers:

1. **Mistaking profession and feeling for love.** "I love God" can be sustained by devotional warmth, correct confession, even intense religious experience. None of that is love yet. Love is the act with a cost attached (3:16–17).

2. **Exploiting God's invisibility.** Here is the sharp edge. The unseen God, as an object of our devotion, makes no inconvenient demands: he never irritates us, never contradicts us, never needs our patience. The brother is *given*, unchosen, and tiresome — he is the one toward whom love cannot be simulated. Dostoevsky's Elder Zosima puts it exactly: "active love is a harsh and dreadful thing compared with love in dreams." A piety that thrives toward the unseen and curdles toward the seen has chosen the dream. (Kierkegaard makes the same point: because God is invisible, love for him is oddly *safe* from factual correction — so God mercifully sets the neighbor before us as resistance, as reality.)

3. **Keeping two ledgers.** The man is a "liar" not chiefly because he deceives others, but because the falsifying evidence stands before his eyes — his brother's face — and he refuses to let it adjudicate his claim. That is self-deception in its strict form: maintaining two accounts, one for the inner religious self and one for actual conduct, and never letting them be audited against each other. The deception may be quite sincere; sincerity is precisely what makes it deception of oneself rather than of others.

## The mercy in the test

The verse is often read as an accusation, but Augustine, preaching on this very letter, heard a gift in it: since you cannot yet see God, love the brother you *can* see — practice on the visible, and you will be made capable of the God you will one day see. The neighbor is not only love's examination hall; he is its training ground. What John exposes as a lie, he exposes in order that the claim might become true.
