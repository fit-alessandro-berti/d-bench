**Luke 6:37 does not forbid discernment. It forbids the appetite to condemn.** Moral judgments matter greatly; what matters even more is the spirit in which we make them and the person we become through them.

## What “judge not” means

Modern English can make this verse sound like a blanket prohibition on moral assessment. In Greek, however, *krinein* (“judge”) and *katakrinein* (“condemn”) both belong to the judicial sphere; Luke is not simply giving us “discern” for one word and “condemn” for the other. The force of the saying comes from its context: “with what judgment ye judge, ye shall be judged” (Luke 6:38).

The question, then, is not, “May I make a moral assessment?” but, **“Do I use my assessment to execute a verdict on another person?”** Human judgment is provisional: we see in part, we are capable of self-deception, and we cannot know another person’s whole story, repentance, or destiny. Final judgment belongs to God. Our judgments are meant to serve love, not usurp God’s throne.

## Why moral judgments matter

Scripture commands discernment elsewhere:

- “Test the spirits” (1 John 4:1).
- “Be wise as serpents and innocent as doves” (Matthew 10:16).
- “Speak the truth in love” (Ephesians 4:15).
- “Correct a fellow servant in a spirit of meekness” (Galatians 6:1).
- “Rebuke sharply” those who teach falsely (Titus 1:9).

Some moral judgments are acts of love. Discerning deception from truth, danger from safety, repentance from manipulation, or false teaching from genuine faith is not moral callousness; it is love doing its homework. Refusing to distinguish anything is not humility. It is sometimes a decision to protect wrongdoing, and that decision is itself a moral judgment.

Jesus himself judged sin, named hypocrisy, and warned his disciples. What he refused to do was sentence sinners as beyond redemption.

## Discernment and the appetite to condemn

The difference is less about *whether* a judgment is made than about *what the judgment is for*.

| | Discernment | Appetite to condemn |
|---|---|---|
| **Aim** | To see clearly and respond wisely | To vindicate oneself, defeat another, or maintain superiority |
| **Focus** | The act, the situation, and what response love requires | The other person as guilty, especially as a label or fixed verdict |
| **Posture toward self** | Humble about one’s own limits and need for mercy | Self-righteous; uses others’ faults to feel exempt |
| **Speech** | Proportionate, truthful, often private, aimed at healing and change | Public shaming, rehearsed grievance, tearing down |
| **Effect** | Frees a person to act rightly and leaves room for repentance | Hardens both judge and judged and closes off the possibility of repair |

The clearest diagnostic is the **motive**. Discernment asks, “What is true here, and how should love respond?” The appetite to condemn asks, “Who is guilty, and how can I make them pay?” It often masquerades as zeal for holiness. But it feeds on the very knowledge that should humble us: *I know their faults, and therefore I know I am comparatively fine.*

Discernment can say: *This is false. It has harmed people. Stop, confess, make restitution, and change.* The appetite to condemn wants to say: *You are rotten, and I want everyone to know it.*

Notice that the first judgment leaves the person’s agency intact. The second tends to turn their worst moment into their identity—and, in the judge’s heart, into a permanent sentence.

## Forgiveness is not the same as excusing wrongdoing

“Forgive, and ye shall be forgiven” means that forgiveness is a fundamental pattern of the kingdom, not an optional pleasantry. To forgive is to refuse to keep a ledger, to cancel the debt as a basis of relationship, and to release the other from the grip of our verdict.

But forgiveness does not require pretending that sin did not happen. Nor does it require remaining in danger, concealing abuse, or abandoning justice. Love can protect while refusing to dehumanize; it can establish boundaries while declining to feed the appetite to punish.

Jesus models this in John 8:1–11. He stops a mob, exposes the hypocrisy of the accusation, refuses the unjust sentence—and then says to the woman, “Neither do I condemn thee: but go and sin no more.” There is moral truth without condemnation. He neither excuses her sin nor treats her as beyond repair.

That is the heart of the difference: **discernment says, “This is wrong, and there is a way forward.” Condemnation says, “You are lost, and I will see you stay lost.”**

## A practical test of our own judgments

When you find yourself judging someone, ask:

1. **Am I seeking truth or victory?**
2. **Would I receive this same scrutiny if our positions were reversed?**
3. **Is my speech proportionate, and private where possible and appropriate?**
4. **Can I hold conviction about the wrong and hope for the person at the same time?**

If these questions produce only suspicion, we are probably not discerning—we are condemning. If they produce grief, courage, and a willingness to speak the truth in love, we are probably doing what Luke is redirecting us toward.

Luke 6:37 therefore draws a distinction not between judgment and moral numbness, but between **two spirits**. Moral judgments shape the truth of our lives, the health of our communities, and the character of our own souls. The appetite to condemn does the opposite. We are called to discern sin clearly, speak mercy honestly, forgive without keeping score, and leave the final verdict to the God who alone can judge the heart—and reach someone where our condemnation cannot.
