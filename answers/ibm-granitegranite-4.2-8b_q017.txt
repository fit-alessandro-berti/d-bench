**First, a quick, clear restatement of the passage (using your wording for consistency):**  
> “And this is the condemnation, that light is come into the world, and men loved darkness rather than light, because their deeds were evil.  
> For every one that doeth evil hateth the light, neither cometh to the light, lest his deeds should be reproved.  
> But he that doeth truth cometh to the light, that his deeds may be made manifest, that they are wrought in God.”

**Key clarification up front (important for avoiding confusion):**  
- “Light” here is **not** literal physical light (like sunshine). It is a **symbol** (common in Scripture, e.g., John 1:5; 8:12; 1 Timothy 1:10) for **God’s revealing truth**—the clear, exposing, morally clarifying reality that shows what people truly do, think, and value.  
- “Darkness” is the **contrast**: the state of preferring not to see clearly, living in vague or self-deceiving ways, avoiding direct scrutiny of one’s own deeds. It is not necessarily “evil” as a place, but the *choice* to stay in it when light is available.  
- “Doeth evil” and “doeth truth” are plain descriptors: “one who acts/lives in ways that are morally wrong/untrue” versus “one who acts/lives in alignment with truth/goodness.” (The word “doeth” is archaic but simply means “does”; the meaning is clear.)

**Now, directly answering your question: “Why would light be hated?”**

> Light (i.e., God’s clear, exposing truth) would be “hated” (i.e., actively avoided or disliked) **not because light itself is unpleasant, but because it inevitably shows what a person is actually doing when they act “evil” (i.e., deeds inconsistent with true goodness or moral reality).**  
> People avoid light because **the light reveals their deeds plainly**, and that plain revelation can feel uncomfortable, threatening, or painful—especially if those deeds contradict how they see themselves or what they hope others think of them. In short: *light hates no one; it simply shows; people hate coming to light because it shows their evil deeds, which they would rather hide.*

**Why does this plain showing cause avoidance? Exploring truth, exposure, shame, and the desire not to be seen clearly—step by step, using only what the passage implies and everyday human experience:**

1. **Truth = Clear, exposing reality (as the passage says)**  
   - The verse says the light’s purpose is to make deeds “made manifest” (clearly visible) and to show they are “wrought in God” (i.e., done in alignment with God’s good order). Truth here isn’t abstract philosophy; it’s the straightforward, God-revealed picture of what people actually do. It doesn’t soften, excuse, or hide; it names the deed (e.g., selfishness, dishonesty, unkindness, pride) in context.  
   - *Why this matters:* When truth is clear and exposing, it leaves little room for vague self-justification like “It wasn’t that bad” or “Everyone does it.” It points to the actual action and its effect.

2. **Exposure = Seeing the deed plainly, without hiding**  
   - The light doesn’t shout or condemn immediately; it simply *makes the deed manifest*. Think of it like turning on a bright, clear light in a room where someone has been acting quietly or misleadingly. Suddenly, the action is visible—not hidden by shadows, excuses, or pretending.  
   - *Why this matters:* Most people, even when they do something they know isn’t fully right, prefer some level of privacy or ambiguity about it. They might downplay it internally or not mention it openly. Exposure removes that cover.

3. **Shame (as naturally felt when truth meets hidden deed) = The inner “ouch” of seeing the mismatch**  
   - The passage doesn’t use the word “shame,” but it directly describes the fear: people “hateth the light, neither cometh to the light, lest his deeds should be reproved” (i.e., lest they be pointed out/seen as wrong). The word “lest” shows a realistic, human fear: *if my deed is seen plainly, I will feel called out or judged.*  
   - Shame in this context (not guilt, which is “I did wrong”; shame is often “I am wrong/bad in this way”) naturally arises when someone sees their own action clearly *contrasting* with how they see themselves (e.g., “I’m a good person,” “I care about fairness,” “I’m honest”) or with the standard they (consciously or unconsciously) hold for themselves or others. Example: Someone who lies to get ahead sees the lie clearly in the light and thinks, “That’s not who I am; that’s wrong,” which feels unsettling. It’s not just “others will judge”; it’s the inner realization: *“This is what I actually did; it doesn’t match my better intention or self-image.”*  
   - *Why this matters:* Shame isn’t necessarily a theological punishment; it’s a common human emotional response when clear truth reveals a gap between action and self-view. The verse’s “lest his deeds should be reproved” directly reflects this realistic fear: *I don’t want this clear sight of my deed to be pointed out to me or others, because it will highlight the mismatch and cause that uncomfortable feeling.*

4. **The desire not to be seen clearly = Choosing darkness (i.e., staying in the vague, self-protective place)**  
   - “Men loved darkness rather than light” (v. 19) and “neither cometh to the light” (v. 20) together show the practical choice: people *prefer* the state of darkness (i.e., staying in the shade of ambiguity, avoiding clear scrutiny) over coming to the light. Why? Because darkness comfortably hides the deed from plain view—both from others *and* from one’s own clear seeing. It preserves the easier self-story (“It wasn’t that bad,” “I had a reason,” “No one really noticed”).  
   - *“Not seen clearly”* means: not seen in full, unvarnished detail; not seen against the clear standard of truth/God. People naturally seek some level of blurring or deflection because clarity can feel risky: it can confirm the uncomfortable shame (“Yes, I did that”), require honesty (“I need to admit it and repair it”), or invite relational consequence. Darkness (the preference for not coming to light) is the everyday human instinct when a clear truth might expose a hidden flaw or wrong deed. It’s not necessarily sinful avoidance in all cases (e.g., needing privacy); it’s specifically about *avoiding clear exposure of one’s own evil deeds when light is available*, as the verse describes.

5. **Putting it together simply (in everyday language):**  
   > When God’s clear truth (the “light”) shows up in the world, it naturally points out what people are actually doing. If someone’s deeds are “evil” (i.e., not aligned with true goodness or God’s good order), seeing those deeds clearly—without excuses or shadows—can feel threatening. It can trigger the inner feeling of shame: “This is what I did; it doesn’t match who I thought I was or what I value.” Because that feeling is uncomfortable, people naturally choose to stay in the “darkness” (i.e., avoid coming to the light, stay vague, not seek clear scrutiny) so they don’t have to face the plain sight of their deed being “reproved” (pointed out). Thus, they “love darkness rather than light” in that moment—not because darkness is good, but because it feels safer for avoiding the clear exposure.  
   > Conversely, the person who “doeth truth” (lives consistently with goodness/God) *does* come to the light. Why? Because for them, their deeds are already “wrought in God” (done in alignment with truth). When light shows them clearly, there is no mismatch or shame; instead, there is peace, clarity, and affirmation: “Yes, this is what I did, and it flows from God’s goodness.” So they welcome the light to make it manifest.

**Why this interpretation fits the passage without overreach:**  
- The verse itself says people avoid light *lest* their deeds be reproved (i.e., shown/pointed out). That directly explains the avoidance: fear of the clear seeing.  
- It says people “love darkness rather than light” because their deeds are evil. “Love” here means a practical, repeated preference (not necessarily an emotional worship of darkness); the reason given is the deed’s character (evil), which naturally conflicts with clear light.  
- It contrasts: evil-doers hate light/avoid it; truth-doers come to light to have deeds made manifest and seen as “wrought in God.” The logic is consistent: clear truth exposes; for the evil-doer, exposure = risk of shame/reproof; for the truth-doer, exposure = affirmation.  
- It avoids adding ideas like “all shame is sinful” or “light always causes guilt”; it simply notes the natural human fear (“lest his deeds should be reproved”) and the corresponding choice (“neither cometh to the light”). This matches how people actually experience truth in daily life.  
- It respects that “light” is symbolic (truth), not literal; thus “hating light” means “avoiding the clear, exposing truth” because it shows the deed plainly.

**In short, answering your question plainly:**  
> Light (God’s clear, exposing truth) would be “hated” (avoided) by people whose deeds are evil because the light inevitably shows those deeds plainly. That plain showing can trigger the natural human feeling of shame—seeing the deed contrast with how they see themselves or what they value. To avoid that uncomfortable feeling (“lest his deeds should be reproved”), people naturally choose darkness (i.e., stay vague, avoid clear scrutiny, not come to the light). Thus, “loving darkness rather than light” reflects the practical, everyday desire not to be seen clearly about one’s own deeds when truth would expose them. Those who live in truth welcome the light because their deeds already align with God, so seeing them clearly brings peace, not shame.

**A gentle note for personal reflection (if helpful):**  
This is a descriptive observation about human experience as revealed in John’s words—not a judgment on anyone. Many people (including all of us at times) have felt the pull to stay in “darkness” when faced with clear feedback about their actions (e.g., avoiding a hard conversation, downplaying a mistake). That pull is natural because clarity can feel risky. John isn’t saying avoidance is always wrong; he’s naming the pattern: *when deeds are at odds with true goodness, clear truth naturally meets resistance because it reveals what was hidden.* The invitation in the verse is simple and gracious: if your deeds are in God (i.e., aligned with truth/goodness), come to the light; it will make your good work visible and confirm it is done rightly. If your deeds are not aligned, the light doesn’t shame you into silence—it invites you (as all people are invited) to see clearly, face the mismatch, and choose the truth. That choice—coming to light—is the alternative to staying in darkness.
