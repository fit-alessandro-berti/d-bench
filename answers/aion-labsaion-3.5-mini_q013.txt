The passage rejects worship so sharply because the problem Amos is addressing isn't an imbalance—it's a fundamental inversion. The worship itself had become the vehicle of the injustice, not its antidote.

**Why not "better balance"?**

Notice that God doesn't say "your worship is lacking" or "add justice alongside your rituals." He says the rituals themselves are intolerable—*"I hate, I despise"*—and commands, *"Take thou away from me the noise of thy songs."* This is categorical rejection, not correction. The reason is that in Amos's Israel (8th century BCE), the religious establishment and the economic exploitation were the *same system*. The wealthy elite who trampled the poor (Amos 2:6–8, 8:4–6) were the same people filling the sanctuaries at Bethel and Gilgal. Their offerings were funded by grain extorted from peasants; their feast days were windows of legal immunity during which debt-servitude and bribery flowed freely. Amos even implies this directly in 5:12—"ye afflict the just, ye take bribes, and ye turn aside the poor... from their right."

So the worship wasn't *despite* the injustice; it was the liturgical celebration and ratification of it. To ask for "balance" would imply the worship was acceptable in kind and merely excessive in degree. Amos's point is that it was corrupt in kind.

**What fraud is exposed?**

Several layers:

1. **Worship as laundering.** The sacrifices functioned as moral laundering—converting wealth extracted unjustly into religious legitimacy. The fat of the peace offerings was the surplus of a rigged economy, offered back to God as if it were devotion. The fraud is treating God as the final recipient of stolen goods and expecting Him to certify the transaction.

2. **Worship as substitution.** The rituals had become a substitute for obedience rather than its expression. By offering more and more elaborate cultic performance, the worshippers implicitly claimed the covenant contract was being kept—when the covenant's core demands (justice for the vulnerable) were being violated. It's the fraud of paying with a currency God never asked for while defaulting on the debt He did.

3. **Worship as anesthetic.** The songs and viols created an atmosphere of peace and divine favor that numbed the conscience. Amos 6 calls these people those "at ease in Zion." The liturgy sedated them against the coming judgment. This is why the music is called "noise"—from God's perspective, the melody is sound without truth, a spiritual anesthetic.

4. **Worship as misrepresentation of God.** By celebrating feasts in God's name while embodying everything God hates, the people were presenting a false God to the world—a deity who blesses exploitation. That's the deepest fraud: idolatry committed through orthodoxy.

**What "judgment" and "righteousness" mean here**

The famous line—*"let judgment run down as waters, and righteousness as a mighty stream"*—uses *mishpat* (just legal and social order) and *tsedaqah* (covenant faithfulness, right relationship). The flood imagery is deliberate: these should be continuous, pervasive, unstoppable—like a wadi in flash flood, like the waters of the sea. Not occasional religious events on a calendar (feasts, solemn assemblies) but a constant social reality. God is saying: I want the river of justice, not the ceremonial ponds you've built beside it.

**The underlying principle**

The passage exposes a permanent temptation of religion: the desire to be devout toward God while remaining unjust toward neighbor, and to have the devoutness *excuse* the injustice. Amos's logic is that these cannot be separated. The prophets consistently say this—Isaiah 1:11–17 ("your hands are full of blood... wash you, make you clean"), Micah 6:8 ("do justly, love mercy"), Hosea 6:6 ("I desired mercy, and not sacrifice"). Amos is the sharpest formulation: worship that coexists with injustice is not imperfect worship; it is *anti*-worship. It offends God more than no worship at all, because it uses His name to sanctify what He abhors.

That's why the rejection is total. The fraud isn't insufficient piety—it's piety deployed as a cover. And a cover, once exposed, isn't repaired by adding more of what it covered. It has to be torn off, and what it was hiding has to be dealt with directly.
