Paul names what love *does not do* because some of the things that pass for love are really ways of defending our pride. Envy turns another person’s good into a loss for us. Boasting makes us the center of the relationship. Irritability treats every inconvenience as a personal offense. And “thinketh no evil,” often translated “keeps no record of wrongs,” challenges the habit of saving up grievances to use against someone later. The negations make love concrete: it matters not only what we feel, but what we refuse to do when we feel slighted, threatened, or overlooked.

That mattered in Corinth, where gifts meant to build up the community had become occasions for rivalry and status. Paul puts patience and kindness ahead of the ability to impress. **The strength he proposes is the freedom not to make everything about yourself.** It takes strength to let someone else shine, to resist the quick satisfaction of retaliation, and to remain kind when kindness costs you something.

That strength is not passivity or gullibility. Love “rejoiceth in the truth,” so it does not have to deny a wrong in order to endure it without becoming vindictive. Paul’s picture is of someone strong enough to face what is true without letting pride or resentment decide how they will respond.
