The opening verse is the exegesis — Luke tells you exactly how to read it before Jesus even speaks:

> *pros tinas tous pepoithotas **epi heautois** hoti eisin dikaioi **kai exouthenountas** tous loipous* — to some who **trusted upon themselves** that they were righteous, **and despised** the rest. [18:9]

Everything else is illustration. The Pharisee is not condemned for one thing on your list. He is condemned for a single complex sin whose anatomy is pride -> comparison -> contempt -> display. False doctrine is the *least* accurate description.

### 1. What does NOT condemn him

That matters, because we instinctively read this anachronistically.

**a) Not false doctrine.** Nothing the Pharisee says about God, Torah, fasting, or tithing is theologically unorthodox by first-century Jewish standards. Pharisees were not proto-Pelagians trying to earn their way to heaven without grace. As E.P. Sanders and later the "New Perspective" showed, mainstream Judaism was *covenantal nomist*: you are brought into covenant by God's mercy; you maintain covenant by obedience.

Fasting twice a week [Mon/Thurs] and tithing *panta hosa ktōmai* — everything I acquire — goes beyond what the Law requires, but it is supererogatory piety, not heresy.

**b) Not that his virtues are fake.** Luke gives us no reason to think he is lying. He probably *does* fast. He probably *does* tithe meticulously. He probably *is* not an extortioner, unjust, adulterer.

This is crucial: Jesus is not contrasting a hypocrite vs. a sincere man. He is contrasting two kinds of sincerity, and saying one kind doesn't justify.

**c) Not prayer itself.** Two men *went up... to pray*. The setting is the Temple, probably at the time of the afternoon Tamid offering. Both are doing exactly what pious Jews should do.

What condemns him is what he does *with* that orthodoxy and virtue.

### 2. What DOES condemn him: A four-fold anatomy of self-righteousness

Think of them as organic stages, not separate options:

**1. Pride [trusting in himself] - the Root**

*pepoithotas epi heautois* is literally "having-conviction upon themselves." His confidence-resting-place has shifted from God's faithfulness to his own record.

He still uses God-language: *"God, I thank thee..."* But grammatically it is revealing. A thanksgiving psalm would thank God *for what God has done*. His thanksgiving is for what *he himself* has done and is not. God is the audience, not the actor.

Chrysostom puts it sharply: "He did not say 'thank you for making me so,' but declared his own virtues as if they had no other source."

That is why Luke's famous ambiguous phrase matters in 18:11:

*ho Pharisaios statheis pros heauton tauta prosēucheto*

It can mean "standing *by himself* he prayed these things" OR "he prayed *these things to himself*." Most scholars think Luke intends the double-meaning: spatially apart for purity, and theologically closed in on himself. Coram Deo becomes coram se.

**2. Comparison - the Mechanism**

Pride cannot sustain itself in a vacuum. It needs a foil.

*"that I am not as other men are... or even as this publican"* 

Notice the move from category to specimen: *other men* -> extortioners, unjust, adulterers -> *this* one right here. The tax-collector ceases to be a person and becomes a prop. He must exist *as lower* so the Pharisee can experience himself *as higher*.

Comparison turns the moral life from a vertical relation [have I loved God with all my heart?] into a horizontal ranking [am I doing better than him?]. Virtue becomes competitive.

**3. Contempt [exouthenountas] - the Fruit**

Luke's introductory word is not mild dislike. *Exoutheneō* means to count as nothing, to annihilate, to treat as zero.

This is the vertical and horizontal Commandments failing simultaneously. In Jewish thought, to despise one made in God's image while praising God in the next breath is incoherence. You cannot love God whom you have not seen if you render nothing the brother whom you have seen. Cf. 1 John 4:20.

His sin is not just thinking well of himself; it is needing to think badly of another to do it.

**4. Virtue as Self-Display - the Perversion**

*"I fast twice in the week, I give tithes of all..."*

Again, fasting and tithing are good. The perversion is instrumental: virtues meant to be

* fasting = hunger for God, solidarity with the poor, discipline of desire
* tithing = acknowledgement that all belongs to God

become exhibits in a self-defense case. 

The Reformation described this as the search for *coram hominibus* — righteousness before humans — smuggled into *coram Deo* — righteousness before God. Modern psychology would say virtue becomes identity-signaling.

He does not *offer* his fasting; he *brandishes* it. That is why Jesus says elsewhere of such prayer: "they have received their reward" Matt. 6:5. If virtue's purpose is to make you admirable, it succeeds — but it cannot justify.

In short: **Pride supplies the premise ("I am righteous"), comparison supplies the method ("righteous-er than"), contempt supplies the attitude toward neighbor, and display supplies the attitude toward God.**

That whole complex is what is not-justified.

### 3. Why is the tax-collector's posture "spiritually truer"?

It is not truer because publicans are generally better people, because self-hate is holy, or because bare emotion justifies. It is truer on three levels:

**a) Epistemologically truer — it conforms to reality.**

*stōs makran, ouk ēthelen oude tous ophthalmous eis ton ouranon eparai*

Standing far off, not willing even to lift eyes to heaven, beating his breast.

Every gesture encodes truth in an honor-shame culture:
* distance = recognizes alienation; he belongs among the unclean in the Court of the Gentiles, not claiming proximity
* lowered eyes = shame/humility; will not seize intimacy
* striking chest = grief, mourning rite; body confesses what tongue confesses

The Pharisee's prayer says, in effect: "God, reality is accurately summarized by my resume." The tax-collector's body says: "God, reality is accurately summarized by my breach with you and others." 

Only one of those is true of every human before perfect holiness. As Augustine comments, the Pharisee was *pleading his worth*, the publican *pleading his wound*; a doctor listens to the second.

**b) Theologically truer — it appeals to the right ground of justification.**

He says exactly seven words in Greek:

*Ho theos, hilasthēti moi tō hamartōlō.*

God, be merciful / make atonement for me the sinner.

*Hilasthēti* is not generic *eleēson* (have pity). It is the verb of *hilasmos/hilastērion* — propitiation, atonement, the mercy-seat covering. See Lev. 16, Rom. 3:25, Heb. 9:5. In the Temple where he stands, blood was offered daily to cover sin precisely because keeping covenant perfectly was impossible.

He asks for nothing based on record: no fasting, no tithes, no comparative decency. He asks for *covering* because he knows he needs it.

And he calls himself "*tō hamartōlō*" — not *a* sinner, but *the* sinner par excellence, the one whose sin is on display. This is not performative self-loathing. It is the refusal to dilute guilt: "I will not compare. There is no 'even as this Pharisee' clause for me."

Justification by definition must be received, not asserted. The Pharisee asserts righteousness; the publican begs for it. Only begging can receive gift.

That is why Luke's verdict is stark:

*katebē houtos dedikaiōmenos... para ekeinon*

*this* man went down justified, *rather than the other*. Not more justified *alongside* the other. The other was not justified at all in that exchange.

**c) Relationally truer — it leaves room for God and neighbor.**

Paradoxically, the man who isolates himself physically makes true relation possible, while the man who performs relation with God makes it impossible.

The tax-collector's prayer has only two characters: God in mercy, and himself in need. No third party dragged in to trip over. By refusing comparison, he leaves judgment of the other to God. He repents *for* himself, not *from* superiority to others.

Humility here is not a virtue added to his record to tip the scales. It is the empty hand that lets mercy be put in it. As the Desert Fathers said, humility is not thinking less *of* yourself, but thinking of yourself *less* — so you can finally see God accurately.

> In Christian tradition:
> * **Catholic:** sees classic *humilitas et contritio*: infusion of grace responding to penitent disposition.
> * **Protestant:** sees *extra nos*: justification by mercy alone through faith, apart from works of law.
> * **Orthodox:** sees *metanoia*: the publican enacts the Jesus Prayer centuries early — Lord have mercy on me a sinner.
>
> All agree despite their differences on mechanism: the posture that is true is the one that is empty of self-justification.

Ultimately Jesus does not teach, "Become bad so you can be humble like a tax-collector." He teaches: whatever your life looks like — fast if you will, tithe if you will — do not turn virtue into a mirror. Use it as a window to God's generosity and as a bridge to neighbor's need.

For "everyone who exalts himself will be humbled, and he who humbles himself will be exalted" — because only one of them is telling the truth.
