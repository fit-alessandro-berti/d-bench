Isaiah is deliberately whole-person in his call. It's not redundancy, it's diagnosis.

> "Let the wicked [rasha] forsake his way [darko], and the unrighteous man [ish aven] his thoughts [machshevotav]"

In Hebrew poetry those two lines are parallel — and together they mean *all* of sin must be left.

**1. The "way" — outward conduct**

*Derekh* is a path, a journey, a formed habit of life. The wicked man doesn't just commit wicked acts, he *walks* in them. It's his direction, his trade routes, his relationships, his injustices, his habits.

To "forsake" — *ya'azov*, to abandon, divorce, leave behind — is stronger than to manage or moderate. Repentance is not "walk better," it's get off that road entirely.

God cares about lived life, not just good intentions.

**2. The "thoughts" — inward imagination**

*Machshevot* are not fleeting ideas. They are plans, schemes, reasonings, fantasies — the inner factory where the outer way is first designed, excused, and enjoyed.

The "man of iniquity" lives first in his mind: his rationalizations for why the wicked way is necessary, his bitter or lustful imaginings, his low, small thoughts *about God himself* — that God won't provide, won't see, won't forgive.

That's why outward reformation is never enough. You can quit a behavior while still cherishing the imagination that created it, and it will grow back. Jesus makes the same point: murder begins in anger, adultery in lust. Isaiah says it 700 years earlier: forsake both the fruit *and* the root.

Repentance therefore means:

* Stop *doing* it, and
* Stop *thinking* your way back into it — surrender the inner narratives that justify it.

**3. Why that makes mercy possible — and abundant**

Notice the order: *Seek... call... forsake... return — and he will have mercy... for he will abundantly pardon.*

The Hebrew is beautiful here. *Virachamehu* — He will mother him with womb-compassion — and *ki-yarbeh lisloach* — literally, "for He will multiply to forgive." Not meager pardon, but multiplied pardon to meet multiplied sin, in act and in thought.

And Isaiah makes a direct link to the next verse:

v.7: forsake... your **way** and your **thoughts**
v.8: "For my **thoughts** are not your **thoughts,** neither are your **ways** my **ways,** saith the Lord."

That's the exchange. We abandon our low ways and low thoughts precisely so we can receive His higher ways and higher thoughts. Chief among His higher thoughts is this shocking one: that a holy God *wants* to abundantly pardon the returning wicked.

Our thoughts say: God is far, mercy is scarce, my sin is too calculating and too habitual. His thought says: Come home, I have already planned mercy.

So Isaiah 55:6-7 doesn't demand perfect self-cleaning before mercy. It demands a full turn — outward and inward — *toward* mercy while He is near.
