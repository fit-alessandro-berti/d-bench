## Luke answers you in verse 9

The interpretive key is the frame verse, which is not decoration but diagnosis. Luke names two faults before the parable begins: those *πεποιθότας ἐφ' ἑαυτοῖς ὅτι εἰσὶν δίκαιοι* — who have settled into the settled confidence that they are righteous — and *ἐξουθενοῦντας τοὺς λοιπούς* — who habitually count the rest as nothing. The verb *ἐξουθενοῦντες* is stronger than "despise": it means to regard as *nothing*. So the parable's own author gives a two-part answer: **misplaced trust** and **contempt**. Your five candidates are not rivals so much as one fault at different depths.

## The five candidates, adjudicated

**False doctrine — no, not in the propositional sense.** Nothing he says is heretical, his address is formally correct ("God, I thank thee"), and the parable never disputes his works. He *really does* fast twice a week — twice the Torah's requirement (the only commanded fast was the Day of Atonement; twice-weekly fasting was voluntary ascetical discipline, cf. Didache 8.1). He *really does* tithe everything he acquires — beyond what the law required. As Jeremias observed, the Pharisee is not a hypocrite; his virtues are genuine. There is a doctrinal distortion, but it is *enacted*, not asserted: righteousness treated as a possession one has rather than a standing one receives.

**Pride — yes, but it must be specified.** Not vanity before an audience — there is no crowd in the temple. It is self-groundedness before God: his righteousness has its center of gravity in himself.

**Comparison — the mechanism, not the root.** But it is structurally essential. His identity is purely differential: he can only state what he *is* by stating what he is *not*. Remove the comparison and the prayer collapses — there is nothing left to say. Paul diagnoses exactly this structure: "measuring themselves by themselves, and comparing themselves among themselves, are not wise" (2 Cor 10:12). And comparative righteousness has a hidden economy: it *requires inferiors*. He needs the tax collector. His standing is parasitic on the other man standing lower.

**Contempt — explicitly charged, and the poison moment of the prayer.** The only other human being he mentions, he mentions as an exhibit: "or even as this publican." He drags his neighbor into his thanksgiving as a foil. Luke 16:14–15 is the twin text: the Pharisees "derided" Jesus, and he answers, "Ye are they which justify yourselves before men; but God knoweth your hearts." Derision and self-justification always travel together.

**Virtue as self-display — a real symptom, but subtler than it looks.** Display to whom? Probably no crowd. The scandal is that the display is made to God *and to himself* — the best-attested reading is *πρὸς ἑαυτὸν*, "to himself" (the variant *περὶ ἑαυτοῦ*, "about himself," is ancient; either way the prayer circles the self). God appears at the head of the sentence and then functions as auditor of a résumé. The tell: **the prayer contains no petition.** He asks for nothing because he needs nothing. His virtues have been converted from gratitude (response to God) into credit (claim on God).

**The root:** righteousness relocated into the self. Once righteousness is a measurable possession, comparison follows automatically (there is no other way to measure), contempt follows (measurement requires inferiors), display follows (the possession must be presented), and doctrine quietly distorts (God becomes the bookkeeper confirming the ledger). Note the parable's most unnerving feature: his moral record may be entirely *clean* — and that is precisely the point. No amount of moral effort escapes this condemnation; the better the record, the more material for the claim. Psalm 143:2 states the axiom the parable enacts: "in thy sight shall no man living be justified."

## Why the publican's posture is truer

First, what it is *not*: not a technique. If lowered eyes acquired justification, breast-beating would simply be the Pharisee's prayer in humble costume, and self-display would have migrated to the other corner of the temple. The posture is treated as truer because it **corresponds to reality on both sides of the relationship**.

Both men stand — standing is ordinary prayer posture; it is not the Pharisee's fault. The differences are distance, eyes, and hands. He stands *μακρόθεν*, far off — mapping his actual condition onto the sacred geography and voluntarily taking the lower seat. He *was not willing* (*οὐκ ἤθελεν*, a refusal, not an inability) even to lift his eyes to heaven, though lifted eyes were the standard posture (Ps 121:1). He beat his breast — *ἔτυππεν*, imperfect: kept striking — the grief-gesture of mourning (Luke 23:48). Before he says a word, his body has preached an accurate sermon about himself. Nothing in this is meritorious. It is *accurate*.

Then five Greek words: *ὁ θεός, ἱλάσθητί μοι τῷ ἁμαρτωλῷ*. Notice:

- The circuit is open — addressed to God. The Pharisee's was addressed "to himself."
- One petition, no defense. The Pharisee's: exhibits, no petition.
- *ἱλάσθητί μοι* is not generic kindness: the verb is from the propitiation/atonement root (the noun *ἱλαστήριον* is the mercy-seat, Heb 9:5; Rom 3:25). He asks God to do what the temple exists to do — be propitious to a sinner who cannot supply his own atonement. He asks God to be *what God is* (Exod 34:6); the Pharisee asks God to confirm what the Pharisee already believes about himself.
- *τῷ ἁμαρτωλῷ* — with the definite article: "me, *the* sinner," as though the category had one member. The Pharisee pointed at "this publican"; the publican points only at himself.

And here is the deepest irony in the parable: **every factual claim the Pharisee makes is true — he does fast, he does tithe — and his prayer is existentially false. The tax collector makes no factual claims at all, and his five words are existentially the whole truth.** Fact and truth come apart, and the verdict falls on the side of truth.

The verdict word is chosen with care: *κατέβη οὗτος δεδικαιωμένος* — he went down *justified*. Forensic, perfect, passive: a standing constituted by a verdict already rendered — done *to* him, not achieved *by* him. The frame verse said the audience trusted themselves that they *were* righteous (self-designation); only one man left the temple righteous by *declaration*. Possession versus verdict. And he goes straight down to his house: no probation, no prescribed restitution — restitution is a different story (Zacchaeus, Luke 19), and there it is the *fruit* of welcome, not its price. Luke has already sketched the pattern: at John's baptism "the publicans justified God... but the Pharisees and lawyers rejected the counsel of God" (7:29–30). In Luke's theology, who ends up justified by God tracks who first justified God — who accepted God's valuation rather than presenting their own.

## The trap

The parable is self-involving. Its invitation is to identify with the tax collector, and its danger is that the identification instantly regenerates the Pharisee: *"God, I thank thee that I am not as other men are... or even as this Pharisee."* Anyone who uses the parable to classify someone else has just prayed its condemned prayer from the far corner of the temple. This is why the tradition never treated the publican's five words as a one-off lesson but made them the seed of unceasing prayer (the Jesus Prayer: "Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me, a sinner"). The posture survives every century precisely because it is not a posture but a truth: the refusal to claim more than one is, joined to the refusal to ask for less than mercy.

To compress the whole answer: the Pharisee is condemned by the root named in v. 9 — trust transferred from God's mercy to his own righteousness — of which comparison is the mechanism, contempt the fruit, self-display the liturgy, and the implied doctrine the rationalization. The tax collector is vindicated because his body and his words tell the truth about both parties in the transaction: himself a sinner; God propitious. In that courtroom, the only accurate act is an appeal.
