All five elements are present in the elder brother’s outburst, functioning less like separate options and more like layers of the same spiritual condition. Looking closely at the text—and the father’s gentle correction—reveals how those layers build from surface emotions down to the root problem.

### 1. The Surface Symptoms: Anger and Envy
**Anger** (v. 28) is the immediate reaction that keeps him outside the house, and **envy** is its close companion. He measures his brother’s "fatted calf" against the "kid" (young goat) he claims he was never given (vv. 29–30). Yet envy isn't the deepest issue here, because as the father points out in v. 31, *"all that I have is thine."* Having already distributed the younger son’s share earlier in the chapter, the remaining estate already belongs to the elder brother. He isn't actually deprived; he simply doesn't value what he has until he sees his brother celebrated.

### 2. The Moral Framework: Pride in Obedience and Wounded Fairness
Beneath his anger lies a strict, transactional worldview built on **pride in obedience** and **wounded fairness**:
> *"Lo, these many years do I serve thee, neither transgressed I at any time thy commandment..."* (v. 29)

He views his relationship with his father not as family intimacy, but as a contract. Notably, the Greek word translated "serve" (*douleuō*) literally means to serve as a slave or bondsman. In his mind, obedience is currency meant to earn favor and eventual rewards—and even then, he wants his reward *"that I might make merry with my friends,"* apart from the father. From a purely transactional standpoint, his sense of wounded fairness makes total sense: if rewards are wages for good behavior, paying a prodigal more than a dutiful worker is unjust.

### 3. The Deepest Problem: Discomfort with Mercy (Rooted in a False View of the Father)
If we ask what triggers all of the above, it is **discomfort with mercy itself**—specifically because unmerited mercy exposes and threatens a merit-based identity.

* **Mercy breaks his system:** If the younger son can squander the family living and still receive the fatted calf simply by coming home, then the elder brother’s decades of dutiful, joyless rule-keeping lose their bargaining power. Grace feels like an insult to someone trying to earn their place.
* **Disdain for the forgiven:** He cannot even bring himself to call the prodigal "my brother," distancing himself with *"this thy son"* (v. 30). The father gently corrects this in v. 32 by calling him *"this thy brother."* To accept mercy, the elder brother would have to accept kinship with the sinner—the exact offense that prompted Jesus to tell this parable to the Pharisees and scribes who murmured, *"This man receiveth sinners, and eateth with them"* (Luke 15:2).
* **Blindness to grace:** Ultimately, the father’s reply in v. 31 (*"Son, thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine"*) diagnoses the root cause behind his discomfort with mercy. The elder brother doesn't realize that **he has been living off his father’s grace the whole time, too.** Because he relates to his father like a hired servant earning a goat rather than a beloved son sharing a home, he can neither enjoy his own inheritance nor rejoice when grace is given to someone else.

### Summary
* **Anger** and **envy** are the visible symptoms.
* **Pride in obedience** and **wounded fairness** are the moral logic fueling those symptoms.
* **Discomfort with mercy**—born from mistaking sonship for servitude—is his deepest problem. He cannot enter the party because he refuses to live in a household run on grace rather than scorekeeping.
