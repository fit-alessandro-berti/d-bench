Pride is linked to collapse because, in this proverb, it is not only a moral fault but a failure of sight. A haughty spirit misreads its own strength, other people, and the limits of the situation. Destruction is not added afterward as a penalty; it follows because the proud person keeps acting on a distorted picture until reality corrects it.

The sequence matters. Pride goes *before* destruction, and a haughty spirit *before* a fall. Wisdom literature treats arrogance as a disposition that arrives early and does its damage by shutting down correction. The proud do not merely feel superior. They stop taking counsel, stop noticing warning signs, and stop locating themselves accurately among others. That is the blindness: not ignorance of facts in general, but resistance to any fact that would lower the self. The fall is what happens when an inflated self-estimate finally meets an uncooperative world.

The contrast with “the lowly” sharpens the psychology. Humility here is not self-contempt. It is a willingness to stand with people who have no spoil to divide—to accept a lower place, shared vulnerability, and the kind of company that does not flatter. “Dividing the spoil with the proud” is the alternative moral world: advantage taken in alliance with arrogance, status purchased by proximity to those who extract. The proverb judges that bargain worse, even when it pays.

So arrogance, on this reading, is a social and epistemic habit as much as a feeling. It needs the proud as partners and cannot bear the lowly as companions, because the lowly expose limits. Humility keeps a person near those limits, and therefore nearer to reality. Pride protects the self-image by abandoning that nearness—and the protection is what makes the collapse unsurprising.
