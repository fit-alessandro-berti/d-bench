The verse treats love of the visible brother as a test because **love for God must take concrete form in our treatment of people**. In 1 John, these are not two unrelated loves: “We love him, because he first loved us” (4:19). To receive God’s love is to be drawn into his way of loving.

The contrast between *seen* and *unseen* exposes how easily religious language can evade reality. An invisible God can be praised without confronting us with another person’s needs, faults, or demands. A visible brother does confront us. Loving him requires patience, generosity, forgiveness, and sometimes costly action. If someone refuses that concrete love, his claim to love God has lost its practical credibility.

The point is not simply that visible things are easier to love. It is that **the person before us tests whether we love God himself or merely an agreeable idea of God**.

The self-deception being exposed is therefore a kind of divided spirituality:

- **Mistaking profession for reality:** “I love God” becomes a substitute for actually living in love.
- **Mistaking religious feeling for moral transformation:** devotion, enthusiasm, or spiritual experiences are treated as proof of love while hatred remains untouched.
- **Imagining that devotion excuses contempt:** someone believes loyalty to God can coexist with dehumanizing a person God calls him to love.

Calling such a person “a liar” identifies a contradiction between his claim and his life; it need not mean that he consciously recognizes the contradiction. He may sincerely believe his own profession.

“Brother” here refers especially to a fellow member of the Christian community, though the principle has wider implications for our neighbors. And love does not mean approving wrongdoing or abandoning necessary boundaries.

**The test is not whether we feel warmly toward everyone, but whether our supposed love for God makes us willing to seek another person’s good rather than cherish hatred.**
