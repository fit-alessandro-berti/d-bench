In Luke 18:9-14, the introduction in verse 9 gives the interpretive key for the whole parable:

> "to certain which trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and despised others"

Jesus is not condemning the Pharisee for what he *did*, but for *why* and *how* he related his deeds to God and to other people.

### 1. What condemns the Pharisee?

It is not one of those things alone, but all of them together as expressions of **self-righteousness**. Traditional exegesis sees it this way:

**a) Not false doctrine or false facts -** The Pharisee likely *did* fast twice a week and *did* tithe scrupulously. Fasting was only required once a year on the Day of Atonement, so twice a week was supererogatory piety. Tithing "of all that I possess" went beyond the Law. His problem is not that he is lying about his virtue.

**b) Pride - "trusted in themselves that they were righteous"** - The Greek is `pepoithotas eph' heautois hoti eisin dikaioi` - to have confidence *upon* themselves as the ground of their righteousness. He treats his observance as a claim God must honor, rather than as obedience or gratitude.

**c) Comparison and contempt - "and despised others" - `exouthenountas`** - literally to count as nothing, to treat with contempt. His prayer is built on comparison: "I am not as other men are... or even as this publican." His identity as righteous depends on someone else being unrighteous.

**d) The use of virtue as self-display - "prayed thus with himself"** - The phrase can be translated "prayed *to himself*" or "prayed *thus within himself*." Either way the point is that though addressed to God - "God, I thank thee" - the prayer never actually reaches God. It is self-talk, self-congratulation in God's presence. Thanksgiving becomes a vehicle for self-exaltation.

In short: Virtue itself is not condemned. What is condemned is turning virtue into a ground for justification and a ground for contempt. The Pharisee uses prayer not to seek God, but to display himself to God and to distinguish himself from his neighbor.

### 2. Why is the tax collector's posture treated as "justified"?

The publican - a tax collector for Rome - would have been seen by that audience as ritually impure, dishonest, and a collaborator. He offers no resume.

His posture expresses the opposite of the Pharisee's in four ways:

**a) Distance and shame - "standing afar off, would not lift up so much as his eyes unto heaven, but smote upon his breast"** - This is the bodily language of mourning and unworthiness in the ancient Near East. He does not claim access; he acknowledges he has no claim.

**b) No comparison -** He does not compare himself to the Pharisee or anyone else. He does not say "I am better because I am humble." He only speaks of himself before God.

**c) Awareness of sin - "God be merciful to me a sinner"** - More literally, "God be *propitiated* to me *the* sinner" - `hilastheti moi to hamartolo`. He asks not for reward for virtue, but for atonement/mercy. He acknowledges a need that only God can meet.

**d) Appeal to mercy, not merit -** The verb `hilaskomai` is the language of the sacrificial system - to make atonement. He asks for what the temple was actually for.

That is why Jesus concludes: "this man went down to his house justified rather than the other."

In the logic of the parable, justification is not earned by moral achievement and then displayed, but received as a gift by one who knows he needs it. The principle Jesus states elsewhere and repeats here is:

> "for every one that exalteth himself shall be abased; and he that humbleth himself shall be exalted."

The Pharisee exalts himself, even while using religious language. The tax collector humbles himself and appeals to mercy. Within the theology of the parable, the second posture is "truer" because it corresponds to reality as God sees it - that all stand in need of mercy, and that mercy cannot be demanded as a debt for virtue.
