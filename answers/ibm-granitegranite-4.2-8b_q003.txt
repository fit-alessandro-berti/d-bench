> **Why the summary feels "demanding":**  
> Micah 6:8 is short, but its brevity makes the moral weight feel immediate and personal. It doesn't offer vague ideals or distant goals; it states *exactly* what God requires *of "O man" in daily life*: "to do justly," "to love mercy," and "to walk humbly with thy God." Because it names concrete actions ("do," "love," "walk") tied directly to God's revealed good ("He hath shewed thee what is good"), there is no hidden clause, no "when convenient" exception. The phrase "what doth the Lord require of thee" frames it as a direct, personal summons. For someone aware of God's standard (as the verse assumes), hearing these three precise verbs can feel demanding because they touch the core of daily choices—how we treat others (justice), how we respond with compassion (mercy), and how consistently we align our identity and walk with God (humility). It resists easy sidestepping (e.g., "I’ll do one or two" or "I’ll do it perfectly later"); it presents all three as *simultaneously* required for the life God shows as good.

> **How justice, mercy, and humility correct (i.e., balance, check, and deepen) one another:**  
> These three are not sequential steps, isolated virtues, or hierarchical ranks (justice > mercy > humility). Instead, they are interdependent *correctives* that prevent the excesses of the others and uphold the fullness of God’s good for human life. They "correct" one another in this practical, relational way:
>
> 1. **Justice corrects mercy from becoming passive or enabling harm:** Strict, unbending justice *without* mercy can harden hearts, ignore context, punish without restoring, and miss the brokenness behind the wrongdoing (e.g., harsh punishment for a poor person's minor theft out of principle alone). **Mercy**, without grounding in justice, risks overlooking legitimate boundaries, enabling repeated harm, or failing to uphold fair standards (e.g., always forgiving abuse without addressing safety or accountability). **Thus, humility** (recognizing God's perspective on both human brokenness *and* moral order) helps discern *when* and *how* mercy is appropriately applied *within* just boundaries. Humility prevents both cold rigidity *and* naive permissiveness; it asks, "What does restoration truly require? What protects the vulnerable *and* honors the person's dignity?"
>
> 2. **Mercy corrects justice from becoming harsh or abstract:** Rigid, purely procedural justice (focusing only on rules, penalties, or logical consistency) can miss the relational pain, situational need, or underlying cause of wrong (e.g., applying a fine without considering a family crisis). **Mercy**, when grounded in humility, invites us to see the human story behind the violation and seek repair alongside accountability. **Thus, humility** helps us pause the purely legalistic impulse, listen deeply, and ask, "What does healing and restored relationship look like here?" Humility prevents justice from becoming coldly punitive or overly formal; it ensures justice reflects compassion for the offender *and* the victim.
>
> 3. **Humility corrects both justice and mercy from becoming self-focused or self-righteous:**  
> *   Justice done *from* a place of arrogance ("I know best what is fair") risks overlooking others' perspectives and truthfully assessing context.  
> *   Mercy offered *from* a place of superiority ("I am so compassionate") can subtly pressure others to accept less than they need or avoid setting necessary boundaries.  
> **Humility** ("walk humbly with thy God") anchors all action in recognizing our finite, sinful, and dependent place before God and others. It constantly asks: "Am I acting *with* God's perspective on goodness and fairness, or am I acting *as if* I have final authority on what is just or merciful?" Humility prevents justice from becoming a tool of dominance and mercy from becoming patronizing or dismissive of legitimate needs. It ensures we seek God's wisdom (the ultimate standard revealed) in *how* to apply justice and mercy rightly, rather than relying on our own quick judgments.
>
> **In simple, practical balance:**  
> - When tempted to be *strictly* just but *unmerciful* (e.g., "They broke the rule, so full penalty"), humility prompts us to ask: "What need or brokenness might explain this? How can I seek restoration *alongside* accountability?" (Mercy corrects excessive rigidity; humility guides discernment).  
> - When tempted to be *generously* merciful but *unjust* (e.g., "Let it go; don't enforce the rule"), humility prompts us to ask: "Does this honor the rule's purpose? Does it protect the vulnerable or the integrity of the community? What fair, restorative step is needed?" (Justice corrects excessive permissiveness; humility guides fair discernment).  
> - When acting from pride (wanting to be seen as "just" or "merciful"), humility prompts us to pause and seek God's view first ("Walk humbly with thy God"), ensuring our motive aligns with love for God and neighbor, not self-glorification.

> **Why this answers "correct one another":**  
> Each virtue *exposes the danger* of overemphasizing the other two without it. Justice risks hardness; mercy risks naivety; humility risks arrogance or passivity. By constantly asking the humility-rooted question ("What does God, through wisdom, require *here*, for *all* involved?"), we *correctly apply* justice and mercy *in balance*. Humility is not a fourth virtue added *on top*; it is the integrative, grounding posture that ensures justice is compassionate and mercy is fair. They mutually reinforce the everyday, relational practice of living out God's revealed good. Thus, the seemingly simple summary is demanding precisely because it requires constant, humble attention to *how* all three work together in concrete daily choices—not just professing one or the other. The demand is real, but it is also manageable precisely *through* the corrective, clarifying role of humility.
