**Ephesians 4:31‑32 (ESV)**  

> “Let **all bitterness**, and wrath, and anger, and clamor, and evil speaking, be put away from you, with all malice:  
> And be ye **kind** one to another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, even as God for Christ’s sake hath forgiven you.”

---

## 1. Why are the negatives listed right next to the positives?

| Negative item | What it represents | How it connects to the next line |
|---------------|-------------------|-----------------------------------|
| **Bitterness, wrath, anger** – raw emotions | Feelings that arise when we allow ourselves to be hurt, rejected, or wronged. | These emotions give rise to the other items. |
| **Clamor** (noisy, disruptive speech) & **Evil speaking** (speech that attacks or sows discord) | External manifestations of the same unrest. | They are the outward expressions of the inner turmoil. |
| **“Put away … with all malice”** | A command to eradicate them *without* any intention of harming anyone else. | Sets up the expectation that the remedy will be **goodwill‑driven**. |
| **Be kind, tenderhearted, forgiving** | The opposite attitude: love, humility, reconciliation. | The contrast is explicit: *remove the poison* → *cultivate the cure*. |

Paul does not treat the list as three separate topics; he groups them because they share a common origin—**a failure to love and forgive**—and because each fuels the others. If bitterness remains, it naturally produces wrath, anger, and eventually noisy, hostile speech. By putting them together, he forces the believer to see the whole web of damage before trying to fix individual pieces.

---

## 2. What kind of thing is “bitterness” here?

### a. **Feeling vs. habit**
- **Feeling:** A momentary surge of resentment when we hear a slight insult or witness injustice.  
- **Habit:** A repeated pattern built over time when we choose to store the hurt rather than release it.  
- **Posture:** A deliberate stance of the heart that says, “I deserve this pain.”  

In the New Testament, bitterness is usually presented as a **choice**—a posture adopted under pressure. Ephesians 4:27–29 already lists “the fruit of the flesh” (including envy, jealousy, etc.) and urges us to “turn from such things.” So here, bitterness is not merely a fleeting mood; it is a **chosen attitude** that we have allowed to take root.

### b. **Contagious?**
- The word *clamor* (“shouting”) and *evil speaking* show that bitterness can spill into the world.  
- When someone speaks with bitterness, his words can **influence others**, turning neutral conversation into conflict.  
- Yet the text never says “bitterness spreads like a virus”; it simply notes that its effects include “clamor” and “evil speaking.” This hints that the **attitude itself is transmittable**: if we keep harbingering resentment, we encourage others to do the same.

**Conclusion:** The most accurate reading is that bitterness is a **chosen posture/attitude** that can become a habit and, through speech and action, become contagious. It is less a transient emotion than a settled disposition that shapes how we relate to others.

---

## 3. The link to forgiveness

- **Forgiveness** is presented as the mirror image of bitterness. God forgave us “even as He has forgiven you,” showing that true reconciliation begins within us.  
- When we **forgive**, we deliberately reject the posture of holding a grudge. The parallel “even as God…hath forgiven you” tells us to **model** that divine mercy in our own lives.  
- Thus, the placement of “forgive one another” immediately follows the call to “put away bitterness”—they are paired as complementary responses to the same problem.

---

## 4. Putting it all together

1. **Identify the source:** Unresolved hurt creates a habit of bitterness.  
2. **Recognize the ripple effect:** That habit generates wrath, anger, loud arguments, and hostile speech.  
3. **Choose the opposite:** Kindness, tenderness, and genuine forgiveness break the cycle.  
4. **Act without malice:** The instruction “with all malice” reminds us that the removal must be done out of love, not revenge.

By clustering the negatives, Paul makes the **necessity of total change** obvious: you cannot simply “think about” being better; you must **eliminate** the very qualities that feed them. The result is a holistic transformation—first purging the internal posture, then expressing it outwardly through kind words and forgiving behavior.

--- 

**Bottom line:**  
- Bitterness is best understood as a **chosen posture/attitude** (often reinforced by habit) that can become contagious through speech and conduct.  
- Its presence invites wrath, anger, clamor, and evil speaking; therefore, it is placed alongside those outcomes to stress the interconnectedness of the problem and its solution.  
- The call to “be kind, tenderhearted, forgiving” offers the remedy, echoing God’s own example of forgiveness.
