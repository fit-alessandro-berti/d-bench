Enemy-love is difficult to take seriously because it violates nearly every ordinary assumption we have about morality: that love is earned, that enemies are objects of judgment rather than mercy, that justice must first punish, and that self-protection is the first obligation. It sounds either too weak to be true—mere sentimentalism—or too strong to be livable—something only saints, martyrs, or the naïve attempt.

But in Matthew 5:44–45, Jesus is not offering a soft-feeling moral slogan. He is issuing a radical claim about what moral strength actually is.

## 1. Why enemy-love is hard to take seriously

### It seems to contradict justice

When someone curses, hates, mistreats, or persecutes us, our first moral instinct is often not “love them,” but “stop them,” “expose them,” or “punish them.” To be told to bless and pray for such people can feel like an insult to justice itself.

The danger is that “love your enemies” can be misread as:

- “Do not resist evil.”
- “Let yourself be abused.”
- “Pretend harm did not happen.”
- “Sacrifice yourself for the comfort of your oppressor.”

But that is not the only possible meaning of the text. Jesus is not saying, “Approve of evil.” He is saying, “Do not let evil define your soul. Do not repay hatred with hatred. Do not let the enemy turn you into what the enemy already is.”

That is not sentimental. It is unsettlingly serious.

### It refuses to dehumanize the other

Human beings find it psychologically easier to hate someone by reducing them to their worst actions. The enemy becomes “the monster,” “the wicked,” “the unjust one,” “the persecutor.” That reduction feels protective, even righteous.

Enemy-love resists that reduction.

To pray for an enemy is to acknowledge: this person is not merely an obstacle, not merely a threat, not merely a name in a list of offenses. They are a person made in God’s image, however distorted, however dangerous, however wounded.

That is difficult because it refuses to give us the moral comfort of contempt.

### It threatens our identity as victims

Sometimes the deepest wound is not only what the enemy did to us, but what we became afterward: angry, defensive, suspicious, bitter, hardened.

Enemy-love challenges the identity of the injured person as someone entitled—perhaps rightly so in some sense—to resentment. It suggests that the final authority over our soul should not be the one who harmed us. That is psychologically threatening. It can feel as though it asks us to surrender our rightful grievance.

Jesus does not deny grievance. But He redirects the response away from vengeance and toward prayer.

### It appears impractical in a dangerous world

People rightly ask: “How can I love someone who is actively harming me?”

If “love” means emotional warmth, it sounds absurd. If it means trust, it can be foolish. If it means allowing continued abuse, it can be harmful. If it means refusing to defend yourself or others, it can be unethical.

That is why the harder, truer meaning of enemy-love must be distinguished from naïve passivity.

## 2. Is enemy-love sentimental?

No—at least not in the biblical sense.

Sentimentalism treats love as a feeling. Jesus’ command does not first say, “Feel warm toward your enemy.” It says, “Love. Bless. Do good. Pray.” These are actions, disciplines, intentions of the will.

You may not feel benevolence toward someone who has betrayed you. But you can refuse to wish their destruction. You can stop feeding yourself revenge fantasies. You can pray, “God, if it is possible, turn this person from their harm. And if that does not happen, keep me from becoming hateful.”

That is not sentimental. Sentimentality is cheap. Enemy-love is costly.

Jesus does not say, “Your enemies are harmless.” He knows people “despitefully use” and “persecute.” The context is hostility, injury, and powerlessness. So enemy-love is not for comfortable people with no enemies. It is for people under threat, insulted, wounded, and tempted to hatred.

That makes it the opposite of soft.

## 3. Is enemy-love strategic?

Partly, yes—but not only.

There are strategic fruits of enemy-love. It can:

- break cycles of revenge,
- expose injustice without becoming consumed by it,
- preserve one’s own humanity,
- disarm hostility,
- create space for conversion,
- strengthen communities against bitterness.

Praying for enemies is strategically important because it refuses to let the enemy occupy the center of your interior life. Hatred may wound the hated person, but it devastates the hater. Prayer reorders the soul.

However, if enemy-love is reduced to strategy, it becomes manipulable. It can turn into:

- a way to feel morally superior to your enemies,
- a tactic to disarm them while secretly seeking advantage,
- a spiritual performance to avoid confrontation,
- a means of maintaining unjust relationships because “doing good” is easier than demanding justice.

So enemy-love has strategic consequences, but it is not merely strategy. Strategy asks, “What will this accomplish?” Biblical love asks, “What is good for the other, before God?”

The two are not identical.

## 4. Is it a deeper form of moral strength?

Yes. This seems closest to its meaning.

Enemy-love is not weakness. Weakness cannot love its enemies because weakness is overwhelmed by fear, bitterness, or passivity. The weak may obey, submit, or pretend, but the command here requires inner sovereignty.

Jesus is calling for a strength that is:

- self-controlled in the face of provocation,
- truthful without being cruel,
- merciful without denying justice,
- resistant without becoming hateful,
- willing to suffer injury rather than inflict unjust injury,
- able to seek the good of someone who does not seek your good.

That is moral strength of a very high order.

The “sun rising on the evil and the good” and “rain on the just and unjust” show that God’s kindness is not merely emotional. It is cosmic benevolence. God gives life, sustenance, seasons, common goods even to those who reject Him. That is not approval. It is grace.

To “be children of your Father” means to imitate this kind of divine goodness: not by pretending evil is good, but by refusing to make your love contingent on the enemy’s worthiness.

## 5. The crucial distinction: love is not the same as approval, trust, or passive acceptance

Enemy-love must not become doormat theology.

The text does not say:

- “Let your enemy abuse you.”
- “Never say no.”
- “Never resist.”
- “Never demand justice.”
- “Never set boundaries.”
- “Never report harm.”

Biblical love seeks the good of the other—but it does not require you to cooperate with the other’s evil.

Sometimes loving an enemy means refusing to be their victim. Sometimes it means resisting them publicly. Sometimes it means bringing injustice to light. Sometimes it means saying, “What you did was wrong, and it cannot continue.”

Indeed, praying for them may make resistance more honest, because it keeps us from dehumanizing them.

A better definition might be:

> Enemy-love is the disciplined refusal to wish destruction upon one who has harmed you, while seeking their repentance, your safety, others’ protection, and God’s justice—without being ruled by hatred.

That is neither sentimental nor merely strategic. It is a form of moral strength that requires self-denial, truthfulness, and courage.

## 6. Why it remains difficult even for serious believers

Because it requires living from a future judgment rather than present retaliation.

Jesus does not say, “Your enemies will never pay.” The Sermon on the Mount assumes God is the judge. Enemy-love is difficult because it asks the injured person to trust God rather than settle accounts.

That is not cowardice. It is faith.

It says:

- “I do not get to become my own god.”
- “I do not get to finish this person off morally.”
- “I will not let hatred make my soul its home.”
- “I will not repay evil by becoming more evil.”

That is why it is often mistaken for weakness: it refuses the world’s preferred form of strength, which is domination, exposure, vengeance, or annihilation of the enemy.

## 7. A possible summary

Enemy-love is hard to take seriously because it appears to demand what human nature most resists: goodness toward those who are hostile to us.

It is not sentimental, because it does not feel comfortable or weak; it is often painful, costly, and counterinstinctive.

It is not merely strategic, because its aim is not first to win advantage but to become like God.

It is a deeper moral strength: the strength to remain human under dehumanizing treatment, to refuse to make the enemy the lord of your soul, and to seek good without surrendering to evil.

In short: enemy-love is not the denial of danger. It is the refusal to let danger corrupt you. It is not approval of oppression. It is the decision, before God, not to be defeated by hatred. And in Matthew’s vision, that decision is not naïveté—it is a mark of true moral maturity.
