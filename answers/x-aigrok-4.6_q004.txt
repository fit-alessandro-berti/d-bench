**Wholehearted reliance on God rather than autonomous self-reliance.** The verse calls for complete, undivided trust in the Lord (with “all thine heart”) that refuses to treat unaided human reasoning as the final court of appeal. It is not a command to stop thinking, studying, planning, or using evidence; it is a command to refuse to *lean* (rest the full weight of life, decisions, and interpretation) on one’s own finite, fallen understanding as if it were sufficient or ultimate.

### Humble self-distrust
This is the posture the text commends:
- Human understanding is real and valuable (Proverbs itself is a book of wisdom, observation, and instruction). It is simply not *sovereign*. We are limited in knowledge, prone to self-deception, and affected by sin (cf. Prov 14:12; 16:25; Jer 17:9).
- Therefore the wise person acknowledges God *in all ways*—seeking His revealed will, submitting plans and interpretations to Him, and expecting His direction. This is compatible with vigorous use of the mind: “The fear of the Lord is the beginning of knowledge” (Prov 1:7) and “of wisdom” (9:10). Jesus later commands love of God with the *mind* as well as the heart (Matt 22:37).
- The promised result is divine guidance of one’s paths, not the abandonment of reason.

This is the same distinction found throughout Scripture: “Trust in the Lord… and do not lean on your own understanding” sits alongside repeated calls to get wisdom, seek counsel, and test everything (Prov 4:5–7; 11:14; 1 Thess 5:21). Humble distrust of *self* as final authority is the opposite of intellectual sloth.

### Mere anti-intellectualism
That is *not* what the verse teaches. Anti-intellectualism treats the intellect itself as suspect or unnecessary, dismisses learning, evidence, or careful reasoning, and often substitutes emotion, tradition, or slogans for thought. Proverbs 3:5–6 does not license that. It warns against *autonomy* (“my own understanding” as the last word), not against understanding. God is the giver of both revelation and the rational faculties that receive and apply it. Rejecting the latter in the name of “faith” is a distortion, not obedience.

In short: the trust required is *dependent* trust—using every gift of mind and observation while refusing to make the self the measure of all things. That is humility, not anti-intellectualism.
