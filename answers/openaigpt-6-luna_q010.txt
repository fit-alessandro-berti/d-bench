The power praised here is **not the power to compel, but the power to give oneself**. Christ’s greatness is not denied; the passage begins with his divine status. Yet he does not use that status for advantage. He takes the servant’s form, accepts human vulnerability, and remains obedient even to death on a cross. His power appears as freely chosen service and costly faithfulness.

That combination can feel more unsettling than open domination because domination is familiar: it announces its terms and can be resisted as domination. Humility joined to greatness refuses that script. The one who could claim privilege does not; the one who could command serves. That choice exposes how often power protects itself, and it makes the question harder to evade: if greatness can take the form of service, what justifies our own grasping for status?

The passage does not praise weakness for its own sake. It presents a strength that does not need to prove itself by ruling over others—and whose self-giving becomes a judgment on domination.
