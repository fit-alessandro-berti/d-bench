The verse pairs them tightly because, in the Greek behind this text, the two are not just neighboring ideas but opposing forces with a kind of causal gravity: bitterness is what happens when forgiveness is refused, and forgiveness is what loosens bitterness’s grip.

A few things are worth noticing.

**1. Bitterness here is not merely a passing emotion.**  
The first verb is an imperative—*put away*—and it targets a cluster: bitterness, wrath, anger, clamor, evil speaking, malice. These are not isolated moods so much as a family of corrosive attitudes and behaviors. Bitterness functions as the underlying tendency that the rest emanate from. It is closer to a settled disposition than a spike of feeling.

**2. It is a posture, and habits can harden into posture.**  
Bitterness is what grows when hurt is nurse-ged rather than released. It becomes a lens through which others are read—everyone eventually becomes the one who wronged you. That is why it belongs with “all malice”: it is not just a feeling but a stance that interprets and shapes the world. The text treats it as something to be actively removed, which implies it is at least partly chosen, even if it began as wound.

**3. Forgiveness is the direct counterforce.**  
Forgiving does not mean pretending the harm did not happen or that it did not matter. In this context, forgiveness is the deliberate refusal to let bitterness become the reigning posture. It is a decision to release the debt and refuse revenge, grounded in the believer’s own experience of being forgiven by God “for Christ’s sake.” The phrase “even as” makes forgiveness both the motive and the pattern: because God has forgiven, the forgiven are to forgive.

**4. Bitterness is contagious, but forgiveness is the cure.**  
You ask whether the text treats bitterness as more than a feeling or habit. It does—bitterness is portrayed as infectious and self-sustaining. It spreads from the wounded person to relationships, from grudge to contempt, from malice to evil speaking. Forgiveness, by contrast, is the remedy that interrupts the cycle. That is why the two are placed so directly against each other: the text is not just listing virtues but showing a battle between a contagion that hardens and a forgiveness that releases.

So the short answer: bitterness is being treated as a **settled, contagious posture**—one that begins with a feeling but becomes a habit and then a chosen way of holding onto injury—and forgiveness is placed directly opposite it as the freeing counteraction.
