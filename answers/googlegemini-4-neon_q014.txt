At first glance, most readers instinctively flip Jesus’s logic around and hear him say, *“Where your heart is, there you will put your treasure.”* That reading makes treasure purely diagnostic—a mirror that **reveals** what we already love. 

While there is truth to that, the actual sequence and grammar of the text point in the opposite direction: **treasure primarily trains and pulls the heart.**

---

### 1. Does treasure reveal the heart, or train it?

Notice two clues right in the wording of the passage:

* **The logic of the command ("For..."):** In verses 19–20, Jesus gives an actionable command about behavior (*"Lay not up... But lay up"*). Verse 21 begins with *"For"*—it is the *reason* to obey the command. If treasure only revealed an already-fixed heart, commanding someone to relocate their treasure would merely address a symptom. Instead, Jesus tells his listeners to move their treasure *in order to* relocate their hearts.
* **The shift in tense ("is" → "will be"):** Jesus says, *"Where your treasure **is** [present], there **will** your heart **be** [future] also."* The heart follows the treasure like iron filings follow a magnet. 

In other words, treasure does **reveal** the heart at any given moment—you can look at your bank statements, calendar, and anxieties and get an honest diagnosis of your attachments. Yet Jesus’s deeper insight is formative: **you do not have to wait for your heart to feel heavenly before you invest in heaven.** Because the heart tracks our investments, deliberately placing your resources in eternal things actively rewires your desires.

---

### 2. How possession, attention, and love become tied together

In biblical thought, the "heart" (*kardia*) is not merely the seat of emotion; it is the whole inner control center—mind, attention, will, and affection combined. Matthew 6:19–21 shows how possession captures that entire control center in a single chain reaction:

1. **Possession creates gravity.**
   To "lay up treasure" is to pour your finite life—your labor, time, wealth, and hopes—into a specific storehouse. The moment you invest in something, you have "skin in the game." You are no longer a neutral observer of it; its fate is now tied to yours.

2. **Possession commands attention.**
   Notice how Jesus describes earthly treasure: it is constantly under threat from *moth, rust, and thieves*. Because earthly possessions are fragile, owning them demands mental vigilance. You have to insure them, maintain them, check their value, and guard them against loss. (This is why the very next verses in Matthew 6 turn immediately to the focus of the *eye* in vv. 22–23, divided *allegiance* in v. 24, and *anxiety* in vv. 25–34.) Whatever you store up automatically claims your cognitive bandwidth.

3. **Attention and investment cultivate love.**
   We tend to assume that love comes first and investment comes second. Often, the reverse is just as true: **we grow to love what we tend, protect, and sacrifice for.** If you spend years building, guarding, and worrying over an earthly empire, your affection and sense of identity will inevitably fuse with it. Conversely, if you invest your time, generosity, and mercy into the kingdom of heaven—the kinds of hidden acts of righteousness Jesus just discussed in Matthew 6:1–18—your attention naturally shifts toward God’s purposes, and your affection follows.

---

### The Takeaway

Jesus treats the heart less like a fixed personality trait and more like a compass whose needle is pulled by whatever heavy object you set next to it. Possession is never just an economic act; it is an act of spiritual direction. What we hoard claims our attention, what claims our attention shapes our love, and what we love ultimately defines us.
