**Both—but the saying’s force is not merely that treasure reveals the heart. It is also that treasure draws the heart after it.** Jesus is not only offering a diagnosis of our loves; he is commanding a practice that shapes them.

### Treasure reveals the heart

What we treat as treasure makes our priorities visible. What do we protect, sacrifice for, fear losing, and regard as indispensable? Those commitments disclose what matters to us.

“Heart” here means more than feeling. In biblical usage, it includes desire, thought, intention, and will—the center from which a person lives. So the question is not simply, “What do you own?” but, “Where have you located your security, hope, and good?”

Having something is not necessarily the same as treasuring it. Yet what we do with our possessions helps show the difference.

### Treasure also trains the heart

Notice the direction of the final sentence:

> “Where your treasure is, there will your heart be also.”

Jesus does not say only, “Your treasure will be wherever your heart already is.” He speaks of the heart being where the treasure is. The future wording should not bear the whole argument by itself, but the sequence matters: **lay up treasure here rather than there, because your heart will become attached to its location.**

On this reading, we need not wait until our desires are perfectly ordered before acting differently. Our practices of storing, spending, giving, and protecting can help reorder those desires. Generosity, for example, can both express an existing love and cultivate a deeper one.

### Possession, attention, and love

The connection can be described as a reinforcing movement:

- **Possession stakes something of ourselves in a thing.** We put resources, effort, and expectations into it.
- **That investment recruits attention.** We monitor it, maintain it, imagine its future, and worry about its loss.
- **Repeated attention deepens attachment.** The thing becomes increasingly bound up with our sense of safety, identity, or fulfillment.
- **Attachment then directs further investment.** We give still more to what we have come to love.

That is why moth, rust, and thieves matter. Earthly treasure is vulnerable, and a heart bound to it becomes vulnerable with it. What can happen to the treasure begins to govern what happens within us.

The surrounding passage reinforces this connection: Jesus goes on to speak of the eye, serving God rather than mammon, and anxiety about material provision. Treasure is not an isolated financial issue; it concerns how we see, whom we serve, and what occupies our concern.

### What does “treasure in heaven” mean?

In this context, it means directing one’s life toward what has enduring value before God—rather than treating earthly accumulation as ultimate security. The chapter’s concern with sincere giving and devotion to the Father helps give this practical shape. It is not a scheme for purchasing God’s favor.

So the text describes a relationship running in both directions: **we invest in what we love, and we learn to love what we invest in.** Its challenge is therefore deeper than “Check whether your possessions reflect your heart.” It is also: **“Choose where you place your treasure, because that choice helps determine where your heart will live.”**
