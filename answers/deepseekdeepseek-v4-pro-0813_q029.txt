The moral posture required is **humble, gentle, restorative, and self-suspecting**—not censorious, superior, or harsh.

Paul says:

> “If a man be overtaken in a fault, ye which are spiritual, restore such an one in the spirit of meekness; considering thyself, lest thou also be tempted.”

Several things are held together here:

1. **“Restore”**  
   The Greek word Paul uses (*katartizō*) means to mend, repair, or set in order—like setting a broken bone or repairing torn nets. The aim of correction is **healing and restoration**, not humiliation or punishment. The person is seen as someone overtaken, trapped, or burdened, not merely as an offender to be condemned.

2. **“In the spirit of meekness”**  
   Meekness is not weakness; it is **strength under control**, gentleness governed by the Spirit. Correction must be done with patience, tenderness, and humility. A harsh or self-righteous spirit cannot truly restore someone, because it tends to crush rather than heal.

3. **“Considering thyself, lest thou also be tempted”**  
   This is **self-suspicion**. The one correcting must remember their own vulnerability to sin. No one is beyond falling. This awareness keeps correction from becoming prideful or hypocritical. It produces compassion rather than contempt.

So why are restoration, meekness, and self-suspicion kept together?

Because they belong together:

- **Restoration** is the *goal*.  
- **Meekness** is the *manner*.  
- **Self-suspicion** is the *safeguard*.

Without meekness, correction becomes harshness.  
Without self-suspicion, correction becomes pride.  
Without restoration, correction becomes mere scolding or punishment.

Paul’s vision of correction is deeply moral and communal: the spiritual person does not stand above the fallen person, but alongside them in humility, seeking to restore them gently, knowing that they too could fall.
