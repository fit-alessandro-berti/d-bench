In the context of Ephesians 4, bitterness and forgiveness are placed directly against one another because they represent two mutually exclusive orientations of the heart toward offense and relationship. The text treats them as antithetical **postures** that inevitably produce different communities.

Here is a breakdown of why this contrast is so sharp and what kind of thing "bitterness" is treated as in this passage:

### 1. The Moral Zero-Sum of Forgiveness
The structure of the verse makes forgiveness the remedy for bitterness. You cannot "put away" bitterness while holding onto a grudge. In the logic of the text, forgiveness is not merely an emotional release; it is a decision to cancel a debt, mirroring the way God forgave the Ephesians "for Christ's sake." Bitterness, conversely, is the insistence on collecting that debt. They are opposites because one consumes the soul in resentment, while the other releases it in grace.

### 2. Bitterness as a Root (The Contagious Element)
The text treats bitterness as something more than a transient feeling. The Greek word used here (*pikria*) literally refers to **sharpness** or a **bitter taste**, often associated with poison or harsh herbs.
*   **As a Root:** In the broader New Testament context (e.g., Hebrews 12:15 warns against a "root of bitterness"), bitterness is viewed as something that takes hold underground and defiles many. It is not just a spark of anger; it is a cultivated state.
*   **As Contagious:** Paul lists "bitterness" alongside "wrath, anger, clamour, and evil speaking." He also addresses "all malice." This list suggests a cascade. Bitterness is the internal condition that fuels the external explosions (clamour, evil speaking). Because the Ephesians were called to be a unified body ("one new man"), bitterness is dangerous not just to the individual, but to the community. It spreads.

### 3. Habit and Posture vs. Feeling
The imperative "be put away" implies that these are established behaviors or habits that require active rejection. A passing feeling of anger might arise spontaneously, but "bitterness" suggests a settled choice to remain angry.
*   **Chosen Posture:** The text contrasts bitterness with being "tenderhearted." This suggests that bitterness is a hardening of the heart. It is a chosen posture of defensiveness or vengeance that becomes a habit of the will.
*   **Habitual:** By pairing it with "malice" (wickedness or active ill-will), the text moves bitterness out of the realm of involuntary emotion and into the realm of moral responsibility. You are commanded to put it away; therefore, you have the agency to remove it.

### 4. The Standard of Imitation
The reason for this drastic contrast is the second verse: "even as God for Christ's sake hath forgiven you." This is the ethical anchor. The Ephesians are being asked to mirror the divine posture.
*   If God forgave them while they were still enemies (implied by "for Christ's sake"), holding onto bitterness against a brother or sister renders that model useless.
*   Therefore, bitterness is not just a negative emotion to be managed; it is a failure to embody the identity they have received in Christ.

### Summary
The text treats bitterness as a **root attitude** that generates harmful **habits** (wrath, clamour, evil speaking). It is contagious because it fractures the community unity Paul has been building toward in Ephesians. It is placed against forgiveness because forgiveness is the only mechanism that uproots that bitterness, replacing a heart of stone (hardness/malice) with a heart of flesh (tenderheartedness/kindness).
