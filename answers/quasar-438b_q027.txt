The Pharisee is condemned not merely for having virtue, nor simply for “comparison” in a neutral sense, but for **using virtue as self-display**: he converts prayer into a proof of his own righteousness and a public distancing of himself from lesser men.

His fault has several layers, but they are not separate options:

- **Pride**: he is satisfied with himself.
- **Comparison**: he defines his goodness as “not as other men are.”
- **Contempt**: he names the publican as one of the despised.
- **Self-display**: he recites his fasting and tithing almost as credentials of righteousness.

But the deepest fault is the last one: **righteousness trusted in himself**. He treats prayer as confirmation that he is already justified. He thanks God that he is not like extortioners, unjust men, adulterers, “or even as this publican.” That is not a prayer for mercy or grace; it is a self-administered certificate of moral superiority.

The publican—though socially the worse man—is spiritually truer because he does the opposite. He does not lift his prayers toward heaven as a spectacle of piety. He stands apart, strikes his breast, and says: **“God be merciful to me a sinner.”** That is not boastful righteousness; it is humility before Jesus. He admits his fault and asks for mercy from above, rather than proclaiming his virtue to others.

So the tax collector’s posture is treated as spiritually truer because it keeps the soul open to justification from Jesus. The Pharisee has spiritually closed himself: he has preapproved himself, despised his neighbor, and made his prayer an exhibition. The publican’s prayer confesses sin and dependence; the Pharisee’s prayer awards himself honor.
