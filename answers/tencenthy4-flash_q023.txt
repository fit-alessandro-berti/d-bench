Repentance in Isaiah 55:7 is both outward and inward because the prophet is calling for a whole-person turning back to God, not merely a change of behavior or a change of mood.

“Way” refers to one’s path of life: conduct, habits, choices, the visible pattern by which a person lives. “Thoughts” refers to the inner life: plans, intentions, imaginations, reasoning, desires, and the mental “devices” that shape conduct. In Hebrew thought, “thoughts” are not detached ideas; they are the purposes of the heart that issue in action.

So the verse implies:

1. **Outward conduct must be forsaken**  
   Repentance is not only feeling sorry. The wicked person must abandon his “way”—the actual path of sin, the chosen pattern of life.

2. **Inward imagination must be surrendered**  
   It is not enough to stop certain outward acts while cherishing the same desires, justifications, ambitions, or imaginations. The “unrighteous man” must forsake his “thoughts”—the inner world that feeds and directs the outward life.

3. **Repentance is a return to God**  
   The movement is both negative and positive: forsake sin, then “return unto the Lord.” It is not mere self-reform but a renewed relationship with God.

4. **Mercy is held out to the returning sinner**  
   The ground of mercy is not the adequacy of human repentance but God’s own compassion: “he will have mercy,” and “he will abundantly pardon.” The pardon is generous, not grudging.

5. **God’s higher ways make mercy possible**  
   The following verses say, “For my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways.” Human beings often think in terms of desert, revenge, or strict equivalence; God’s mercy exceeds those expectations.

Thus the passage teaches that true repentance includes both the outer path and the inner imagination—and that when a person turns to God in this way, mercy is not merely possible but promised: God “will abundantly pardon.”
