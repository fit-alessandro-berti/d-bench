Isaiah presents repentance as **a turning of the whole person**, not merely a change in outward behavior—or merely a change of opinion. “Way” and “thoughts” identify two dimensions of the life that must be left behind when someone returns to God.

### 1. “Way” concerns a person’s direction in life

To “forsake his way” means abandoning the settled course one has chosen: one’s habits, decisions, priorities, relationships, and actual conduct. Outward conduct matters because it reveals where a person is headed, not merely whether they appear respectable in any particular moment.

But “way” is more than a collection of isolated acts. It describes an entire life-orientation: the direction in which someone habitually walks. Repentance therefore includes turning away from a life organized around rebellion against God, not simply changing a few public behaviors.

### 2. “Thoughts” concerns inward orientation and aspiration

“His thoughts” reaches into the inner life: what a person imagines, desires, hopes for, intends, and uses to justify a particular direction. Isaiah is not suggesting that inward life and outward life are unrelated. Rather, a person’s conduct grows from an inward orientation.

This matters because someone might behave outwardly respectably while privately preserving ambitions, resentments, self-justifications, or plans contrary to God. Repentance cannot consist solely in rearranging outward conduct while leaving the heart’s governing commitments unchanged. Nor is inward repentance merely acquiring better ideas: it involves turning one’s trust, desires, and purposes back toward God.

### 3. The two belong together, but neither earns mercy

The passage does not describe outward reform and inward sincerity as separate qualifications that must be completed before God responds. “Way” and “thoughts” are complementary aspects of **one returning person**. Returning to the Lord means abandoning both the outward course and the inward orientation that sustain it.

At the same time, repentance here is not self-improvement that earns divine approval. The final **“for”** connects the promise of pardon to the invitation in Isaiah 55:1–3: “Come, ye that buy without money, and without cost.” The prospect of mercy is grounded in God’s willingness to pardon, not in the adequacy of the returning person’s transformation.

Thus, the verse holds together **God’s abundant mercy and the urgency of human turning**. God does not require people to become morally faultless before approaching him. But the promised mercy is not indifference to sin: those called to return must actually turn from their own way and thoughts.

In short: **“way” describes life in practice; “thoughts” describe life in imagination and allegiance. Repentance addresses both, and mercy remains possible because it is God who “will have mercy” and “will abundantly pardon.”**
