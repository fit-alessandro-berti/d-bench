**Bitterness is called a “root” because it begins hidden, grows unseen, and later produces visible, contagious fruit.**

The image (echoing Deut. 29:18’s “root that bears gall and wormwood”) treats resentment as something that takes hold below the surface rather than as a single isolated act. A root is:

- **Concealed and gradual.** It develops in the heart—unnoticed at first, nourished by grievance, comparison, or perceived slight—while the person may still appear peaceful or even religious. Moral failure here is not primarily a sudden outburst but an inner process that “fails of the grace of God.”
- **Delayed in its damage.** The text says it “springs up” and then “troubles.” The harm is not immediate; the root can remain dormant or small until circumstances (conflict, disappointment, community pressure) cause it to surface as open bitterness, division, or hardness.
- **Contagious.** One person’s hidden resentment “defiles many.” Like a plant whose roots or toxins affect the soil and nearby growth, bitterness spreads through relationships: it models grievance, invites others to take sides, poisons speech, and undermines the “peace with all” and holiness the passage commands. A single untreated root can therefore corrupt a whole group.

The warning is therefore both personal and communal: watch diligently so that what starts as an inner attitude does not become a spreading source of trouble. The remedy implied by the surrounding verses is active pursuit of peace and holiness rather than allowing the root to remain.
