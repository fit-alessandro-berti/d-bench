All five are present, but they are not all at the same depth. **The elder brother’s deepest problem is a self-righteous, transactional understanding of his relationship with his father, which leaves him unable to share his father’s joy in mercy.** His anger is the symptom; his discomfort with mercy is where that deeper disorder becomes visible.

His own language is revealing:

- **“These many years do I serve thee”** — he speaks less like a son enjoying fellowship than like a servant accumulating wages.
- **“Neither transgressed I at any time thy commandment”** — his obedience has become his defense and his claim against his father.
- **“Thou never gavest me a kid”** — the father now appears to him as a debtor who has failed to pay what faithful service deserves.
- **“This thy son”** — he refuses to call the prodigal “my brother.” The father must correct that distance: **“this thy brother.”**

His wounded sense of fairness is understandable and should not simply be mocked. He may indeed have been hardworking and faithful for years while his brother disgraced the family. The parable does not make obedience itself the problem. The problem is what obedience has become in his own account of it: not a life shared with the father, but a record by which the father owes him something.

The father’s reply therefore does not compare the brothers’ moral records. He reminds the elder son of what his resentment has obscured: **“Son, thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine.”** The younger son’s reception has not disinherited or displaced him. The feast is not a wage paid to the prodigal, still less a reward for his sins. It celebrates restoration: **“dead … alive again … lost … found.”** What is fitting is not to approve the younger son’s past, but to rejoice that he has returned.

Thus the elder brother is not opposed to mercy in the abstract. He is offended by **mercy toward someone whose undeservingness seems to highlight his own deservingness**. Grace to his brother feels like an insult to his record because his record has become the basis of his worth. He cannot rejoice in his brother’s good without feeling that his own standing has been diminished.

This is why he stands outside the feast. He is physically near the father but alienated from the father’s heart—lost, in a different way, at home. Yet the father also comes out to him and pleads with him. The parable does not merely condemn him; it invites him in.

In Luke’s setting, this mirrors the scribes and Pharisees who object that Jesus “receiveth sinners, and eateth with them” (Luke 15:2). The unanswered question at the parable’s end is therefore not whether the repentant sinner may be received—he already has been—but whether the righteous-looking objector will enter the joy.

**In short:** pride in obedient performance lies very near the root, but its deepest expression is relational alienation. The elder brother must learn not to obey less, but to live as a son who knows his father’s heart—and therefore as a brother who can rejoice in mercy.
