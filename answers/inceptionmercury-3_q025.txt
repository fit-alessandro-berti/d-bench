The moral danger in Matthew 6:1 is **not visibility itself, but the craving to be seen** — the heart’s desire to perform goodness for human applause.

Jesus says: “Take heed that ye do not your alms before men, **to be seen of them**.” The Greek phrase *pros to theathēnai autois* means “in order to be seen by them.” That purpose clause is the key. It is the *intention* behind the act, not the mere fact of being observed, that makes public goodness corrupt.

Why does this matter?

1. **Visibility isn’t automatically sinful.** Jesus does not forbid all public good works. Earlier in the same sermon, He says: “Let your light shine before others, so that they may see your good works and give glory to your Father who is in heaven” (Matthew 5:16). There, visibility is a means to God’s glory, not to one’s own glory. The difference is the audience.

2. **The problem is divided attention.** When we do good to be seen by people, we are making *them* the judge, the rewarder, and the point. Jesus says if that is your goal, you already have your reward — and you forfeit the reward of the Father. The heart is no longer aiming at God’s approval; it is aiming at human recognition.

3. **Public goodness is morally dangerous because it tempts the heart toward performance.** It creates a stage for hypocrisy, where acts are crafted not for love but for image. But the *sin* is not the stage; it is the actor’s desire to be applauded. The same act done in public, with the intention of honoring God and serving the neighbor, is not condemned.

So the answer: **visibility is a temptation, but the craving to be seen is the corruption.** Jesus is not warning against doing good in public; He is warning against making your goodness a show. The remedy is not to hide all good works, but to check the heart: *Why am I doing this? For whose eyes?*
