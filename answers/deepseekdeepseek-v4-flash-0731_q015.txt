The sharpness of the image comes from its **absurd, vivid exaggeration**—and the painful accuracy of that exaggeration.

A “mote” is a tiny splinter or speck; a “beam” is a massive wooden plank or roof timber. Jesus pictures someone trying to perform delicate eye surgery on another person while a **log sticks out of his own eye**. The image is comical, almost cartoonish—but that is exactly the point. It exposes how ridiculous and self-deceived we are when we fixate on another person’s small fault while ignoring a much greater fault in ourselves.

The passage criticizes hypocrisy without forbidding moral correction altogether because it does **not** say, “Never remove the mote from your brother’s eye.” It says:

> **“First cast out the beam out of thine own eye; and then shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote out of thy brother’s eye.”**

So the brother’s mote is still real, and it still needs to be removed. Correction is not banned; it is reordered. The problem is not that we notice others’ faults—it is that we use their faults to avoid facing our own. The hypocrite is not someone who merely falls short; the hypocrite is someone who plays the role of a righteous judge while refusing to repent.

That is why Jesus calls him **“hypocrite”**—the Greek word originally referred to an actor wearing a mask. The hypocrite wears the mask of moral concern while hiding the reality of his own condition.

The sharpness of the passage lies in its inversion:

- We think our own sin is small and the other person’s is huge.
- Jesus says the truth is usually the reverse: the thing we are so quick to criticize in others is often tiny compared to the thing we ignore in ourselves.
- Self-righteousness is not a minor flaw; it is a beam that blinds us to the very help we pretend to offer.

So Matthew 7:3–5 does not forbid moral correction. It forbids **hypocritical** moral correction. It calls us to begin with self-examination: remove the beam, see clearly, and then—only then—we can truly help a brother or sister with their speck. The goal is not to avoid judging altogether, but to become the kind of person whose judgment is clear, humble, and loving enough to be useful.
