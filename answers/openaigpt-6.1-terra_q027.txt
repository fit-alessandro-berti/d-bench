The passage gives its own diagnosis: Jesus addresses those who **“trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and despised others.”** The Pharisee’s pride and contempt are therefore central; comparison and self-display are how they come out in his prayer.

His fasting and tithing are not condemned in themselves. Nor does the parable establish that his claims are false. The problem is what he does with them: he presents his virtues as evidence of his superior standing and treats the tax collector as beneath him. His prayer ostensibly thanks God, but its attention rests on **“I”** and on the man he disdains.

There is a theological error implicit in that posture: he acts as though his record and favorable comparison with others establish his righteousness before God. But Jesus targets his **self-trust and contempt**, not an explicitly stated creed.

The tax collector’s posture is spiritually truer because he does not pretend to have a claim on God based on his own goodness. He acknowledges his sin and asks for mercy. His lowered eyes and beating of his breast express that repentance; they are not a formula for earning justification.

So the contrast is not “bad religious practice versus good religious practice,” or even “prideful person versus impressively humble person.” It is **self-justification joined to contempt, versus an honest appeal to God’s mercy**.
