What condemns the Pharisee, and why the publican's posture is truer

1. Start with what does NOT condemn the Pharisee

It is important to notice what the parable does not fault him for. Nothing he says about himself is presented as false. He very likely is not an extortioner or an adulterer; he really does fast twice a week and tithe on everything — practices that went beyond what the Law required. Nor is his doctrine wrong in any technical sense: he addresses the true God, in the right temple, and even frames his prayer as thanksgiving ("God, I thank thee"), which is formally correct piety. Jesus does not dispute a single fact in the Pharisee's résumé. That is precisely what makes the parable cut so deep: a man can be factually accurate, doctrinally orthodox, morally disciplined — and still go home unjustified.

2. The actual charge: Luke names it in the frame

Luke gives the interpretive key before the parable begins (v. 9): it is told "unto certain which trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and despised others." Two things, and they come as a pair:

(a) Self-trust as the ground of righteousness. The Pharisee treats his standing before God as something he possesses rather than something he receives. The Greek of v. 11 can be read "prayed thus with himself" or "prayed to himself" — and the ambiguity is telling. His prayer never actually asks God for anything. It has the grammar of thanksgiving but the content of self-report: "I... I... I... I" (four times in two verses). God appears in the prayer only as the audience for the Pharisee's inventory of himself. This is what "using virtue as self-display" means here — not display before other people (there is no indication others hear him), but display before God, as though the point of prayer were to present credentials rather than to encounter the Giver.

(b) Contempt as the necessary by-product. His righteousness is constructed comparatively: "I thank thee that I am not as other men are... or even as this publican." He cannot state his own standing without measuring it against someone else's degradation, and the flesh-and-blood man praying across the courtyard becomes raw material for his self-assessment. This is why comparison and contempt cannot be separated in the parable: righteousness understood as relative standing must despise others in order to exist. If my justification consists in being better than the publican, then I need the publican to be despicable.

So the elements listed in the question — pride, comparison, contempt, self-display — are not competing answers; they are one disease described from four sides. The root is self-trust (pride in the strict theological sense: the self installed as the source and measure of its own righteousness). Comparison is its method, contempt is its fruit, and the display of virtue before God is its liturgical form. "False doctrine" is the one option that does not fit, unless one means the deepest doctrinal error of all: a false picture of how a human being stands right with God — righteousness as achievement and possession rather than as gift and mercy.

3. Why the publican's posture is spiritually truer

The tax collector is not praised for being a better man. He is, by any social and probably moral reckoning, the worse one — tax farmers were collaborators who enriched themselves by extortion, and the parable does not sanitize him. What is commended is the truthfulness of his stance:

- He stands "afar off," will not raise his eyes, and beats his breast. Every element of his posture enacts the same admission: he brings nothing. He makes no comparison — he does not say "at least I'm not a hypocrite like that Pharisee." The Greek is emphatic: "God, be merciful to me, THE sinner" — as if he were the only one, with no one else in view to measure against. He occupies the position of debtor absolutely, not relatively.

- His prayer is the only actual petition in the parable. The Pharisee's prayer contains no request because it presumes no need; the publican's prayer is nothing but request. The verb he uses (hilasthēti) carries the sense of "be propitiated / let atonement cover me" — he appeals not to a lenient overlooking of sin but to God's own provision of mercy. His hope is located entirely outside himself.

- This is spiritually truer because it corresponds to reality. Before God, the actual situation of every human being — Pharisee and publican alike — is that of a debtor without means of payment. The publican's posture is honest about that situation; the Pharisee's is a performance that conceals it. The publican's despair of himself is not morbidity but accuracy, and it is exactly this accuracy that leaves room for God to act. Justification, in the parable's own word (dedikaiōmenos, "having been justified," a passive — something done to him, not by him), can only be received by empty hands. The Pharisee's hands are full of his own achievements, so there is nothing God can put into them.

4. The closing axiom

Jesus seals the parable with the reversal formula: "every one that exalteth himself shall be abased; and he that humbleth himself shall be exalted" (v. 14). This is not a rule of etiquette rewarding modesty. It states a structural truth about grace: God's mercy cannot be received from the posture of self-sufficiency, because that posture is itself the refusal of mercy. The Pharisee is not condemned in spite of his virtues but in the very act of leaning on them; the publican is justified not because sin is preferable to virtue, but because his emptiness is truthful and truth is where God meets a person.

The final irony is the parable's trap for the reader: the moment we finish it and think "thank God I am not like that Pharisee," we have become him. The parable can only be read rightly from the publican's position.
