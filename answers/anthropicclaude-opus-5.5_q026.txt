## The Image: Heart as Storehouse, Mouth as Overflow

The passage rests on two metaphors.

**1. Overflow ("abundance").** The Greek *perisseuma* means surplus or what spills over. Speech comes from what the heart is *full of*. When a vessel is full, what spills out tells you what is inside.

**2. Treasury ("treasure").** *Thēsauros* is a storehouse, a place where valuables are accumulated and kept. The heart is not a blank neutral organ. It holds a stockpile built up over time, and speech is what gets "brought forth" (*ekballei*, literally "throws out") from that store.

Together they suggest that the heart is a stock and words are withdrawals from it. You cannot bring out what was never stored.

## The Context Sharpens the Point

These verses are not a general proverb dropped in from nowhere. Jesus has just healed a man, and the Pharisees have attributed the healing to Beelzebul (12:22–24). Jesus warns about blasphemy against the Spirit (12:31–32). He then gives the tree-and-fruit image (12:33): "the tree is known by his fruit."

So the Pharisees' *words* about Jesus have exposed something. Their accusation was not a slip. It revealed a heart so set against God that it could look at a liberating act of mercy and call it demonic. The passage then continues:

> "But I say unto you, That every idle word that men shall speak, they shall give account thereof in the day of judgment. For by thy words thou shalt be justified, and by thy words thou shalt be condemned." (12:36–37)

That follow-up is essential to your question, because it means words are not *merely* symptoms. They are grounds for judgment.

## Symptoms, Choices, Revelations?

### Words as symptoms
The tree-and-fruit logic points this way. Fruit is organically produced by the kind of tree it grows on. "How can ye, being evil, speak good things?" implies a certain inevitability: corrupt sources yield corrupt output. Speech is diagnostic, like a fever pointing to infection.

### Words as revelations
This is perhaps the dominant sense. "The tree is *known* by his fruit." The point is epistemic: words make visible what was hidden. The heart's treasury is private, but speech opens the vault. The phrase "idle words" (*argos*, careless, unworked) is striking. Jesus does not say rehearsed or carefully managed words will be the test, but the offhand ones. Careless speech is the most revealing precisely because it is unguarded. Polished speech can be performance. Idle speech is overflow.

### Words as choices
Here the passage is subtler. If words were purely automatic symptoms, judging someone "by thy words" would seem odd. Yet Jesus clearly holds people accountable. Two things reconcile this:

- **The treasury was stocked by choices.** Treasure is accumulated. What fills a heart is built up through repeated attention, desire, and consent. The "evil man" is responsible not only for the words but for what he has been storing.
- **Speaking is still an act.** Even overflow involves the will. The Pharisees chose to voice their accusation publicly. The passage never pictures a person as a helpless pipe.

### So, all three?
Yes, but in an ordered way:

- **Choices** (past and present) build the treasury.
- The treasury then produces words the way a tree produces fruit (**symptoms**).
- Those words make the hidden treasury visible to others and before God (**revelations**).
- Because the treasury is one's own, the words become legitimate evidence at judgment.

## A Tension Worth Noticing

Elsewhere, Matthew knows that mouth and heart *can* diverge: "This people draweth nigh unto me with their mouth... but their heart is far from me" (15:8), and the hypocrisy discourse of chapter 23. So the passage does not claim every sentence perfectly mirrors the heart. Rather:

- Over time, and especially under pressure or when unguarded, the heart's true contents come out.
- Hypocrisy is possible in managed speech, but hard to sustain in "abundance," in what spills out when the vessel is full.

## Implications

1. **Speech-control alone is insufficient.** If words flow from the treasury, the real work is on what is stored. This matches Jesus' wider teaching that defilement comes "out of the heart" (15:18–19).
2. **The heart is formable.** A treasury can be restocked. The passage implies that the remedy is a renewed heart, since a bad tree cannot simply decide to bear good fruit (12:33: "make the tree good").
3. **Words are weighty.** Because they reveal the heart, they carry moral significance before God, which is why James can say that one who controls the tongue is "a perfect man" (James 3:2). That isn't because the tongue is everything, but because it indexes everything.

**In short:** the passage treats the heart as a storehouse built by choices, and speech as the overflow that exposes what has been stored. Words are symptoms in how they arise, revelations in what they show, and choices in that we remain answerable for both the words and the treasure behind them.
