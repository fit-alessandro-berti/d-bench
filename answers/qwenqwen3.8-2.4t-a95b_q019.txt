

James 3:9–10 exposes the contradiction of a divided heart: the same tongue is used to honor God and to dishonor those who bear God’s image.

The passage says that with the tongue “we bless God, even the Father,” and with the same tongue “we curse men, which are made after the similitude of God.” The contradiction is not merely that a person says good things at one moment and evil things at another. The deeper contradiction is theological and moral: we claim to reverence God, yet we attack what reflects Him.

To bless God is to honor Him as Creator, Father, and the source of all goodness. But human beings are made “after the similitude of God”—they bear His image and likeness. So when someone blesses God while cursing people, that person is, in effect, praising the Artist while defacing His portrait. The tongue gives worship upward and contempt outward. It confesses God’s worth while denying the dignity He has placed in human beings.

This exposes a false separation between religion and love. A person may sound devout in worship, prayer, or spiritual speech, yet be bitter, slanderous, mocking, or cruel toward others. James says this kind of inconsistency “ought not so to be.” True reverence for God must produce reverence for those made in His image. If we love God, we cannot treat His image-bearers with malice without betraying the very God we claim to bless.

Speech reveals moral disorder so quickly because words are the immediate overflow of the heart. Speech is closer to the inner life than many outward actions. A person can control behavior for a time, but words often escape before restraint can govern them. They reveal what is truly within: pride, anger, envy, contempt, bitterness, or love. As Jesus says, “Out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaketh” (Matthew 12:34). The tongue is small, but it discloses the state of the whole person.

James himself has already compared the tongue to a bit in a horse’s mouth, a ship’s rudder, and a fire. These images show that the tongue is small but powerful, difficult to tame, and capable of exposing the direction of the heart. If the heart is disordered, speech will quickly betray it. Blessing and cursing from the same mouth reveal that the inner life is not unified under God’s rule. The tongue makes visible the conflict between what we profess to worship and what we actually love or hate.

So the passage exposes hypocrisy: outward piety toward God combined with inward contempt for people. It also reveals why speech is such a reliable moral indicator. Words do not merely communicate information; they reveal allegiance. They show whether the heart is governed by love for God and neighbor, or by selfishness, anger, and pride.

In short: James exposes the contradiction of praising God while cursing God’s image. Speech reveals this disorder quickly because the tongue is the heart’s most immediate messenger. It shows, often before we intend it to, what we truly honor and what we truly despise.
