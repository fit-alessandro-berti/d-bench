The short answer: **the craving to be seen** is the moral danger; visibility itself is not the sin. 

Here’s why the distinction matters, straight from the verse and its immediate context:

### 1. The verse critiques *motive*, not *method*
Jesus assumes almsgiving, prayer, and fasting are normal expressions of piety (he immediately follows with “when you give,” “when you pray,” “when you fast”). The danger isn’t that someone is doing good where others can observe it; it’s that the **purpose** of the act is to receive human applause rather than divine approval.

> “Take heed that ye do not your alms before men, **to be seen of them**.”  
> The phrase *to be seen of them* expresses intent, not geography. The sin is in the *why*, not the *where*.

### 2. The craving functions as a competing master
Jesus says the result of performing goodness “to be seen” is:  
> “otherwise ye have no reward of your Father which is in heaven.”

In other words, if your ultimate aim is human recognition, you’ve already received your full reward—here and now. There’s nothing left for God to give you because you’ve already chosen what you wanted. The craving to be seen effectively **crowds out** the possibility of the “reward from the Father,” which in biblical teaching is not a trophy but the deep communion, integrity, and purpose that come from living for an audience of One.

### 3. Visibility is the stage; the craving is the heart issue
The verse doesn’t say “don’t give where people can see you.” It says *“don’t give *for the sake of* being seen.”* A person can give publicly with a clean heart (e.g., serving the poor in a transparent way that enables more help, or living “so that they may see your good works and glorify your Father in heaven” — Matthew 5:16). The difference is whether the person is **using people to get glory for themselves**, or **using the act to point people to God.**

### 4. The broader biblical pattern
Throughout Matthew 6, Jesus repeats the same structure three times (alms, prayer, fasting): *“when you [do X], don’t do it hypocritically so others will notice; go into your room / your secret place, and your Father who sees in secret will reward you.”* The consistent theme isn’t that public spirituality is invalid—it’s that **human approval and divine approval are mutually exclusive currencies.** You can’t cash both at the same time.

### Bottom line
The moral danger isn’t that goodness is visible. It’s that **goodness performed to feed a craving for human admiration short-circuits the very thing God intends to give: a reward that comes from Him, not from an audience.** The craving to be seen is the problem because it turns a selfless act into a self-promoting performance, and in doing so, it loses the only reward that ultimately satisfies.
