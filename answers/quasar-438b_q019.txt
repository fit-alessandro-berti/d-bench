Based on the theological and philosophical context of the passage, here is an explanation of the inner contradiction it exposes and the reason why speech reveals moral disorder so quickly.

### 1. The Inner Contradiction Exposed
The passage exposes a profound hypocrisy in how humanity evaluates existence: **the contradiction of blessing the archetype while cursing its image.** 

James points out that we "bless" God (the Father, the ultimate archetype) but "curse" men, even acknowledging that men are created in the "similitude" (the likeness) of God. The contradiction lies in holding two opposing moral judgments about the exact same ontological reality. If man is made in the image of God, then man carries within him the divine nature. To bless God is to bless the source; to curse man is to curse the offspring of that exact same source. 

By doing both simultaneously, the speaker fractures their moral compass. They are essentially saying: *"I love the original, but I despise the copy, even though the copy is made of the same essence."* It reveals a disjointed theology where humanity claims reverence for the divine, yet harbors deep animosity toward its earthly, mortal reflections. To bless the Father while cursing his offspring is to fail to understand the nature of both. 

### 2. Why Speech Reveals Moral Disorder So Quickly
James asserts that "Out of the same mouth proceedeth blessing and cursing" to highlight how **language forces a linear, vulnerable exposure of our internal chaos.** 

Speech reveals moral disorder quickly for several reasons:

*   **The Flattening of Thought into Sequence:** The mind can hold contradictory thoughts simultaneously in a kind of hazy, unexamined soup. But speech is linear. Words must be strung together one after another. The moment you speak a blessing and then speak a curse from the same mouth, the temporal proximity strips away the illusion of nuance. The contradiction is laid bare side-by-side.
*   **The Mouth as a Single Source of Intent:** The mouth is the instrument of the soul's outward expression. When radically opposed moral judgments (blessing vs. cursing) issue from the exact same physical organ, it proves that the soul itself is unhinged. The mouth becomes a mirror of internal dissonance; it cannot hide the fact that the speaker’s worldview is fractured.
*   **The Absolutism of Moral Vocabulary:** Words like "bless" and "curse" are extreme moral binaries. They do not allow for middle ground. When speech employs such words in rapid succession, it bypasses the subtle reasoning that might excuse the contradiction. The vocabulary itself demands obedient consistency, and when it is not given, the moral disorder is instantly spotlighted.

### The Conclusion: "These things ought not so to be"
When James concludes, "My brethren, these things ought not so to be," he is making an ethical demand for unification. He demands that we reconcile the creator with the creation. If man is of God, we cannot curse man without ultimately cursing God. We must align our speech so that our mouth exudes one consistent moral trajectory: to bless the Father, we must learn to bless the sons, for they are one.
