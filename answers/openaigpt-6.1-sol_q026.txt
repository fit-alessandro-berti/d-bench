The passage presents speech as the **outflow of an inward moral storehouse**. “Abundance” suggests what fills the heart; “treasure” suggests what is stored there and available to be brought out. In biblical usage, the *heart* is not merely the seat of feelings—it includes thought, desire, intention, and will.

The point is that words do not arise in isolation. They draw on what a person inwardly cherishes, believes, and cultivates.

### Words are symptoms
“For out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaketh.”

Speech expresses an underlying moral condition, rather as fruit expresses the character of a tree—the image Jesus uses immediately before this passage. Persistent malice, deceit, or contempt in speech points toward something deeper than a problem of vocabulary.

### Words are choices
“A good man … bringeth forth good things.”

The storehouse image does not make the speaker a passive instrument. Speaking is an action for which a person is responsible. The following verses reinforce this: people will give account for their words, and their words will figure in judgment (Matthew 12:36–37).

So an inward disposition helps explain speech without excusing it. Words express character, but they are also morally significant acts.

### Words are revelations
The heart is hidden; speech makes something of it audible. In context, Jesus is responding to opponents who attributed his work to demonic power. Their accusation reveals not simply mistaken wording but the hostility behind their interpretation of what he does.

This does **not** mean that any single sentence gives other people infallible access to someone’s entire heart. The passage connects speech and character; it does not authorize effortless mind-reading.

**So, all three—but in a particular order:** the heart supplies the moral stock, the speaker brings it forth in words, and those words disclose the source from which they came. Speech is therefore a **symptom of character, an accountable choice, and a revelation of inward commitments**.

The practical implication is that reforming speech requires more than policing the tongue. It also requires attending to what is being stored in the heart.
