

In Galatians 6:1, the moral posture required for correction is one of **humble, Spirit-dependent restoration** rather than condemnation, superiority, or self-righteousness. The passage explicitly grounds this posture in the phrase "ye which are spiritual" (those walking in the Spirit) and commands them to act "in the spirit of meekness," while simultaneously issuing a warning to "consider thyself."

Here is a breakdown of the moral posture required and why restoration, meekness, and self-suspicion are kept together:

### 1. The Required Moral Posture: Humble Rehabilitation
The instruction calls for a posture that is **pastoral rather than punitive**. To be "overtaken in a fault" implies a sudden stumble in weakness, not a deliberate act of rebellion. Therefore, the "spiritual" person is called to be a **restorer**—someone who helps the stumbling brother back onto his feet—rather than a judge who condemns him.
*   **Spiritual Identity:** The command is addressed to those who live "in the Spirit," implying that this posture is not natural human wisdom but a fruit of the Spirit (Galatians 5:22-23).
*   **Meekness:** This is not weakness, but the strength of self-control under provocation. In this context, meekness means approaching the fallen brother with gentleness, avoiding harshness, arrogance, or a triumphant attitude that would shame him.

### 2. Why Restoration, Meekness, and Self-Suspicion Are Kept Together
These three elements are woven together to prevent two extremes (legalism and laxism) and to ensure the correction is loving and effective.

**A. Restoration (The Goal) and Meekness (The Method)**
Restoration is the end goal, but meekness is the necessary vehicle for achieving it. A harsh or stern approach may produce temporary compliance but destroys the relationship and the conscience of the weaker brother. Meekness ensures that the restoration is received as grace rather than judgment. If the method is harsh, the goal of true healing is compromised.

**B. Self-Suspicion (The Safeguard) and Restoration**
The phrase "considering thyself, lest thou also be tempted" acts as a critical check on the restorer's own heart.
*   **Preventing Hypocrisy:** If the restorer forgets their own vulnerability to sin, they may slip into a spirit of legalism, looking down on the one they help. This hypocrisy undermines the credibility of the restoration.
*   **Preventing Condemnation:** "Considering thyself" implies a sifting of self before judging others. It prevents the restorer from treating their own sin as a lesser issue while treating the brother's sin as a great wickedness.
*   **Communion of Saints:** If the restorer is spiritually arrogant, they place themselves above the brother. But if they "consider themselves," they recognize that they stand in the same grace and are equally susceptible to falling. This levels the playing field, allowing a true "fellowship of sufferers" rather than a "hierarchy of judges."

### Summary
The moral posture required is **sober self-awareness combined with gentle compassion**. The reason these are kept together is pragmatic and theological: **Meekness makes the restoration possible, and Self-Suspicion keeps the restorer from destroying it.** If the restorer is not meek, they will be harsh; if they are not self-suspicion, they will be hypocritical. Together, they ensure the correction serves the brother's spiritual health without compromising the restorer's spiritual integrity.

---

*Contextual Note:* Some Jewish Christian interpreters debated the nature of the "spiritual" person (versus the "fleshly" Galatians). This passage consistently emphasizes the **pastoral duty** of the community to intervene in the life of a fallen member, a theme Paul continues in the following verses (vv. 2 & 5) regarding the sharing of burdens and the law of sowing and reaping.
