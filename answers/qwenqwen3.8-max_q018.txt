They are placed directly against each other because Paul is presenting two opposed ways of living inside the Christian community: one that hardens and divides, and one that reflects the mercy of God.

In Ephesians 4:31–32, bitterness is not treated merely as a passing emotion. It is treated as something that must be “put away,” which suggests a settled condition of the heart that one is responsible to reject. The list that follows helps clarify this:

- **bitterness** — the inward root, the souring of the heart;
- **wrath** and **anger** — the heated outworking of that root;
- **clamour** and **evil speaking** — the social expression, the way bitterness becomes loud, accusatory, slanderous, divisive;
- **malice** — the broader disposition of ill will.

So bitterness is not simply “I feel hurt.” It is more like hurt that has been nursed, allowed to curdle, and turned into resentment. It can begin as pain, but it becomes bitterness when the heart refuses to release the offense and begins to rehearse it, treasure it, and use it as a lens for interpreting others.

That means the text treats bitterness as **more than a feeling**. It is closer to a **habitual posture**. It is the soul taking a stance: “I will hold this against you.” It is the mind repeatedly returning to the injury and feeding the grievance. In that sense, it is chosen—not always in a single conscious moment, but by the many small acts of refusing mercy, refusing honesty, refusing reconciliation, and refusing to entrust the wrong to God.

And yes, the text also strongly implies that bitterness is **contagious**. Notice that Paul moves from bitterness to wrath, anger, clamour, evil speaking, and “all malice.” That is a social spread. Bitterness rarely stays private. It leaks out. It becomes sharp speech, gossip, harsh tone, suspicion, faction, and a community atmosphere poisoned by unresolved resentment. Hebrews 12:15 uses similar imagery when it warns against “any root of bitterness springing up to cause trouble, and by it many become defiled.” That is not the same passage, but it reflects the same moral perception: bitterness spreads.

Forgiveness is placed so directly against bitterness because forgiveness is the only true alternative to that poison. Paul does not merely say, “Be calm.” He says:

> “be ye kind one to another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, even as God for Christ’s sake hath forgiven you.”

Forgiveness here is not a mood either. It is a way of life shaped by the gospel. It is active kindness, compassion, and the refusal to hold an offense over someone as leverage for retaliation. The ground of this is not natural temperament but divine grace: we forgive because God has forgiven us in Christ.

The contrast is therefore very precise:

- **Bitterness says:** “You owe me, and I will keep collecting.”
- **Forgiveness says:** “God has forgiven me in Christ, so I will not live by vengeance or resentment.”

Bitterness freezes a person inside the injury. Forgiveness begins to release the person from the injury—not because the offense was trivial, but because Christ has dealt with sin at a deeper level.

Paul’s context is also important. Ephesians 4 is about the unity of the body. Earlier he says to “endeavour to keep the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace” (4:3), and later warns, “Grieve not the holy Spirit of God” (4:30). Bitterness grieves the Spirit because it breaks the fellowship that Christ has created. It turns members of one body into adversaries. That is why it must be “put away” decisively, and why forgiveness must be practiced continuously.

So the best answer is: **bitterness in this passage is not merely a feeling; it is a nurtured disposition that becomes a habit, a chosen posture, and a contagious relational poison.** Forgiveness is set against it because forgiveness is the Christlike community-practice that stops the poison from spreading and replaces it with grace.
