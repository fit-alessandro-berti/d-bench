## Why the image bites

**The disproportion is comic, and the comedy is the argument.** Jesus doesn't reason toward a conclusion; he draws a cartoon. A man with a roof-beam jutting from his eye socket leans in to perform delicate surgery on a friend's eyelash. The Greek sharpens it: *karphos* is a dry stalk, a bit of chaff or straw — the smallest irritant imaginable; *dokos* is a load-bearing timber. No argument is offered against such a man because none is needed. You laugh, and the laugh is the rebuke.

**The two objects are made of the same stuff.** Both are wood. This matters enormously. The contrast is not between a real fault and an imaginary one, nor between the brother's sin and the critic's innocence. It is a difference of *scale* within a single category. So the passage is not saying "who are you to call anything wrong?" It is saying: you have correctly identified splinter-wood, and you are carrying a rafter of it.

**The obstruction is located in the organ of sight.** This is the stroke of genius. The fault is not in the hand or the foot but in the eye — the very faculty by which faults are detected. Hypocrisy is therefore not merely inconsistent, it is *self-concealing*. A beam in the eye guarantees that the man cannot see the beam in the eye. The condition includes its own alibi. That is why the passage must come as a shocking image from outside rather than as advice: nothing internal to the hypocrite's perception can reach him.

**The verbs are unequal.** "Why *beholdest* thou (*blepeis*) the mote... but *considerest not* (*ou katanoeis*) the beam?" The first is ordinary seeing; the second is attentive, deliberate noticing. The absurdity is not bad eyesight — the man's vision is superb, microscopically so, in one direction. He can spot chaff at conversational distance. What he cannot do is turn that acuity around. Hypocrisy here is a *failure of attention*, not of intelligence, and the asymmetry is the disease.

**"Hypocrite" means actor.** *Hypokritēs* is the man in the stage mask, playing a part. The word exposes the motive that the surgical offer conceals: the concern for the brother's eye is a role, a performance of moral seriousness. Which explains the odd little speech in v. 4 — "Let me pull out the mote out of thine eye." Nobody says that unless they want to be seen saying it.

## Why this is not a ban on correction

The decisive word is **"first."** *First* cast out the beam — *then* you shall see clearly to cast out the mote. This is a sequence, not a prohibition. If Jesus had wanted to forbid moral correction, v. 5 would have ended at "cast out the beam out of thine own eye." Instead the sentence runs all the way through to the brother's speck being removed. The speck is still a speck; it is still worth taking out; and the person who has dealt with his own timber is precisely the one commissioned to do it.

Notice also that the goal is stated as *seeing clearly* (*diablepseis*). Self-examination is not presented as a humility ritual that earns you the right to speak. It is presented as the thing that repairs your vision. A man with a beam in his eye will misjudge the speck's size, position, and even its existence; he will gouge where he means to extract. Removing your own obstruction is a matter of competence, not just credentials.

And the term of address throughout is **"thy brother."** The scene is set inside a family or community, among people who have claims on each other. Strangers do not offer to touch each other's eyes. The passage assumes a relationship in which such intimate help is appropriate — it is asking how to do it well, not whether to do it.

## Reading it with v. 1–2

Matthew 7:1 ("Judge not, that ye be not judged") is often taken as a blanket prohibition, which then makes v. 5 unintelligible. Read together, the unit is about a *manner* of judging: the measure you use is the measure applied to you (v. 2), and the judge whose own case is worse has disqualified himself until he takes it up. There is a further reading worth holding: the beam may *be* the censoriousness itself. The habit of scanning others for faults, of enjoying their faults, is the great deforming timber — larger than anything it detects. On this reading the man's real problem is not some unrelated private sin but the very posture in which we find him.

Either way, the passage performs a neat operation on the reader. It hands you a wonderful insult — "you hypocrite" — and gives you no one to use it on. The only eye you can inspect from the inside is your own.
