They are set against each other because they are rival ways of holding the same thing: a debt.

That is clearest in the word Paul chooses for forgiving. *Charizomenoi* comes from *charis* — grace, gift. It is not the vocabulary of adjudication or of therapeutic release; it is the vocabulary of handing something over freely. Which means forgiveness here is not primarily a change of feeling but a change in the status of a claim. Bitterness keeps the claim open. It says: *this is owed, and I will keep it owed.* Forgiveness cancels it. Both are directed at the identical object — the wrong actually done, not forgotten, not minimized — and they do opposite things with it. You cannot do both to the same debt at the same time. That is why the antithesis is so flat and immediate, with no intermediate term offered.

Notice also what Paul does *not* set against bitterness. Not calm, not detachment, not putting it behind you. He sets kindness, tenderness, and gift. The empty space has to be occupied. Bitterness is not evacuated by an act of will; it is displaced by a different disposition of the same inward region. *Eusplanchnoi*, "tenderhearted," literally means good-boweled — the *splanchna*, the gut, the seat of feeling. Bitterness is also a gut word, a taste word, *pikros* meaning sharp or piercing on the tongue. And *chrēstos*, "kind," was used of wine that had gone mellow, that had lost its harshness. The whole opposition is conducted in the register of palate and viscera: sharp against mellow, clenched against open. This is not a rule about behavior. It is about what has happened to your insides.

**Bitterness is not anger, and Paul has already said so.**

Five verses earlier: "Be ye angry, and sin not: let not the sun go down upon your wrath: neither give place to the devil." Anger is permitted, even commanded. What is forbidden is *giving place* — *topos*, room, lodging, a lease. Bitterness is anger that has been given tenure. It is what remains when the sun has gone down on wrath and wrath has been allowed to stay the night, and then the week, and then move in.

So there is a chosen element, but it is chosen obliquely. Nobody experiences bitterness as a decision. It presents itself as clear sight — *I am simply being honest about what they did.* That is its peculiar armor. Anger knows itself to be a passion and can therefore be repented of; bitterness thinks it is realism, and so it cannot be argued with, because it believes it *is* the argument. This is why Paul's verb for its removal is passive.

**The list is an anatomy of transmission.**

Bitterness, wrath, anger, clamour, evil speaking. Read it as a sequence: a settled inward taste, then heat, then hostility, then *noise*, then *speech about someone*. It begins on the tongue as flavor and ends on the tongue as words. The middle terms are private; the last two require an audience. *Kraugē* is shouting; *blasphēmia* here is slander, the damaging of a name. Bitterness does not stay in the person who has it. It finds a voice, and a voice recruits listeners, and listeners are how a grievance becomes a faction.

This is not a side observation but the letter's whole concern. Ephesians 4 is about a body — "we are members one of another," "the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace." A poison in a body is never local. And the imperative is plural: let it be taken away *aph' hymōn*, from you all, from among you. The removal is corporate as well as interior. Elsewhere the same word carries exactly this sense of spread: Hebrews speaks of a "root of bitterness" that springs up and defiles *many*, and Acts puts Simon Magus not merely as having bitterness but as being *in* the gall of bitterness — a place one can inhabit, which loops back precisely to the *topos* one is told not to grant.

So: feeling, habit, posture, contagion — the text declines to choose, because it is describing a progression. It arrives as feeling's residue, sets into habit by rehearsal, is sustained by a posture toward a debt, and escapes into the community through speech. But the decisive category is the posture, because that is where it meets forgiveness. The other three describe its career; the posture describes its guilt.

**The grammar holds one more asymmetry.**

*Arthētō* — "let it be put away" — is aorist passive: a decisive removal, and something done to you rather than achieved by you. *Ginesthe* — "be ye kind" — is present imperative: continual becoming. You do not taper off bitterness; you have it lifted. And kindness you never finish arriving at. The passive is not quietism. It is an honest description of how the thing actually goes: you cannot reason your way out of a stance that mistakes itself for reason. It gets lifted off you while you are busy doing the other thing — being useful to the person, opening the gut to them, releasing the account.

And then the ground of it, which is not merely motivational but logical: *even as God in Christ hath forgiven you.* Aorist again — a finished act, a settled account. The bitter person is one whose own ledger has been closed by an act he did not pay for, and who is nonetheless keeping a small ledger open against someone else. That is not simply unkind. It is incoherent — a practical amnesia about his own standing, exactly the servant in Matthew 18 who walks out from a cancelled fortune and takes a man by the throat over a hundred pence.

Which is perhaps the final reason the two are set so directly against each other. Forgiveness is not the harder of two options. It is the only one consistent with what has already happened to you.
