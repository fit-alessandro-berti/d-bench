This passage ties speech to what is stored up in the heart by making words the overflow of what is already inside—so the connection is at once causal and revealing.

**Speech as expression of stored moral reality.** “Out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaketh” (v. 34) presents the heart not as a neutral switchboard but as a storehouse. “Abundance” (the Greek *perisseia*) suggests fullness and surplus: speech is not manufactured from nothing but drawn from what has been amassed. A “good treasure” (v. 35) produces good speech; an “evil treasure” produces evil speech. The imagery is of stockpiles—accumulated dispositions, affections, and moral orientation—that shape what eventually surfaces.

**Speech as symptom.** In this sense words function as symptoms of the heart’s stockpile. Jesus’ polemic against the “generation of vipers” turns on exactly this: they cannot speak good because they are evil. Their failure is not primarily a matter of technique but of internal condition. Speech exposes what was already there.

**Speech as revelation.** Because the treasure is hidden, speech becomes the place where the hidden becomes visible. The mouth “bringeth forth” (v. 35) what the heart has stored—words are a kind of outward attestation, a disclosure of inner disposition. What is spoken is not merely private noise but a window onto the moral furniture of the heart.

**Speech as choice.** At the same time, the passage does not treat words as involuntary reflexes alone. Good men “bring forth” good things; evil men “bring forth” evil things. The verb is deliberative and purposive. Speech is an act—something the person does out of what they have—so it involves agency, decision, and moral responsibility. The abundance may be a stockpile, but the bringing forth is still something the speaker does.

**All three, but with a hierarchy.** The passage suggests that speech is at once symptom, revelation, and choice—but in a chain: the heart is the source, the word is the symptom that discloses, and the choice of word is the expression of the heart’s moral orientation. It is not that words are *merely* symptoms (they do real work and express real judgment), nor merely revelations (they are not passive displays but acts), nor merely choices (the choices are constrained and shaped by the hidden stockpile). The passage locates moral weight in the heart while locating accountability in the word—because what comes out of the mouth is both the overflow of what is stored and the responsibility of the one who speaks it.
