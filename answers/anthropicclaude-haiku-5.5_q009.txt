**The passage's definition of victory**

Paul defines victory in two ways. First, victory is about who sets the terms. "Be not overcome of evil" means evil should not dictate your conduct. If an enemy's hostility makes you vengeful, you have been defeated, whatever happens to the enemy. Second, victory is active. "Overcome evil with good" means meeting evil with a positive act, not merely abstaining from retaliation. The verb *nikaō* means to prevail or conquer, so Paul is describing winning, not withdrawing.

The verse is a quotation of Proverbs 25:21–22, and the context matters. Romans 12:14–19 tells believers to bless persecutors, to leave vengeance to God ("Vengeance is mine; I will repay"), and to live peaceably "as much as lieth in you." Verse 20 gives a concrete example: feed the hungry enemy.

**The "coals of fire" phrase**

This is the crux, and it cuts both ways.

- *Repentance reading:* Many interpreters, including Augustine and Chrysostom, took the coals as the shame and conviction that kindness produces, melting hostility into repentance. Some point to the ancient image of carrying a pan of coals on one's head as a sign of penitence, though that evidence is debated.
- *Judgment reading:* Others take the coals as a warning of divine judgment, so that the enemy's guilt is intensified if he refuses kindness.
- *Ambiguity:* The phrase may be deliberately open. Either way, the good act is not presented as a guaranteed technique, and the outcome is handed over to God.

**Weak, manipulative, or strong?**

*Is it weak?* No. Retaliation is the easy path, since it requires only anger. Feeding and watering an enemy requires self-control, humility, and a willingness to absorb cost and insult. Paul is not calling for passivity. Elsewhere in Romans 13 he affirms the state's legitimate use of the sword, so this is not a naive pacifism. Turning the other cheek is a refusal to let hatred define you, which is harder than striking back.

*Is it manipulative?* The phrase "heap coals of fire" can sound like a strategy for causing the enemy shame or distress, and a reader could fairly ask whether kindness offered as a tactic is sincere. The best answer is that the command is coherent only if the good is genuinely directed toward the enemy's welfare. Feeding the hungry is good regardless of its effect on him. If the kindness is a calculated trap for his humiliation, it has already become the thing it was supposed to overcome. Paul's logic makes the motive as important as the act.

*Is it genuinely strong?* Yes, with a clear caveat. It does not promise that enemies will change, and Paul does not claim it always works. Its strength lies in what it refuses to surrender: your character, your conscience, and your agency. It also has a real social logic. Persistent good toward someone who expects hostility can disrupt cycles of retaliation, because it removes the script both sides expect. And it leaves justice where Paul places it, with God, which frees the believer from the impossible task of administering personal vengeance.

**Verdict**

The command is strong, not weak. Its victory is defined less by the enemy's defeat than by your refusal to become like him. The manipulation worry is legitimate only if kindness is used instrumentally; read sincerely, the command is a demanding ethic in which the good is done for the other person and the outcome is left to God. Whether one finds that persuasive depends largely on whether one thinks moral victory can be real even when it fails to win the argument or change the adversary.
