Enemy-love is difficult to take seriously precisely because it seems to violate the grammar of morality as we ordinarily practice it. Most ethical life runs on reciprocity: fairness, desert, proportion. We instinctively organize the world into the deserving and the undeserving, and the enemy sits at the bottom of that ledger. To love the enemy is not merely to be kind in an extreme case—it is to burn the ledger. That is why the command reads less like ethics and more like an offense against common sense.

**Why we can't take it seriously**

There are at least three reasons the command strikes us as incredible:

1. **It contradicts instinct.** Self-preservation and retaliation are wired deep. When someone curses us, the body and the imagination move toward defense and payback before any moral reasoning begins. The command asks us to override not just a habit but something close to a reflex.

2. **It seems to invite exploitation.** A community that loves its enemies looks, from the outside, like prey. The fear is not abstract: abusers, tyrants, and manipulators can weaponize a doctrine of enemy-love, turning it into a demand that victims submit. This legitimate worry makes many people dismiss the whole idea as dangerous naivety.

3. **It demands the impossible.** Love, in our vocabulary, is a feeling—and feelings cannot be commanded. You cannot decide to feel warmth toward someone who has harmed you. So the command seems to legislate an inner state no one can produce, which makes it easy to file away as rhetoric rather than requirement.

**Is it sentimental?**

No—sentimentality is actually the *easiest* way to avoid the command. If enemy-love means "feel fondly toward those who hurt you," it fails immediately and lets us off the hook: since we can't manage the feeling, we assume Jesus couldn't have meant it literally. But the love in Matthew 5 is *agape*—not affection but active willing of the good of the other. It shows up in the verbs: *bless*, *do good*, *pray for*. These are things you can do while your feelings are still raw. The command doesn't ask you to enjoy your enemy; it asks you to refuse to let hatred have the last word in your actions. Sentimentalizing the passage is a way of not taking it seriously while appearing to.

**Is it strategic?**

Partly—but only partly, and the partial truth is a trap. There is real strategic power here: Gandhi and King demonstrated that nonviolent enemy-love can expose injustice, win over bystanders, and break cycles of retaliation more effectively than violence. Loving your enemy can, in fact, *work*. But notice the danger: if love is justified by its results, it is no longer love—it is a technique. The moment the enemy doesn't convert, doesn't relent, doesn't respond, the "strategic" lover has no reason to continue. A love that is really a means to victory will be abandoned the moment it fails as a means. The Sermon on the Mount does not say "love your enemies so that they will stop being your enemies." It grounds the command elsewhere entirely.

**The deeper ground: moral strength**

The actual justification in the text is striking: "That ye may be the children of your Father which is in heaven: for he maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good." The argument is not utility, not sentiment, but *imitation of God*—specifically, God's radical impartiality. The sun does not check the moral record before rising. Rain does not audit its recipients. Enemy-love is the human participation in this pattern of goodness that precedes desert.

This is where enemy-love reveals itself as strength rather than weakness:

- **It refuses to be defined by the enemy.** Hatred is a form of bondage—your enemy occupies your imagination, dictates your responses, sets your agenda. To love is to be free from that captivity. The one who hates you no longer gets to decide who you become.
- **It breaks the mimetic cycle.** Violence and contempt reproduce themselves by imitation; the enemy's hatred makes you into a mirror of the enemy. Enemy-love is the only move that ends the pattern rather than escalating it.
- **It exceeds reciproality rather than abolishing justice.** Notice the surrounding context in Matthew 5: "Do not even the publicans the same?" The command isn't asking for ordinary decency—it's asking for a goodness that goes beyond what any system of exchange can account for. Justice remains; but it is no longer the ceiling of moral life, only the floor.

**The honest cost**

None of this makes it easy, and it should not be preached as though it were. Enemy-love can be misused to silence victims. It does not require remaining within reach of a dangerous person—loving someone and protecting yourself from them are compatible. And it costs something real: the surrender of the ego's demand for vindication, the grievance we secretly treasure, the pleasure of imagining the enemy's downfall. What the command asks for is nothing less than a kind of death—the death of the self that needs to win.

That is why it is hard to take seriously. Not because it is weak, but because it is terrifyingly strong—stronger than our instinct for self-protection, stronger than our love of justice-as-payback. Sentimentality makes it soft; strategy makes it conditional. Only as moral strength—as a deliberate, costly, God-imitating act of freedom—does the command hold together. The sun rises on the evil and the good without distinction. The question the passage puts to us is whether we are willing to be that kind of people.
