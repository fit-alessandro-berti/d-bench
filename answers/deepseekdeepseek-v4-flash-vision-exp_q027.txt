The opening line of the parable is the key:

> “He spoke this parable unto certain which trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and despised others” (Luke 18:9).

So the exact sin condemned is **self-righteousness**—the settled trust in one’s own moral record, which leads to contempt for others. But the other things you mention are not separate sins; they are all facets of that one root sin.

## What condemns the Pharisee?

### 1. False doctrine (in a practical sense)
The Pharisee probably believed correct theology in many ways, but his *functional* gospel was false. He believed he stood before God because of what he did and what he did *not* do. That is justification by works. He was not trusting God’s mercy; he was trusting his own righteousness. This is not just a personality flaw; it is a wrong gospel.

### 2. Pride
His prayer is all about himself. Listen to the words:

> “God, I thank thee, that I am not as other men are… I fast twice in the week, I give tithes of all that I possess.”

There is no petition. He asks God for nothing. He does not confess sin. He does not ask for mercy. He thanks God, but the real object of his attention is himself. He is proud that he is not like the publican.

### 3. Comparison
The Pharisee’s standard of righteousness is horizontal, not vertical. He does not measure himself before the holiness of God; he measures himself against other people. He says, “I am not as other men are—extortioners, unjust, adulterers, or even as this publican.”

That is why Jesus framed the parable as a warning to those who “despised others.” The Pharisee needs to look down on someone to feel righteous. His righteousness is only real by comparison, and that is not righteousness before God.

### 4. Contempt
He does not just compare himself with the publican; he despises him. “Even as this publican” is a sneer. The tax collector is standing in the temple too, praying too, but the Pharisee treats him as a moral category, not a person. Self-righteousness always produces contempt, because if you are justified by your behavior, then people with worse behavior are threats to your self-image.

### 5. Use of virtue as self-display
Fasting and tithing were not bad. The problem was that the Pharisee used them as credentials. He was not simply obeying God; he was displaying his résumé to God. His virtues became a claim on God rather than a response to God. That is what “prayed thus with himself” means: his prayer was circular—he was talking to himself about himself, using God as his audience.

So the answer is: **the root is self-righteous pride**, which produces a false gospel, comparison, contempt, and the misuse of virtue as self-display.

## Why is the tax collector’s posture spiritually truer?

The tax collector’s posture tells the truth about God and about man:

> “And the publican, standing afar off, would not lift up so much as his eyes unto heaven, but smote upon his breast, saying, God be merciful to me a sinner.”

He stands far off, because he knows he has no right to approach.  
He will not lift his eyes, because he is ashamed.  
He beats his breast, because he mourns his sin.  
He prays, “God be merciful to me a sinner,” not “God, look at how much better I am.”

His posture is spiritually truer because it is the only true posture before God. God is holy; we are sinners. We have no claim on Him. The publican does not compare himself to anyone else. He doesn’t say, “I thank you I am not like that Pharisee.” He simply tells the truth about himself and asks for mercy.

The Greek word translated “be merciful” (hilaskomai) in Luke 18:13 can mean “be propitiated toward” or “show atoning mercy.” The publican is asking not for a pat on the back, but for God’s merciful atonement for his sin. That is faith.

## The result

Jesus says:

> “I tell you, this man went down to his house justified rather than the other.”

He was declared righteous by God, not by himself. The publican’s humility did not *earn* justification; it *received* it. He had nothing to offer, so he threw himself on God’s mercy—and God gave him righteousness.

The Pharisee condemned himself by trusting in himself. The publican was justified by trusting in God’s mercy. And the parable ends with the exact point:

> “For every one that exalteth himself shall be abased; and he that humbleth himself shall be exalted.”
