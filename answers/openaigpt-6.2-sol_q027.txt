Luke gives the key in the opening verse: Jesus tells the parable to people who “trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and despised others.” **Self-trust and contempt are the two faults Luke names.** Pride is the root; comparison is how it works in the prayer; self-display is what his virtues become when he recites them as evidence of his standing before God.

Nothing in the parable suggests that fasting or tithing is wrong, or even that the Pharisee is lying about what he does. Nor does Jesus single out a false doctrine that the man professes. His prayer begins with “God, I thank thee,” but its center is “I am not as other men are.” The tax collector becomes a foil: someone whose presumed inferiority helps the Pharisee assure himself of his own righteousness. Even if every item on his moral résumé is true, the conclusion he draws from it is not his to make.

The tax collector’s posture is spiritually truer not because lowered eyes or beating one’s breast earns favor, nor because his sins are less serious. It is truer because **he tells the truth about his need and leaves the verdict to God**: “God be merciful to me a sinner.” He does not excuse himself or look for someone worse. The Pharisee comes to prayer with a favorable verdict already in hand; the tax collector comes asking for mercy, and Jesus says it is he who goes home justified.
