**"Way" and "thoughts" together cover the whole person.** The verse uses Hebrew parallelism: the wicked man's *way* (*derek*, a path or habitual course of life) is paired with the unrighteous man's *thoughts* (*machashavot*, a word that means plans, designs, and schemes rather than passing ideas). The call is to abandon both the life one has been living and the inner designs that produce it.

## Why both are named

- **Conduct grows from the inner life.** Scripture consistently treats the heart as the source of action (Gen. 6:5; Prov. 4:23; Jer. 4:14; Mark 7:21). Changing behavior while leaving its source untouched leaves the root in place.
- **Each without the other is a counterfeit.** Changed conduct with unchanged thoughts is conformity or hypocrisy. Changed feelings with unchanged conduct is sentiment or self-deception.
- **Repentance is relational, not just moral.** The verse has two movements: *forsake* and *return unto the Lord*. The goal is not self-improvement but a restored relationship, and a relationship involves the whole person.

## The link to verses 8–9

The next lines use the same two words: "For my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways." The sinner is asked to forsake exactly the two things in which he differs from God, so repentance is an exchange of one's own ways and thoughts for God's.

The word "for" also matters. In context, the height of God's thoughts above ours explains the pardon. God forgives on a scale humans would not expect or practice.

## What this implies about mercy

- **Mercy reaches as deep as the sin.** If repentance extends to the imagination, so does pardon. "Abundantly pardon" is literally "he will multiply to pardon."
- **Mercy is not purchased by repentance.** The chapter opens with "come, buy... without money and without price." Repentance is not payment. It is turning toward the place where mercy is already offered.
- **Some of the thoughts to forsake are thoughts about God.** These include the despairing thought that one is beyond forgiveness, the proud thought that one must earn it, and the presumptuous thought that one need not seek it. The first two are answered by "my thoughts are not your thoughts," and the last by "while he may be found," which gives the invitation urgency.

In short, the passage treats sin as going all the way down, from deeds to the designs behind them, and mercy as going down just as far. The demand is total, but so is the promise.
