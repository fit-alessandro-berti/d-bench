The difference is not between **obedience and disobedience**, but between **outward action that leaves the inner person unchanged** and **a whole-person transformation that makes obedience willing and Spirit-empowered**.

### External compliance

External compliance means doing what is required while the heart remains resistant, divided, or indifferent. It may be motivated by:

- fear of punishment,
- social pressure or reputation,
- a desire to appear righteous,
- or simply self-interest.

Such conduct can be genuine in part, but it is limited. A person may keep the rules while lacking trust, love, and repentance toward God. External obedience addresses the actions; it does not heal the underlying disposition.

In the OT, the “heart” represents more than emotions. It includes the mind, will, conscience, desires, moral courage, and fundamental orientation. A “stony heart” is therefore not merely a bad attitude; it is a settled orientation of the whole person toward self, sin, or resistance to God.

### Receiving a new heart

A “new heart” is God’s transformation of that inner orientation. It involves:

- a renewed understanding of God,
- repentance and trust,
- a tender conscience,
- reordered desires,
- and a willing desire to please God.

It is not simply trying harder, developing a better attitude, or becoming emotionally enthusiastic. It is a divine work: “I will cleanse you… A new heart also will I give you… And I will put my spirit within you.”

Ezekiel’s sequence is important:

1. God cleanses.
2. God gives a new heart and spirit.
3. God indwells His Spirit.
4. The Spirit causes the people to “walk in my statutes” and “do them.”

Thus, the new heart is not a substitute for visible obedience. Rather, it becomes the source of willing, Spirit-enabled obedience. External compliance says, “This is what the rule requires of me.” A new heart says, “I belong to God, and I desire to do His will.”

### Why hardness is described as a material condition

“Stony heart” and “heart of flesh” are metaphors, not references to a literal substance lodged in the body. Concrete physical language is used to portray an inner spiritual reality:

- **Stone** suggests callousness, rigidity, and resistance. A stony heart cannot be moved, instructed, or penetrated by God’s word.
- **Flesh** suggests tenderness, responsiveness, and life—not sinfulness or mere human effort.

The verbs “take away” and “give” emphasize that superficial adjustment is not enough. Something fundamentally wrong with the old governing disposition must be relinquished, and God must give a new capacity to will and obey. The promise is therefore of **whole-person renewal**, not merely better behavior.

Theologically, this also guards against legalism. Stronger external enforcement can produce more outward conformity, but it cannot create the trust, love, and willing devotion that God wants. Likewise, the “heart of flesh” is not strong enough to overcome sin by itself; it depends entirely on the indwelling Spirit.

So Ezekiel is not contrasting inward renewal and outward obedience as alternatives. He is showing that God works from the inside out: **God changes the heart, and then the life begins to walk in God’s ways.**
