Jeremiah is pessimistic about the heart’s ability to judge itself fairly, but the passage is about more than the limits of self-knowledge. In context, Jeremiah has contrasted trust in human strength with trust in the Lord (17:5–8). The “heart” is not just the seat of feelings; it is where a person reasons, chooses, and places trust. The danger is moral as well as psychological: what I want can shape what I am willing to see.

The turn comes between “who can know it?” and “I the Lord search the heart.” The answer is not that motives are unknowable, but that **God knows them in a way we do not**. And because the verse connects the heart with a person’s “ways” and their “fruit,” a claim about good intentions does not settle the question of how one has acted.

That suggests speaking about motives with **humility, not blanket suspicion**. “I meant to help” can be an honest account without being the whole account. A more searching version might be: “I meant to help, but I notice I kept pressing after you said no. Maybe being seen as helpful mattered to me, too.” That takes conduct seriously without pretending it proves a hidden motive. The same restraint applies to others: “You kept pressing after I said no” is firmer and fairer than “You only wanted to look generous.”

The aim is not endless doubt about whether one’s motives are pure. It is the freedom to give one’s best account, let it be corrected, and take responsibility for what one did even when one’s motives remain mixed.
