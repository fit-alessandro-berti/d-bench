The self being denied is not the self God created — your body, personality, gifts, desires, or personhood. It is the self as *lord*.

That distinction is crucial in the passage itself. In the next breath Jesus asks, “What shall it profit a man, if he shall gain the whole world, and lose his own soul?” (Mark 8:36). A soul that can be lost at such cost is not worthless. Jesus is not teaching self-hatred. He is attacking self-sovereignty.

### The context: Peter's kind of self

Jesus says this right after Peter confesses him as the Christ — and then tries to stop him from suffering. Peter wants a Messiah who will succeed, conquer, and preserve life, not be rejected and killed. Jesus rebukes him: “Thou savourest not the things that be of God, but the things that be of men.”

Then he turns to the crowd and universalizes it: If you want *that* kind of Messiah, you can’t follow me. Following means denying the part of us that, like Peter, wants glory without the cross, resurrection without death, salvation on our own terms.

The Greek word rendered “life” in v.35 is *psyche* — life, soul, self. The paradox is deliberate:

> Whoever tries to secure his *psyche* will lose it; whoever surrenders his *psyche* for Christ and the gospel will save it.

The self to be denied, then, is the grasping, self-protecting, self-defining ego:

- The self that must be in control
- The self that must be approved of, successful, safe, and vindicated
- The self that makes its own preservation the ultimate good
- The self that tries to be its own savior

Augustine and Luther called this the self “curved in on itself.” Jesus is not saying, “Deny yourself *things*” — occasional pleasures, comforts. He says deny *yourself*: dethrone the ego from the center.

And “take up his cross” would not have sounded like a metaphor for minor inconveniences. A cross was not jewelry. It was Rome’s instrument of shameful public execution. To carry one was to be already reckoned dead — to reputation, security, and future prospects. Jesus is calling for a loyalty so complete that even self-preservation is no longer absolute.

### Why it sounds liberating to some

For people exhausted by the project of saving themselves, this is good news.

The ego is a terrible master. If your life is built on protecting your image, proving your worth, controlling outcomes, or never being weak, you are never free. You are always defending a self that is fragile and insatiable.

To such a person, Jesus is saying: *You don’t have to be your own Messiah.* You can stop clutching your life. Your true life is not something you manufacture or defend; it is something you receive.

That is why losing can feel like finding. When the self is no longer the center, life gets larger: there is room for love that is not self-serving, courage that is not self-protecting, joy that does not depend on winning. Many addicts, perfectionists, strivers, and sufferers hear grace here: the prison is not their personhood, but the desperate need to secure it.

### Why it sounds offensive to others

To a culture formed by “be true to yourself,” “follow your heart,” and “you do you,” this sounds like the exact opposite of wisdom. Modernity treats autonomy as sacred: the authentic self is discovered inside and then expressed, never denied. Jesus says the deepest self is found by surrender.

It is also offensive because it exposes our preferred version of following Jesus: Jesus as helper for *my* life project — my success, my healing, my morality, my nation, my comfort. Jesus refuses to be an accessory to the ego. He calls not just for admiration, but allegiance.

And there is a more serious reason for offense. “Deny yourself” has been misused — especially against women, the abused, the poor, and the oppressed — to mean: accept mistreatment, silence yourself, stay small, let others dominate you.

That is a distortion. Jesus’ call is voluntary — “Whosoever *will* come after me” — and it is “for my sake and the gospel's,” not for an abuser’s sake or an empire’s sake. He does not sanctify victimization imposed by others. The cross he calls disciples to is not self-erasure for the benefit of the powerful; it is the costly path of love that leads, as his own did, to resurrection. The goal is not a diminished self, but a saved one.

### The paradox

So the same sentence liberates or offends depending on what we think we are losing.

If the self feels like a burden we are tired of carrying, denial sounds like freedom.
If the self feels like a possession we must protect at all costs, denial sounds like death.

Jesus insists it is both — but in that order. The false, sovereign self must die, so that the true self, received from God and lived for others, can live.
