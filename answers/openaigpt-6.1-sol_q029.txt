The posture required is **restorative humility**: taking another person’s wrongdoing seriously without treating yourself as morally above them.

Paul keeps three things together because each guards the others from distortion:

- **Restoration gives correction its purpose.** “Restore such an one” means the aim is to bring the person back to faithful life—not to humiliate them, defeat them in an argument, or establish the corrector’s superiority. The fault must be addressed, but the person is not reduced to the fault.

- **Meekness governs how correction is given.** Meekness is not indifference to wrongdoing or unwillingness to speak plainly. It is strength exercised without arrogance, harshness, or vindictiveness. The “spiritual” person is someone whose conduct bears the Spirit’s character, not someone entitled to look down on others.

- **Self-suspicion keeps the corrector accountable.** “Considering thyself, lest thou also be tempted” requires awareness of one’s own vulnerability. The corrector is not immune to the same sin—or to the pride, anger, and self-righteousness that can arise in correcting it. This is sober self-examination, not paralyzing doubt about whether any moral judgment is possible.

These belong together because **restoration without meekness can become coercive; meekness without restoration can become mere avoidance; and both without self-examination can become paternalistic**. Paul calls for neither condemnation from a distance nor permissiveness disguised as kindness.

The next verse—“Bear ye one another’s burdens”—reinforces the posture: correction belongs within shared responsibility, not detached superiority.

The underlying stance is: *“What you have done needs correction, and I want your good. But I approach you as someone who also needs grace, vigilance, and correction.”* That allows moral clarity without moral contempt.
