## Pessimism or realism?

I think it is mostly realism about self-deception, though a severe form of it. Three things point that way:

- **The Hebrew is more diagnostic than condemnatory.** The word translated "deceitful" (*ʿāqōb*) means crooked or tortuous and shares a root with the name Jacob, the supplanter. "Desperately wicked" (*ʾānûš*) is better rendered "incurable" or "desperately sick," as most modern translations have it. The image is of a patient who cannot diagnose or cure himself rather than a villain.

- **The question is epistemic.** "Who can know it?" The problem is opacity. The verse doesn't say every motive is evil, only that the heart is a poor witness in its own case.

- **Verse 10 answers verse 9.** The heart is not unknowable, only unknowable from the inside. The context supports this: the preceding verses contrast trusting in man with trusting in God, and a few verses later Jeremiah prays, "Heal me, O Lord, and I shall be healed." The same book later promises a law written on the heart (31:33). An incurable condition that God heals is not final pessimism.

Traditions do weigh this differently. Reformed readers treat the verse as a key text for radical depravity, while Jewish readings tend to stress God's knowledge of hidden things rather than a fixed verdict on human nature.

## How to talk about motives

If the heart is an unreliable witness, a few practices follow:

1. **Hold your own motives tentatively.** Paul models this: "I am not aware of anything against myself, but I am not thereby acquitted... it is the Lord who judges me" (1 Cor. 4:4). You can say "as far as I can tell, my reason is..." without claiming certainty.

2. **Weigh conduct over introspection.** Verse 10 itself points to "ways" and "the fruit of his doings." Patterns of behavior over time are better evidence than how your intentions feel in the moment.

3. **Invite outside scrutiny.** The Psalmist's response to this problem is "Search me, O God, and know my heart" (Ps. 139:23), and "Who can discern his errors?" (Ps. 19:12). Honest friends and community serve the same purpose on a human scale.

4. **Apply the suspicion to yourself first.** The verse is often used against others ("you can't trust your heart"). But if I cannot fully read my own heart, I certainly cannot read yours. The passage reserves heart-searching for God, which should make us slow to attribute motives to anyone.

5. **Don't swing into paralysis.** A deceitful heart can also manufacture false guilt, and endless motive-auditing is its own trap. Since judgment falls on "ways" and "doings," you can act faithfully with mixed motives rather than waiting for a purity you could never verify.

In short, the passage asks for humility about motives, not despair about them. You can still speak of your motives, but as testimony open to correction rather than as a verdict.
