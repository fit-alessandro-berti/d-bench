This verse from Isaiah 55:6–7 has been read for centuries as a call to wholehearted turning—repentance that reaches both the visible and the invisible dimensions of a person. Let me unpack what the pairing of "way" and "thoughts" implies.

## The two dimensions

**"The wicked forsake his way"** addresses outward conduct. A "way" (Hebrew *derek*) is a path—a pattern of action, a direction of life. To forsake a way means to turn from a trajectory of deeds, to stop walking the roads one has been traveling. This is repentance that is at least partly public and observable: it shows up in what a person *does*.

**"The unrighteous man his thoughts"** addresses inward imagination. A "thought" (Hebrew *machashavah*) is the movement of the mind before it becomes deed. To turn from one's thoughts means to be concerned with the interior life—the fantasies, assumptions, and mental habits that may never leave a trace outwardly but still shape the person. This is the deeper, hidden register.

The pairing is deliberate. Scripture refuses to let repentance be either pure outward correction (self-improvement, moral tidying) or pure inward sentiment (feeling remorse without change). It asks for both.

## What this implies

**1. Outward conduct matters, but is not the whole matter.**
The command to forsake a "way" shows that repentance has real, visible content. It is not merely an inner mood. Behavior genuinely turns, and that is good—it is the Bible's insistence that faith and practice belong together. But the call to change one's "thoughts" reveals that the outward way is a *symptom* and *expression* of the inward life, not its sole root. A person can stop a behavior and still harbor the heart that produced it; Scripture treats that as incomplete.

**2. Inward imagination is morally serious.**
To command a change of *thoughts* is to say that what we dwell on—the imagined wrongs, the resentments, the plans, the self-justifying narratives—matters before God even when no one else sees. This is why the tradition (echoed in Matthew 5:28 and Philippians 4:8) treats the interior life as the place where true transformation is either happening or not. Repentance that stops at conduct is like cleaning the windows while leaving the fog machine running.

**3. Both dimensions require the same turning.**
Notice the sequence: *return unto the Lord*. The turning is one act that reaches from the interior to the exterior. You cannot genuinely return to God in your thoughts while keeping your way, or return in your way while pretending your thoughts are unchanged. The "way" is where the "thoughts" walk out; the "thoughts" are where the "way" is formed. Repentance is a reorientation of the whole person, not a repair shop for one department.

## On the possibility of mercy

The verse closes by addressing God, not the sinner: "he will have mercy upon him... for he will abundantly pardon." Two things are worth noting.

First, **mercy is grounded in God's character, not the quality of the repentance.** The text does not say, "If you repent perfectly, he will be merciful." It says, "he will abundantly pardon"—the overflowing, generous pardon belongs to God's own nature. The "abundantly" (Hebrew *rab*) suggests superabundance, more than we can deserve or calculate.

Second, **mercy meets the whole person.** Because repentance must reach both way and thought, the mercy God offers cannot be a mere forgiveness of external acts while leaving the inner life untouched. The pardon God gives is total—he restores the turned person from the inside out. This is why the call to "seek him while he may be found" is urgent: the invitation is to the whole transformation, and it comes from the God who abundantly pardons.

In short: repentance here is not an either/or between moral effort and inward feeling. It is a whole-person turning—deeds and thoughts alike—answered by a God whose mercy is rooted in his own abundance rather than in the merits of the repentant.
