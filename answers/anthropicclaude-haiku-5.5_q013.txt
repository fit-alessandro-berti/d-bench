The sharpness comes from the diagnosis. Amos isn't saying that Israel has the right religious practices in the wrong proportion. He says that the practices, however correct in form, no longer reach God, because they are offered by people whose public life contradicts what the worship claims to express.

**Why not "better balance"?**

A call for balance assumes that worship and justice are separate goods that can be weighed against each other. Amos denies that. In the surrounding text (5:7, 10–15; 2:6–8; 8:4–6), the sins are concrete: bribery at the gate, crushing the poor, turning aside the needy, selling the righteous for silver. These are covenant violations, and in the Torah they are the content of holiness, not a separate department from it (Lev. 19 is the clearest case: holiness is expressed in gleaning rights, honest weights, and not oppressing the neighbor). So when Amos says God "will not smell" the offerings, he is not ranking ethics above ritual. He is saying the ritual has lost its meaning because it no longer communicates what it is meant to communicate: a people bound to a just God.

The verbs are strong ("I hate, I despise," "I will not accept," "I will not hear"), and they are relational language. The feasts are called "your" feasts. The worship has been detached from the God who commanded it and made into a possession of the worshippers.

**The fraud**

Several layers of self-deception are visible:

1. **Ritual as currency.** Sacrifice functions as a payment or insurance policy. People think that proper offerings secure divine favor, and that favor licenses whatever they do afterward. Amos 8:5 makes this explicit: the merchants cannot wait for the new moon and Sabbath to end before cheating customers. Religious observance and economic fraud run on the same schedule.

2. **Election as entitlement.** Israel assumes that being God's chosen people guarantees divine favor regardless of conduct (3:2 turns this on its head: "you only have I known, therefore I will punish you"). Amos 5:14 ("so the LORD God of hosts shall be with you") and 5:18–20 (the "day of the Lord" as a day of vindication for Israel) show the same confidence. Worship becomes a way of claiming a status rather than submitting to a Lord.

3. **Worship as performance that manages guilt.** The festivals, songs, and sacrifices produce a feeling of rightness that dulls the conscience. The more elaborate the liturgy, the less the worshippers notice what their hands are doing. This is why Amos attacks the music and the noise: they are the sound of a people who have stopped hearing.

**Why the contrast is "judgment… as waters"**

Verse 24 doesn't say "in addition." The contrast is with the festival rhythm: feasts come and go, while justice should flow continuously, like a perennial stream. The image describes a character trait that shows up in daily life, not a periodic act.

**A caution**

Amos doesn't abolish worship, and Jesus' later comment in Matthew 23:23 ("these ought ye to have done, and not to leave the other undone") shows that the same logic applies to tithing and justice: the rebuke is for treating one as a substitute for the other. The lesson isn't that ritual is worthless. It is that worship is a form of relationship, and a relationship with the God of the oppressed cannot coexist with the oppression of the poor and the courts' corruption. Worship offered in those conditions isn't just weaker than it should be. It is a lie about who God is and who the worshippers are.
