The parable’s target is given in the opening verse:

> “He spoke this parable unto certain which trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and despised others.”

So the **exact** thing that condemns the Pharisee is **self-justification**: trusting in his own righteousness as the basis of his standing before God. Every other problem in the parable flows from that root.

- **False doctrine** — Yes, but in practice more than in formal belief: he lives as if God accepts him because of his own moral and religious achievements. He has no real need for mercy, because he believes he is already righteous.
- **Pride** — Yes: he “stood and prayed thus with himself.” His prayer is not really conversation with God; it is self-congratulation in God’s presence.
- **Comparison** — Yes: “God, I thank thee that I am not as other men are.” He defines his righteousness downward, by looking at people he considers worse than himself.
- **Contempt** — Yes: he specifically despises the publican: “or even as this publican.” The tax collector is not a neighbor to him; he is a proof of the Pharisee’s own superiority.
- **Self-display** — Yes: “I fast twice in the week, I give tithes of all that I possess.” These are good practices, but he uses them as a resumé—as if God owes him praise or reward.

All of these are symptoms of the central disease: **he trusted in himself that he was righteous**.

## Why is the tax collector’s posture spiritually truer?

The tax collector tells the truth about reality:

- He stands “afar off,” unable to approach with confidence because he knows his guilt.
- He “would not lift up so much as his eyes unto heaven”—not superstition, but honesty about unworthiness.
- He “smote upon his breast”—a physical gesture of grief and repentance.
- He prays: **“God be merciful to me a sinner.”**

This is not a clever act of humility. It is the only honest position before God. He does not compare himself to anyone else. He does not offer his good deeds. He asks for mercy, and mercy is exactly what he receives:

> “I tell you, this man went down to his house justified rather than the other.”

The Pharisee was not condemned for fasting and tithing. Those things were not the problem. The problem was trusting in them to make himself righteous and then despising anyone who did not measure up. The tax collector is not justified because his humility is a meritorious work; humility is the **empty hand** that receives grace. He sees himself as God sees him—a sinner in need of mercy—and that is why Jesus gives the final principle:

> “Everyone who exalts himself will be humbled, but the one who humbles himself will be exalted.”
