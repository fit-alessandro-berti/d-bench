Genesis 4:6–7 draws a striking picture of moral agency: the human being stands **at a threshold**, and what happens next depends on a real choice. Evil is not depicted as an irresistible impulse or an inescapable habit. It is pictured as a **crouching, predatory power waiting at the door**.

God first asks Cain:  
> “Why art thou wroth? and why is thy countenance fallen?”

This is a call to self-examination. Cain’s anger is not just something that happened to him; he is responsible for what is happening in his heart. His “fallen face” is a warning sign. Then God offers him the way back:

> “If thou doest well, shalt thou not be accepted?”

The word “accepted” could also be translated “lifted up.” In other words, if Cain does good, his face can be lifted again. The remedy is not a better sacrifice, but a turning of the heart. Moral agency means that the good is available to him, and his response determines his standing.

Then comes the vivid warning:

> “If thou doest not well, sin lieth at the door.”

The Hebrew verb for “lieth” is *rōbēṣ*, which means **to crouch**, like an animal waiting to pounce. Sin is not an abstract idea or a mechanical impulse. It is a living, dangerous presence crouching at the entrance of Cain’s life. It is at the door—**not yet inside, not yet in control**. But it is waiting.

Then God says:

> “Unto thee shall be his desire, and thou shalt rule over him.”

The word “desire” (*t’shuqah*) is the same word used in Genesis 3:16, where the woman’s desire is toward her husband and he rules over her. Here, sin’s “desire” is toward Cain: sin wants to have him, to master him. But the command is clear: **Cain must rule over sin.**

So what kind of evil is this? It is like a **beast**, but not a blind beast. It is a personal, predatory force of temptation that wants to dominate the human heart. It is at the door because a person can still choose. Sin is not yet a habit, and it is not yet a destiny. It is an intruder seeking a home, and the human being is the gatekeeper.

The responsibility is entirely on Cain. God does not say, “You are doomed,” or “Your anger is too strong.” He says:

- You can do well.
- You will be accepted if you do.
- If you do not, sin is waiting.
- Its desire is for you.
- But you shall rule over it.

This is the essence of moral agency in the text: human beings are not helpless victims of evil. They are called to stand at the door of their own hearts and **rule over the crouching enemy**. Cain later fails because he does not rule. He lets sin in, and sin rules him. But that failure was not inevitable. Genesis 4:7 shows that, by God’s word and by human responsibility, the door can stay closed—if the person will do well.
