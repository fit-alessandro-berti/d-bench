## Treasure, Heart, and the Circuit of Attachment

### The Two Readings

The verse most naturally invites the question you pose, and the answer is that it operates on **both levels simultaneously** — but with a decisive emphasis on the second.

**Treasure as mirror:** In one sense, what you treasure reveals what your heart already was. If someone's hoard is material wealth, their heart was already going there. The treasure is evidence, a symptom.

**Treasure as training ground:** But the **logic of the passage** — particularly the causal connector *γάρ* ("for") — points the other direction. The sentence "for where your treasure is, there will your heart be also" is the **reason Jesus gives for the commands that precede it.** The structure is:

> Do not lay up earthly treasure — *because* your heart will follow it.
> Do lay up heavenly treasure — *because* your heart will follow it.

If treasure merely *revealed* the heart, this reasoning would be circular and useless as motivation. You would need to change your heart first and then your treasure would follow. But Jesus commands the **practical, external decision first**, and treats the heart as the downstream consequence. His pastoral logic says: *where you put your stuff, your affections will be dragged along behind.*

So **treasure trains the heart.**

### The Circuit: Possession → Attention → Love

The three terms you name form a self-reinforcing loop:

**Possession → Attention.** The moment you own something, you attend to it. You check on it, guard it, think about it, worry about it. The investor watches the market. The homeowner worries about the roof. Ownership creates a permanent field of cognitive and emotional investment. You did not choose to spend mental energy on it; the possession imposed itself on your awareness.

**Attention → Love.** Where attention settles over time, affection deepens. We become attached to what we continually look at. Love does not appear fully formed — it *accretes* through sustained regard. A person who checks a particular investment forty times a day, though initially just being prudent, begins to *care* about it in a way that exceeds its instrumental value. Attention is the soil in which love grows.

**Love → Possession (intensified).** What we love, we want to secure, protect, and increase. The cycle closes: deepened love produces deeper attachment to possession, which generates more attention, which deepens love further.

This is why Jesus does not simply say "don't *love* earthly things." That would be addressing only the third stage of a cycle that has already been set in motion by the first. He addresses **laying up** — the act of possession — because that is the point of entry where the entire circuit begins.

### Why "Moth and Rust" Matters Here

The imagery of moth and rust corrupting is not just about material decay. It reveals something about the **quality of the attention the cycle produces.** Earthly treasure trains your heart into a posture of *anxiety* — checking, guarding, worrying about what is already being eaten away. The cycle becomes vicious: possession demands attention, attention produces worry and grasping, worry deepens attachment to the very thing that is disappearing.

Heavenly treasure, by contrast, cannot decay. When your heart is trained toward it, the attention it generates does not produce anxiety but **security** — the "treasure in heaven" is stable, so your heart rests rather than grasps.

### The Summary

The text says **both** — but the operative claim is that **treasure trains the heart.** Possession enlists attention, attention cultivates love, and love reinforces the original possession. Jesus breaks the cycle not at the level of feeling (he does not say "feel less attached") but at the level of **practice**: don't lay it up. The heart follows where the hands have placed the treasure.
