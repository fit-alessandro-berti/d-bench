## Why it's hard to take seriously

**1. It cuts against how moral life usually works.** Most ethics is reciprocal: help friends, resist enemies, and give people what they've earned. Enemy-love breaks that ledger. It can feel like a betrayal of justice, or of the people the enemy has harmed.

**2. It gets caricatured.** We tend to hear "love" as warm feeling, so the command sounds either impossible (you can't manufacture affection for a persecutor) or fake (pretending you aren't angry). Hearing it as sentiment makes it easy to dismiss.

**3. It's often been misused.** It has been turned on victims, telling the abused to stay quiet and the oppressed to be docile. That history makes suspicion reasonable.

**4. It exposes us.** Few people have no enemies, and most of us cherish some resentments. The command is uncomfortable because it is clear.

## Sentimental, strategic, or strength?

**Not sentimental.** The verbs are concrete: *bless, do good, pray*. Jesus doesn't say "feel fondly toward." Love here is an orientation of the will, a refusal to let the enemy's conduct determine yours. You can act for someone's good while still grieving, angry, or cautious.

**Not merely strategic.** Enemy-love sometimes works. Gandhi and King showed its power, and kindness can disarm hostility. But the text grounds it elsewhere: *"that ye may be the children of your Father."* The logic is imitation, not outcome. God sends sun and rain on the evil and the good without checking credentials. If love were only a tactic, it would collapse the moment it failed, and Jesus expects that it often will, since the path leads to the cross.

**Closest to a deeper moral strength**, with qualifications:

- It is **freedom from being defined by your enemy**. Hatred keeps you bound to the person who wronged you, and loving them refuses that control.
- It **keeps the enemy human**. That is the precondition for any real reconciliation, and it guards against becoming what you oppose.
- It **doesn't require dropping justice.** Loving an enemy is compatible with restraining them, testifying against them, or protecting others. What it rules out is vengeance and dehumanization. Paul and Augustine both held love and restraint together.
- It **doesn't mean enabling harm.** Seeking someone's good sometimes means resisting what they're doing.

## The real challenge

The passage's boldest claim is theological: God's goodness is not scaled to merit. If so, enemy-love isn't an exotic extra but a picture of what goodness looks like at its root, generosity that doesn't depend on the recipient's worthiness. Nietzsche saw this and called it weakness. The Christian tradition claims the opposite, that only the strong can do it, because it means absorbing harm without passing it on.

Whether you find that persuasive may depend less on the argument than on whether you've seen it done, by someone who forgave without being a doormat. Those examples are rare, but they're why the command keeps its hold.

Do you read it more as a personal ethic, or as something that could guide politics and institutions?
