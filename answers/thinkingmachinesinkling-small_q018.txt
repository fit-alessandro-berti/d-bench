The direct collision in Ephesians 4:31–32 is not accidental; it is **economic**. Paul is describing two incompatible ways of relating to others: one is a system of **retention** (holding a grievance, letting it calcify), the other is a system of **release** (grace transmitted). The command “put away” (Greek *apothethēmi*) is the same word used in 4:22–24 for stripping off the “old self.” Bitterness is not being treated as a private, unavoidable feeling, but as a **chosen posture that can be removed because it has been clothed**—and because it is contagious enough to destroy the church.

Here is how the text treats it:

### 1. Bitterness as a root, not just a mood
The Greek *pikria* (bitterness) evokes sourness, like something chemically corrupted at its source. In Hebrews 12:15, “a root of bitterness” (*rhiza pikrias*) springing up is precisely what defiles a community. Paul lists it first because it is the **seed**; from it grow “wrath” (*thumos*), “anger” (*orgē*), “clamor” (*kraugē*), and “evil speaking” (*kakia* / blasphemia). The progression is from internal disposition to external violence: bitterness is silent, then loud, then maligning. So yes—**it is a feeling, but the text treats it as a habit and a posture that generates more sin**.

### 2. It is volitional (you “put it away”)
The imperative “let it be put away from you” is active. You do not wait for bitterness to dissipate like weather; you **strip it off**. That means the apostle views it as a posture one can—because of God’s forgiveness—**repudiate**. It is not “if you feel hurt,” but “if you are nursing a grievance, remove it.”

### 3. It is contagious
The list moves from private to public. Bitterness does not stay in the heart; it produces “clamor” (shouting, brawling) and “evil speaking” (slander, bad talk about the other). One bitter person poisons the relational atmosphere; this is why it is placed against **kindness** (*chrēstos*) and **tenderheartedness** (*eusplanchnoi*, literally “good bowels”/inner compassions). Forgiveness (*charizomenoi*) is the communicable antidote: because God “graced” (*echarizeto*) you in Christ, you now pass that grace along. Bitterness stops the flow; forgiveness keeps it circulating.

### 4. The direct contrast is christological
The climax is: *“Forgiving one another, even as God for Christ’s sake hath forgiven you.”* Bitterness assumes a **debt is owed to me** that must be retained. Forgiveness assumes a **debt was owed to God**, and God cancelled it “for Christ’s sake” (in Christ’s work). To hold bitterness is therefore to deny the economy of the cross: if God did not retain your debt against you, how can you retain another’s debt against them?

**In short:** The text treats bitterness as an **interior root that becomes an exterior contagion**—a voluntary, relational posture that must be actively removed because it is incompatible with the grace that defines the new self. Forgiveness is not just its opposite emotion; it is the only thing that can replace the logic of grievance with the logic of grace.
