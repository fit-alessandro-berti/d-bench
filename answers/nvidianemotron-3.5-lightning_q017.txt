The passage from John 3:19–21 presents a concise theological and psychological framework for why light–framed here as truth, revelation, or the presence of God–is hated by those whose lives are oriented toward concealment. Here’s an exploration of the dynamics the text raises, moving through the four concepts you mentioned.

### 1. Why light is hated: exposure as threat
The text states that men “loved darkness rather than light, because their deeds were evil.” In this framework, light isn’t merely illumination; it’s **disclosure**. When light enters a space, nothing remains hidden. For someone whose actions are rooted in self-interest, deception, or harm, that kind of visibility is a loss of power. Light removes the ability to:
- Control how one is perceived
- Avoid consequences
- Maintain a false narrative about oneself
- Harm others without accountability

The “hatred” of light, then, is often a defensive reaction: the stronger the light, the less room there is for self-deception or manipulation. The text suggests that this hatred isn’t arbitrary—it’s tied to the nature of the deeds themselves.

### 2. Truth and exposure
Truth, in this passage, functions **existentially**, not just intellectually. It doesn’t just convey facts; it changes the status of a life from “hidden” to “known.” 
- For the one doing “evil” (in the text’s moral framing), truth is threatening because it dismantures the gap between public persona and private action.
- For the one “doing truth,” by contrast, light is welcome because their internal and external alignment means exposure doesn’t threaten their identity. They have nothing to defend or hide.

The passage flips the usual expectation: we typically think of truth as liberating, but for those whose lives are built on secrets, truth feels like an assault.

### 3. Shame as the emotional mechanism
Shame is the feeling that arises when we believe we are seen in ways we don’t want to be seen. The text implies a causal chain:
- Light → exposure → deeds “reproved” (examined, condemned, or shown for what they are)
- The anticipation of reproof triggers avoidance behavior (“neither cometh to the light, lest his deeds should be reproved”)

Shame here isn’t presented as a virtuous feeling, but as the **motivating force** behind hiding. The individual chooses darkness not just to escape punishment, but to escape the internal experience of being “found out.” The hatred of light is, at least partially, an attempt to preemptively manage or eliminate that shame.

### 4. The desire not to be seen clearly
This is a universal human instinct, though the text moralizes it. Psychologically, the desire for obscurity serves several functions:
- **Safety**: Being unseen reduces vulnerability to judgment, rejection, or retaliation.
- **Identity protection**: We often tie our sense of self to being “good,” “smart,” or “righteous.” Exposure risks revealing that the identity we’ve constructed is fragile or false.
- **Autonomy**: Visibility can feel like a loss of control over one’s narrative.

The New Testament framing takes this neutral instinct and locates it morally: when the desire to avoid being seen clearly is coupled with deeds the actor knows are harmful or misaligned with a higher standard (here, “God”), the instinct becomes resistance to truth itself.

### The contrast: coming to the light
The passage ends by pointing to the alternative: “He that doeth truth cometh to the light, that his deeds may be made manifest, that they are wrought in God.” This suggests that the resistance to light isn’t inevitable. When a person’s life is ordered toward honesty, integrity, or alignment with a value larger than self-interest, exposure becomes **affirming** rather than threatening. They don’t need to hide because their actions are already in sync with who they claim to be—or with the standard they aspire to.

### Summary
The text argues that light is hated because it **closes the gap between hidden action and known consequence**. The hatred is rooted in the anticipation of shame, the defense of a self-image built on concealment, and the loss of control that comes with full disclosure. The desire not to be seen clearly is a normal human instinct, but when paired with deeds the actor knows to be harmful or inauthentic, it becomes a rejection of truth itself. The passage positions “coming to the light” not as masochism, but as the natural outcome of a life already oriented toward honesty and alignment with a greater good.
