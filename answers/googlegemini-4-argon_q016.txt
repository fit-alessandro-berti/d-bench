While the elder brother displays every single one of the traits you listed—anger, envy, wounded fairness, pride, and a distaste for mercy—theological reflection on this parable suggests that these are actually **symptoms** of a deeper, singular root problem. 

The elder brother’s deepest problem is **a transactional heart that views himself as a servant rather than a son, which ultimately alienates him from his father’s love.** 

He is just as "lost" as his younger brother was, even though he never left the farm. We can see how this root problem manifests in the text and gives birth to all the other issues you mentioned:

### 1. The Root: "Lo, these many years do I *serve* thee..."
In the original Greek, the word the elder brother uses for "serve" is *douleuō*, which literally means **to be a slave**. 
This is the key to his psyche. He does not view his relationship with his father in terms of filial love; he views it as an economic contract. He puts in the labor and the moral obedience, and in return, he expects compensation (the inheritance, a goat, social status). 

### 2. How this root produces the other symptoms:
Because his relationship with the father is purely transactional, the father’s radical grace shatters the elder brother's entire worldview, triggering the exact issues you listed:
* **Pride in Obedience:** Because he thinks he is working for a wage, he keeps a meticulous ledger of his own righteousness ("neither transgressed I at any time thy commandment"). 
* **Wounded Fairness and Anger:** In a transactional system, grace is an injustice. If the younger brother gets the fatted calf after doing *nothing*, then the elder brother’s years of "slavery" were pointless. His anger is the rage of a businessman who feels he has been cheated out of a contract.
* **Envy:** Notice what he says: "Thou never gavest me a kid, that I might make merry *with my friends*." He didn't even want a feast to celebrate *with his father*; he just wanted the father's stuff so he could party *apart* from the father. In truth, he secretly envied the younger brother’s freedom to indulge himself. Both brothers wanted the father's wealth; neither wanted the father.
* **Discomfort with Mercy:** Mercy cannot be calculated on a ledger. Therefore, the elder brother cannot recognize his sibling, referring to him coldly as *"this thy son"* rather than "my brother." To accept mercy for his brother would mean dismantling the merit-system upon which the elder brother has built his entire identity.

### The Father's Diagnosis
We know that the elder brother’s core problem is a **lack of sonship** because of how the father responds to him. The father does not argue about the goat, the harlots, or the rules. Instead, he goes straight to the relational heart of the matter:

> *"Son [Teknon - 'Child'], thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine."*

The father is essentially saying: *Why are you acting like a starving slave begging for a goat when you already own the whole herd? Why are you working for my stuff when you already have ME?*

### Conclusion
Jesus told this parable in response to the Pharisees and scribes who were grumbling, *"This man receiveth sinners, and eateth with them"* (Luke 15:2). 

Therefore, the elder brother’s deepest problem is **self-righteousness born of spiritual slavery.** While the younger brother tried to escape the father through **rebellion**, the elder brother tried to control the father through **morality**. His discomfort with mercy, his wounded sense of fairness, and his pride are all the natural results of a heart that knows how to obey the Father’s rules, but has never actually known or shared in the Father’s love.
