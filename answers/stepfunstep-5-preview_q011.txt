The text moves from **“cease to do evil”** to **“learn to do well”** because stopping wrongdoing is only the first step, not the whole of righteousness.

In context, Israel’s worship had become empty because their public life was marked by oppression and injustice. So Isaiah tells them not merely to feel sorry, nor even simply to stop the obvious sins, but to acquire a new way of living. The word **“learn”** is significant: goodness is treated as something that must be practiced, taught, and formed—not as something that happens automatically once evil stops.

That suggests goodness is **not mere restraint**. Refraining from harming someone may be better than harming them, but it is not yet the love and justice God calls for. The text immediately defines “doing well” concretely:

> seek justice,  
> relieve the oppressed,  
> defend the fatherless,  
> plead for the widow.

Here goodness becomes visible as active concern for people who are vulnerable and easily exploited. It is not only “don’t be corrupt”; it is “take up the cause of those being crushed.” Justice here is practical: defending the weak, correcting oppression, and making communities reflect God’s own care.

So Isaiah presents goodness as a **practiced form of love and justice**. The negative command opens space—*stop the evil*—but the positive command fills it with a renewed habit of life. Goodness, in other words, is not just a clean conscience; it is a posture of life shaped by compassion, advocacy, and righteousness toward others.
