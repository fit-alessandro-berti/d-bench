# Enemy-Love: Sentimental, Strategic, or Something Harder?

This question is worth taking slowly, because the passage itself is doing something that resists easy classification — and that resistance is precisely what makes it so disorienting.

---

## Why It's So Hard to Take Seriously

Several things conspire to make enemy-love feel like a platitude at first reading:

- **It violates reciprocity.** Humans are wired for tit-for-tat fairness. Refusing to retaliate feels like injustice, even self-abandonment.
- **It seems to erase moral distinctions.** If you love the persecutor alongside the persecuted, have you collapsed the difference between good and evil?
- **It can appear to enable abuse.** Where is the line between forgiving and being passive? Victims are rightly suspicious of teachings that seem designed to keep them compliant.
- **It sounds impossibly idealistic.** We encounter it so often in sentimentalized form — embroidered on pillows, whispered in eulogies — that the actual demand gets papered over.

The difficulty is not that the teaching is vague. It's that it is **painfully concrete** and yet **philosophically disorienting.**

---

## Testing Each Category

### 1. Sentimental?

**Almost certainly not — at least not as the passage actually constructs it.**

Sentimentalism reduces moral action to pleasant feeling. It substitutes warmth for cost. But note what Jesus actually prescribes — not *feelings* but **four distinct actions** in the imperative mood:

| Verb | Demand |
|------|--------|
| **Love** (ἀγαπᾶτε) | A deliberate, willed form of care — *agape*, not *philia* (friendship-affection) |
| **Bless** (εὐλογεῖτε) | Speak well of them — to their face and behind their back |
| **Do good** (καλῶς ποιεῖτε) | Actively benefit them — tangible, costly, concrete |
| **Pray** (προσεύχεσθε) | Stand before God on their behalf — the most intimate and radical of the four |

This is a **program of action**, not a mood. Sentimentality would say, *"Try to feel kindly toward those who hurt you."* This text says, *act on their behalf even when every impulse recoils.* The gap between feeling and action is where moral seriousness lives.

### 2. Strategic?

**This is a tempting but ultimately unsupported reading.**

The instrumental version says: *love your enemies so they'll convert, so they'll stop persecuting you, so you'll win moral high ground and strategic advantage.* Tolstoy, Gandhi, and MLK Jr. did show that enemy-love carries enormous **political power** — nonviolent resistance has toppled empires. And there are real-world fruits.

But the text **doesn't offer strategy as its rationale.** It gives a metaphysical reason:

> *"That ye may be the children of your Father which is in heaven."*

The goal is **ontological** — becoming like God — not **consequentialist**. The proof offered is not "and your enemies will repent" but:

> *"He maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good."*

God sends rain on the unjust **not as a conversion strategy** but because indiscriminate generosity is **the structure of divine being.** The sun falls where it falls. Grace is not an instrument. It is an overflow of nature.

If enemy-love is purely strategic, it collapses the moment the strategy fails — the moment the enemy refuses to change. But the teaching demands loving the enemy **precisely then**, which means it cannot be grounded in expected outcomes.

### 3. Moral Strength?

**This comes closest to the text's own logic — but only if "strength" is understood carefully.**

What makes this a form of *moral strength* rather than weakness?

- **It demands the overcoming of instinct.** Vengeance is natural. This teaching requires a will that overrules the body's demand for retaliation.
- **It refuses moral deformation.** Hatred changes the hater. The passage implies that refusing enemy-love doesn't just fail to convert the enemy — it makes *you* into something ugly. "That ye may be children of your Father" carries the implicit warning: *or you will become children of your enemy's hatred.*
- **It reclaims agency.** The persecuted person is not reduced to reactive passivity. They choose the terms of their response. This is extraordinarily powerful — as the civil rights movement demonstrated at scale.
- **It holds together justice and mercy** without collapsing into either. Jesus loved his enemies but also drove the money changers from the temple. The prophetic tradition of naming injustice is not abandoned; it is **inhabited differently.**

But even here, "moral strength" can be misread as stoic endurance — a kind of noble suffering-through. The passage is richer than that. It describes an **active generosity that mirrors God's own** — not the suppressed bitterness of one who endures, but the genuine overflow of one whose character has been formed by a particular vision of reality.

---

## The Deeper Difficulty

The real reason enemy-love is so hard to take seriously is that it **doesn't fit into any single framework.** It is:

- **Too costly** to be mere sentiment
- **Too unconditional** to be mere strategy
- **Too active** to be mere stoicism
- **Too demanding** to be mere wishful thinking

It asks us to live as though **the moral law of the universe is generosity** — not reciprocity, not justice-as-retribution, but a sun that rises on the evil and the good alike. Whether or not one is Christian, this is a staggering moral claim: that the fundamental structure of reality is **benign**, and that to refuse enemy-love is not just to disobey a command but to **misread the cosmos.**

---

## A Final Tension Worth Holding

The teaching is genuinely dangerous if detached from context. Victims must not be told to "love your enemies" as a way of silencing their legitimate outrage. Forgiveness is not acquiescence. And enemy-love is not the same as failing to set boundaries, pursue justice, or protect the vulnerable.

What Jesus models in the same Gospel is a person who **loves enemies and confronts them in the same breath** — who calls the Pharisees "brood of vipers" and then weeps over Jerusalem. The tension is real and unresolvable in a neat formula. Perhaps that's the point.

**Enemy-love is not sentimental. It is not merely strategic. It is a moral and spiritual discipline that demands the most rigorous form of human courage: the refusal to let hatred have the final word about who you are.**
