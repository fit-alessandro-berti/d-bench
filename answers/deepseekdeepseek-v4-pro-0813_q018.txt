Paul places bitterness and forgiveness in direct contrast because they are two opposite ways of handling hurt, offense, and debt within the body of Christ. Bitterness keeps a wrong alive; forgiveness releases it. The whole passage is about replacing the old-community vices with new-community graces.

### 1. Bitterness here is more than a passing feeling

The Greek word translated “bitterness” is *pikria*. It literally refers to bitter gall or poison, and metaphorically to bitter resentment. In Ephesians 4:31 it is not just “sadness” or “hurt feelings.” It is a settled, resentful disposition of the heart.

Notice the list:

> “all bitterness, and wrath, and anger, and clamour, and evil speaking … with all malice.”

Paul moves from the hidden inward attitude to outward conflict:

- **Bitterness** — inward, lingering resentment  
- **Wrath** — boiling, passionate anger  
- **Anger** — settled anger  
- **Clamour** — loud quarreling  
- **Evil speaking** — slander, abusive speech  
- **Malice** — ill will, wanting harm  

So bitterness is the root; the others are the fruit. That is why Paul says it must be “put away” (Greek *arthētō*), like taking off an old garment. The command treats bitterness not merely as something you feel, but as something you must decisively renounce and remove.

### 2. Bitterness is also contagious

Ephesians 4 is intensely communal. Paul has already said:

> “We are members one of another.” (Ephesians 4:25)

And:

> “Grieve not the holy Spirit of God.” (Ephesians 4:30)

Bitterness may begin privately, but it does not stay private. It spreads. Hebrews 12:15 warns:

> “lest any root of bitterness springing up trouble you, and thereby many be defiled.”

That is exactly how bitterness works in a family or a church. One bitter person can poison relationships, color how others see people, and create factions. So the text treats bitterness as a contagious spiritual and relational poison, not just a private emotional problem.

### 3. Forgiveness is the opposite of bitterness

The Greek for “forgiving” in Ephesians 4:32 is *charizomai*, which comes from *charis*, “grace.” It means to deal graciously with someone, to release a debt.

That is why the two are placed directly against each other:

- **Bitterness says:** “I will keep this debt on the ledger. You owe me.”  
- **Forgiveness says:** “I release the debt. I will not keep holding this against you.”

Paul grounds this in the gospel:

> “even as God for Christ’s sake hath forgiven you.”

Or more literally, “as God in Christ forgave you.” God’s forgiveness is not a vague sentiment. It is a costly act accomplished in Christ. Therefore Christian forgiveness is not pretending the wrong didn’t matter. It is releasing the wrong because God has already dealt with our own far greater debt in Christ.

### Conclusion

So in Ephesians 4:31–32, bitterness is treated as:

- **A feeling** — it includes real emotional resentment and pain.  
- **A habit** — it becomes a settled pattern of rehearsing wrongs.  
- **A chosen posture** — because Paul commands it to be “put away.”  
- **Something contagious** — because it defiles relationships and communities.

But ultimately, bitterness is the opposite of grace. It holds a debt and spreads poison. Forgiveness releases the debt and spreads the same grace God has given us in Christ. That is why Paul sets them side by side: one belongs to the old self; the other belongs to the new self in Christ.
