Luke 6:37 – "Judge not, and ye shall not be judged: condemn not, and ye shall not be condemned: forgive, and ye shall be forgiven."

1. The verse cannot mean that moral judgments do not matter

The simplest way to see this is to notice that the verse ends with a command to forgive. Forgiveness is impossible without moral judgment. You cannot forgive what you have not first recognized as a wrong; you can only overlook it, excuse it, or fail to notice it. The person who says "it doesn't matter" is not forgiving anyone. Forgiveness says the opposite: "It did matter, it was wrong, and I release you from it anyway." So the third clause of the verse presupposes exactly the moral perception that a careless reading of the first clause seems to abolish. Jesus is not telling us to stop seeing wrong; he is telling us what to do once we have seen it.

The immediate context confirms this. The saying sits in the Sermon on the Plain, right after "Be ye therefore merciful, as your Father also is merciful" (6:36), and it is followed at once by the image of the mote and the beam (6:41–42). That image is often quoted as if it forbade all correction, but read it to the end: "first cast out the beam out of thine own eye, and then shalt thou see clearly to pull out the mote that is in thy brother's eye." The mote is still to be removed. The brother's fault is real and is still to be dealt with. What Jesus regulates is the order and the spirit of the operation: self first, then the other; clear sight, not blurred sight. A few verses later he says a tree is known by its fruit and "an evil man out of the evil treasure of his heart bringeth forth that which is evil" (6:43–45) – a statement that requires moral discrimination to be made at all. In the same Gospel he asks, "Why even of yourselves judge ye not what is right?" (12:57), tells his disciples "If thy brother trespass against thee, rebuke him; and if he repent, forgive him" (17:3), praises Simon with "Thou hast rightly judged" (7:43), and pronounces woes on the rich, the full, and the laughing (6:24–26) in the same sermon. Elsewhere he says "judge righteous judgment" (John 7:24). Whatever "judge not" means, it does not mean that Jesus wanted people incapable of telling right from wrong.

There is also a simple logical point. "Moral judgments never matter" is itself a moral judgment, and Luke 6:37 is itself a moral judgment against a certain kind of judging. A reading that turns the verse into relativism makes the verse self-refuting. Indifference is not what is being commended.

2. What the verse actually forbids

The Greek verb translated "judge" (krinō) and the one translated "condemn" (katadikazō) are placed in parallel, and the whole triplet – judge, condemn, forgive – belongs to the courtroom. The picture is not of a person forming an opinion but of a person taking the bench: hearing the case, pronouncing sentence, closing the file. The natural reading is that "judge" here carries its forensic weight: to pass sentence on a person, to settle their worth and their fate. Paul uses the same word in the same sense when he writes, "judge nothing before the time, until the Lord come" (1 Cor. 4:5) – not "have no opinions," but "do not issue the final verdict, which is not yours to issue."

Three reasons lie behind the prohibition, and each of them explains why discernment survives while condemnation does not.

First, competence. To sentence a person justly you would need to know the whole story: what they were given, what they were up against, what they meant, what they will yet become. "Man looketh on the outward appearance, but the LORD looketh on the heart" (1 Sam. 16:7). We can often see an act clearly; we almost never see a person whole. Discernment stays within what we can actually see. Condemnation claims to see what we cannot.

Second, jurisdiction. The verdict on a human life belongs to God. When I condemn, I do not merely make a mistake; I occupy a seat that is not mine. The passive verbs – "ye shall not be judged," "ye shall not be condemned" – are what grammarians call divine passives: it is God who does the judging that the sentence declines to name. The one who insists on sitting in the judge's chair should not be surprised to find that the chair has a defendant's dock attached to it.

Third, reciprocity. "With the same measure that ye mete withal it shall be measured to you again" (6:38). Every standard I apply to another I have thereby endorsed for myself (Rom. 2:1). To condemn is to set the terms on which I in turn will be assessed. The merciful obtain mercy (Matt. 5:7); the servant who would not forgive a hundred pence found his ten thousand talents reinstated (Matt. 18:23–35). This is not a bargain so much as a description of how the moral world is built: the posture I adopt toward others becomes the posture in which I stand.

3. Discernment and the appetite to condemn

The question rightly speaks of an appetite. Condemnation is not merely an erroneous conclusion; it is something we want, and the wanting is what makes it dangerous. It is worth asking what the appetite feeds on.

It feeds on self-justification by comparison. The Pharisee in Luke 18 prays, "God, I thank thee, that I am not as other men are," and every item in his indictment of the publican is a plank in his own platform. Condemning others is the cheapest available way to feel righteous: it costs nothing, requires no change in me, and delivers the sensation of moral seriousness without any of its labor. It also feeds on displacement. The fault I find intolerable in others is often one I have not faced in myself, and the noise of denunciation drowns out the quieter accusation from within. It feeds on power: a verdict is a way of placing oneself above another. And it feeds on the pleasure of finality – the relief of no longer having to think about a person, of having filed them under a single word.

Discernment is a different activity with a different aim. The contrast can be drawn along several lines.

Aim. Discernment seeks to see truly, in order to act well – to help, to protect, to correct, to avoid being deceived, to know what to forgive. Condemnation seeks a verdict, and behind the verdict, a sentence. The first wants the good of the person seen; the second wants the person's badness confirmed.

Object. Discernment judges acts, words, patterns, fruits – things that are actually visible. Condemnation judges the person: their heart, their worth, their standing before God. "This was wrong" is discernment. "You are worthless" or "you are beyond hope" is condemnation.

Direction. Discernment begins at home: the beam before the mote. It is self-implicating; whatever I see in you I know I must look for in myself. Condemnation exempts the judge. Its whole satisfaction depends on the judge being outside the circle of the accused.

Temper. Discernment is reluctant, slow, provisional, and grieved. It would be glad to be wrong. Condemnation is eager, quick, final, and – if we are honest – pleased. Anyone who has felt the small warm glow of hearing bad news about someone they dislike knows the appetite from the inside.

Time. Discernment leaves the story open. It expects that a person may repent, and keeps the door unlocked for the return; "if he repent, forgive him" (Luke 17:3). Condemnation closes the case. It treats a person's worst moment as their definition and forecloses the future. This is the deepest reason the Gospel forbids it: the whole logic of the Gospel is that judgment is not the last word about anyone, and the one who condemns is trying to make it so.

Cost. Discernment is expensive. To rebuke a brother to his face (17:3) risks the relationship and demands that I be the sort of person who can be heard. Condemnation is cheap: it is usually done at a distance, about people rather than to them, and often to an audience whose approval is the real point.

Effect on sight. The beam in the eye is not only a moral fault but an optical one: the man with the beam cannot "see clearly." The condemning temper actually corrupts perception – it magnifies the other's fault, minimizes context, discounts every mitigating fact, and reads motive with confidence it has no right to. Humility, by contrast, clears the eye. This is the paradox at the heart of the passage: the person most eager to judge is the person least equipped to judge well, and the person who has first faced his own beam is the only one who can help with the mote. The prohibition on condemnation is not opposed to good moral judgment; it is a precondition for it.

4. Why indifference is the greater failure

It should now be clear that "judge not" and "nothing matters" are not neighbors on a spectrum but opposites. Moral indifference makes love impossible, because to love someone is to want their good, and wanting their good requires knowing good from harm. It makes forgiveness impossible, because there is nothing to forgive. It makes protection of the weak impossible, because no one can be identified as a wrongdoer. It makes repentance impossible, because there is nothing to repent of. A community that could not judge in this sense could not do any of the things the Sermon on the Plain commands it to do – love enemies (which requires recognizing that they are enemies and are doing wrong), do good to those who hate you, forgive, restore.

What Jesus asks for is harder than either indifference or condemnation. It is to see wrong clearly and refuse to hate the one who did it; to name a fault and keep the door open; to remove a mote with a hand that has first been to work on its own eye; to leave the verdict to God while doing everything within human reach to bring the other person back. The condemner and the indifferent person have this in common: neither has to do anything about the wrong they see. The one dismisses the sinner; the other dismisses the sin. Luke 6:37 dismisses neither. It holds the wrong and the wrongdoer together, and answers both with mercy – which is only possible for someone who has judged rightly and chosen not to condemn.
