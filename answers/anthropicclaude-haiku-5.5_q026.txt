The passage is a diagnostic claim with a moral charge. Jesus is answering the Pharisees, who had just attributed his exorcisms to Beelzebul (12:24), and he argues that their speech exposes what they are. The verse supports all three readings you name, but they operate at different levels, and the passage is clearest when they are held together.

**1. Words as revelation**

The main claim is epistemic: "out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaketh." Speech discloses an interior reality that is otherwise hidden. The metaphor of the "treasure" (*thesauros*) implies a store that is not visible until something is taken out of it. Jesus's accusation against the Pharisees works this way. Their words about Jesus show what they have been storing. Verse 33 makes the same point with the tree and its fruit: you know the tree by its fruit.

**2. Words as symptoms**

"Abundance" (*perisseuma*) suggests overflow. What spills out is what has accumulated past the point of containment. On this reading, speech is involuntary in the sense that it leaks from a condition that already exists. A person may guard their words, but under pressure, in anger or surprise, the stored content comes out. This is why the verse fits ordinary experience so well: we often say that someone "let something slip," and the slip is usually the truest thing they said.

**3. Words as choices**

The passage does not stop at revelation. Verses 36–37 insist that "every idle word that men shall speak, they shall give account thereof in the day of judgment," and that by one's words one will be "justified" or "condemned." If speech were merely symptomatic, accountability for it would make little sense. So words are also acts for which a person is answerable.

The treasure image also points to choice at a deeper level. A treasure is accumulated over time. The heart's store is built through repeated attention, desire, speech, and action. Speech therefore feeds back into the heart: the words one chooses to say rehearse and deepen the dispositions they express. The symptom is partly a product of earlier choices, and each new choice adds to the store.

**How the three fit together**

- Speech reveals the stored condition of the heart.
- That condition is the cumulative result of past choices, including past speech.
- Present speech is itself a choice that either reinforces or begins to reshape the store.

So the verse is not deterministic. The rhetorical question "how can ye, being evil, speak good things?" sounds like a claim of incapacity, but the surrounding teaching about accountability and the parallel in Luke 6:45 ("a good man out of the good treasure...") leave room for change. The Pharisees cannot produce good speech *from their present treasure*, but the treasure is not fixed.

**A caveat**

Speech can also conceal. Jesus elsewhere condemns those who "make clean the outside of the cup" (23:25). The verse assumes that, in the long run and under pressure, the mouth tends to betray the heart. It does not claim that every word transparently reflects inner truth, and a person can cultivate speech that masks what they have stored. Still, the mask itself is a choice that the heart must sustain, which is part of why the passage treats speech as a window into character rather than a neutral surface.
