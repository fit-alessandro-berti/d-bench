In Ephesians 4:31–32, bitterness and forgiveness are set in direct opposition because, in the logic of the Apostle’s House (Book IV), they represent the two possible responses to the exact same human condition: the reality of imperfection, fault, and injury. We constantly err, wound, and disappoint one another. The verse asks what our posture will be in the wake of that injury. 

Bitterness is what happens when forgiveness is withheld; forgiveness is what happens when bitterness is put away. They are perfect inverses.

But to answer the second part of your question: the text treats bitterness not merely as a feeling, but as a **habit, a chosen posture, and something highly contagious**. The specific words of the verse reveal each of these dimensions.

### 1. More than a Feeling: Bitterness as an Escalating Posture
If bitterness were merely an emotion, it would pass. But Ephesians places it in a list of progressively externalized behaviors: *“bitterness, and wrath, and anger, and clamour, and evil speaking... with all malice.”* 

Notice the trajectory of this list. It moves from the internal to the external, and from the spontaneous to the deliberate.
*   **Anger** is the instant, internal reaction to a slight.
*   **Wrath** is the desire to strike back.
*   **Bitterness** is the residue—the sustained, sour disposition that remains after the moment of wrath has passed.
*   **Clamour** (outcry/noise) and **evil speaking** (slander/mockery) are the exterior projections of that residue.

By the time bitterness manifests as "evil speaking," it has ceased to be a mere feeling. It has become a *chosen posture*—a decision to narrate the world through the lens of grievance. It is a habit of holding a grudge, not just in one’s heart, but in one’s tongue and conduct. 

The text commands that this posture be *“put away from you.”* You cannot “put away” a flash of emotion like anger, but you *can* put away a habit. You can lay down a posture. 

### 2. Something Contagious: The Social Spread of Bitterness
The verse strongly implies that bitterness is contagious, spreading like a moral plague through a community. This is implicit in the words *“clamour”* and *“evil speaking.”* 

When bitterness is vocalized—when we speak evil of one another—we inject the poison into the communal bloodstream. If I speak evil of my neighbor, I make it harder for the next person to be kind to them. Bitterness replicates. It demands assent from the listener, breeding a shared resentment. This is why the Ephesian text treats it almost like a pathogen: it must be entirely flushed out, *“put away from you, with all malice,”* so the environment can be purified.

The cure for this contagion is deeply social and mutual: *“be ye kind one to another... forgiving one another.”* The repetition of “one another” emphasizes that the cure requires a synchronized, communal shedding of the disease. 

### 3. The Cosmic Mirror: Why the Opposition Matters
The brilliance of Ephesians 4:32 lies in its theological modeling. The text commands Christians to be tenderhearted and forgiving, *“even as God for Christ’s sake hath forgiven you.”*

In Luther’s theological vision (which this verse distills), God grants grace (gifts, creation, mercy) to humanity *despite* their endless vices and errors. God is the model of pure forgiveness. God does not hold humanity’s endless faults against them; God is kind.

The verse places bitterness and forgiveness against each other to remind the Christian of their obligation to mirror the cosmic order: **If God can forgive you your immense faults for Christ’s sake, you can forgive your brother his small ones.** 

Bitterness, therefore, is not just a personal failing; it is a theological rebellion. It is the stubborn refusal to participate in the divine economy of mercy. To hold onto bitterness is to claim you are less forgivable than God is, or that your neighbor is less forgivable than you are.
