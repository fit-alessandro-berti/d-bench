Luke 6:37 is not saying that moral judgment is irrelevant. It is saying that **merciless, self-righteous, condemnatory judgment is spiritually dangerous**. The verse assumes that real good and evil exist; otherwise “forgive” would mean nothing. Forgiveness only makes sense where there is something real to forgive.

So the difference is not between “moral judgment” and “no moral judgment.” The difference is between **discernment** and **the appetite to condemn**.

---

## 1. What Jesus is forbidding

The verse has a parallel structure:

> Judge not, and you will not be judged;  
> condemn not, and you will not be condemned;  
> forgive, and you will be forgiven.

The movement is important. Jesus does not end with neutrality. He ends with forgiveness. That means the passage is not teaching moral indifference; it is teaching mercy.

In the broader context of Luke 6, Jesus says:

> “Be merciful, even as your Father is merciful.”  
> — Luke 6:36

So “judge not” is not a command never to recognize wrongdoing. It is a warning against becoming the kind of person who eagerly places others under judgment while forgetting their own need for mercy.

A few verses later, Jesus speaks about the speck and the log:

> “You hypocrite, first take the log out of your own eye, and then you will see clearly to take out the speck that is in your brother’s eye.”  
> — Luke 6:42

That is very revealing. Jesus does not say, “Never notice the speck.” He says, “First deal with yourself, then you will see clearly enough to help your brother.”

So the text does not abolish moral discernment. It purifies it.

---

## 2. Moral judgments still matter

If moral judgments never mattered, then nothing in Luke 6:37 would make sense.

If there is no real wrong, then:

- there is nothing to forgive;
- there is no need for mercy;
- there is no difference between justice and injustice;
- there is no need for repentance;
- there is no meaning to “condemn not,” because there is no real evil to avoid.

Jesus is not teaching relativism. He is not saying, “Nothing is truly wrong.” He is saying, “Do not take pleasure in condemning.”

In fact, the passage assumes that people do wrong. That is why forgiveness is commanded. Forgiveness does not say, “What you did did not matter.” Forgiveness says, “What you did was real, but I will not hold it over you endlessly.”

So Luke 6:37 is not opposed to moral judgment as such. It is opposed to a certain kind of moral judgment: harsh, self-exalting, unforgiving, final.

---

## 3. Discernment is not the same as condemnation

There is a major difference between **seeing clearly** and **casting down**.

Discernment says:

- “This is true.”
- “This is harmful.”
- “This is sinful.”
- “This must be named.”
- “This requires repentance, boundaries, justice, or healing.”

Condemnation says:

- “This person is beyond hope.”
- “They deserve to be crushed.”
- “I am superior to them.”
- “They must pay.”
- “I take pleasure in their exposure.”

Discernment recognizes evil. Condemnation feasts on it.

A doctor who diagnoses a disease is not “judging” the patient in a condemning sense. The diagnosis is an act of truth that makes healing possible. But a person who delights in the patient’s illness, mocks the patient, and uses the diagnosis to destroy them has moved from discernment into cruelty.

That is the difference Jesus addresses.

---

## 4. Discernment names reality; condemnation disposes of persons

Discernment can be painful, but it is aimed at restoration. It may say, “What you did was wrong,” but it does not say, “Therefore you are no longer a person worthy of love, repentance, or future.”

Condemnation, however, tends to flatten people into their worst acts. It refuses to distinguish between the person and the evil. It wants to settle the verdict now, permanently, and with contempt.

This is why Scripture can say both:

- “Test everything; hold fast what is good” (1 Thessalonians 5:21),
- and “Judge not, that you be not judged” (Matthew 7:1).

These are not contradictions. One commands moral perception; the other forbids merciless final judgment.

A mature moral life requires both truth and mercy. Truth without mercy becomes cruelty. Mercy without truth becomes sentimentality or complicity.

---

## 5. The appetite to condemn

The phrase “appetite to condemn” is helpful because it points to something deeper than the act of judgment. It names a hunger.

People can become addicted to moral outrage. They can enjoy feeling superior. They can become eager to detect fault, expose failure, assign blame, and pronounce sentence. This appetite often disguises itself as zeal for righteousness, but its fruit is contempt, anger, impatience, and pride.

Signs of an appetite to condemn include:

1. **Haste**  
   The person rushes to verdict before knowing the facts.

2. **Certainty without humility**  
   The person is sure they see everything clearly, including motives they cannot actually know.

3. **Contempt**  
   The judgment is not sorrowful but scornful.

4. **Pleasure in exposure**  
   The person enjoys revealing someone’s failure more than seeing them restored.

5. **Refusal to consider one’s own sin**  
   The person sees the speck clearly but ignores the log.

6. **Desire for punishment rather than healing**  
   Justice may be desired, but the deeper desire is to make the other suffer.

7. **Finality**  
   The person acts as though no repentance, change, or mercy is possible.

That appetite is spiritually corrosive because it places the condemner in the position of God. It assumes ultimate authority over another person’s soul.

---

## 6. Discernment is sober, humble, and reluctant

True discernment is often quieter and sadder than condemnation.

It says:

- “I need to be careful.”
- “I may be wrong.”
- “I also have sins I do not see.”
- “This behavior is destructive.”
- “Boundaries may be necessary.”
- “Justice may be required.”
- “But this person remains an image-bearer of God.”
- “My goal is not to destroy them.”

Discernment can be firm. It can call evil by its proper name. It can protect the vulnerable. It can confront sin. It can uphold standards. But it does so with humility, because the discerning person knows they too stand in need of forgiveness.

This is why Luke 6:37 connects judgment with forgiveness. The person who discerns rightly also remembers: “I too need mercy.”

---

## 7. The passage forbids a godlike posture, not moral responsibility

Human beings are often called to make moral judgments. Parents must discern. Courts must judge. Churches must discipline. Friends must confront. Societies must distinguish justice from injustice.

The problem is not that people judge. The problem is that people often judge wrongly, proudly, and without mercy.

James says:

> “There is only one lawgiver and judge, he who is able to save and to destroy. But who are you to judge your neighbor?”  
> — James 4:12

That does not mean we never evaluate. It means we must not act as though we possess final authority over our neighbor’s soul.

We may judge actions, motives as far as they are evident, patterns, fruits, and consequences. But we must not pretend to render God’s final verdict on a person.

There is a difference between saying:

- “This action is wrong,”  
and saying:  
- “This person is irredeemably contemptible.”

The first may be discernment. The second is close to condemnation.

---

## 8. Forgiveness presupposes moral seriousness

The command “forgive” is perhaps the clearest proof that Luke 6:37 does not abolish moral judgment.

Forgiveness is not the same as saying, “It did not matter.” Forgiveness is not denial. It is not excuse-making. It is not pretending harm did not happen.

Real forgiveness says:

> “This was truly wrong. It caused real harm. I am not calling it good. But I will not keep the debt open forever.”

Forgiveness is costly precisely because it takes moral reality seriously. If nothing truly mattered, forgiveness would be meaningless.

So Jesus is not calling us into moral blindness. He is calling us into moral maturity: clear enough to see sin, humble enough to remember our own, and merciful enough to leave room for repentance.

---

## 9. A useful distinction: judge the fruit, not the final soul

Jesus elsewhere says:

> “You will know them by their fruits.”  
> — Matthew 7:16

That requires discernment. We are not forbidden to recognize false teaching, destructive behavior, hypocrisy, cruelty, or injustice. In fact, love sometimes requires us to name these things plainly.

But recognizing bad fruit is not the same as pronouncing final destruction on the tree.

Discernment asks:

- What is true?
- What is happening?
- What is harmful?
- What is needed for healing, justice, or correction?

Condemnation asks:

- How can I prove myself right?
- How can I make them pay?
- How can I expose them?
- How can I establish my superiority?

The first posture serves truth and mercy. The second serves pride.

---

## 10. The deeper point: mercy triumphs over judgment

Luke 6:37 is part of Jesus’ larger call to imitate God’s mercy. The verse before it says:

> “Be merciful, even as your Father is merciful.”  
> — Luke 6:36

The Father’s mercy does not mean He is indifferent to evil. God is not morally blind. He is holy. But His response to fallen humanity includes patience, forgiveness, and the offer of redemption.

Jesus’ teaching is not: “Never recognize evil.”  
It is: “Do not become a person whose dominant posture is condemnation.”

The Christian life is not called to be a constant tribunal. It is called to be truthful under the rule of mercy.

As James puts it:

> “Mercy triumphs over judgment.”  
> — James 2:13

That does not cancel judgment. It means that when mercy and judgment meet, mercy should have the final word in our disposition.

---

## In short

Luke 6:37 is not moral relativism. It is not saying that moral judgments never matter. It is saying that we must not become harsh, self-righteous, unforgiving judges of others.

The difference is this:

- **Discernment sees evil clearly in order to truthfully address it.**
- **Condemnation seizes upon evil in order to destroy, shame, or exalt oneself.**

Discernment says: “This is wrong.”  
The appetite to condemn says: “This person deserves to be crushed.”

Discernment can lead to correction, justice, boundaries, and forgiveness.  
Condemnation leads to contempt, hardness, and spiritual blindness.

So the verse does not abolish moral judgment. It transforms it. It calls us to see truth without becoming cruel, to name sin without delighting in damnation, and to remember that we too live by mercy.
