The passage requires **humble, gentle, redemptive correction** — not superior condemnation.

In Galatians 6:1, Paul is speaking within the family of believers: “Brethren.” That word already sets the tone. The person being corrected is not an enemy to be crushed, but a fellow member to be helped. The one correcting is called “spiritual,” not because he is morally superior, but because he is expected to act according to the Spirit — with the fruit of the Spirit: love, patience, kindness, gentleness, self-control.

The moral posture required is therefore that of a **physician, not an executioner**; a **fellow pilgrim, not a judge**.

### 1. Restoration: the goal is healing

The word translated “restore” carries the idea of **mending**, **setting right**, or **putting something back into place**. It was sometimes used of setting a dislocated limb. That is very important.

Correction is not meant to shame, dominate, or vent righteous anger. Its aim is recovery. The fallen person is to be helped back onto the path. If correction does not aim at restoration, it has already lost its Christian character.

So the first thing kept before us is this: **correction is for the good of the fallen person, not for the exaltation of the corrector.**

### 2. Meekness: the manner must be gentle

The correction must be done “in the spirit of meekness.” Meekness here does not mean weakness. It means **strength under restraint**. It is the refusal to be harsh, scornful, impatient, or self-important.

A person may be genuinely guilty. The fault may be real. But even truthful correction can become sinful if it is delivered with contempt, irritation, or pride. Meekness ensures that truth is spoken without cruelty.

A harsh spirit says, “How could you fall into this?”  
A meek spirit says, “This is terrible, but you are not beyond grace; come, let us seek healing.”

Meekness remembers that the purpose is not to prove how serious one is about sin, but to help a brother or sister be made whole.

### 3. Self-suspicion: the safeguard against pride

Then comes the warning: “considering thyself, lest thou also be tempted.”

This is essential. The person who corrects must not imagine that he stands in some secure region of moral superiority. He must watch himself. He must remember his own weakness. The same temptation, perhaps in another form, could overthrow him too.

This “self-suspicion” is not pathological self-hatred. It is sober realism. It says:

> “I am not above this person. I am not made of better material. If I have not fallen in this way, it is by mercy, not because I am naturally superior.”

Without this self-awareness, correction easily becomes proud, cold, and cruel. The moment a person corrects another while feeling spiritually untouchable, he has already entered danger.

### Why these three are kept together

They are kept together because each one corrects a dangerous tendency in the others.

- **Restoration without meekness** can become harsh surgery — technically right, but brutal.
- **Meekness without restoration** can become weak tolerance — gentle in tone, but unwilling to confront real sin.
- **Restoration and meekness without self-suspicion** can become spiritual arrogance — helpful in aim and gentle in manner, yet secretly proud.

Together they form a complete Christian posture:

- **Restoration** gives the goal: the fallen must be healed.
- **Meekness** gives the manner: the healing must be gentle.
- **Self-suspicion** gives the inward guard: the healer must remember his own vulnerability.

In short, Paul requires correction that is **serious about sin, tender toward the sinner, and humble about oneself**.

The one correcting must say, in effect:  
“This fault is real and must be addressed; but I come to you not as your superior, not with contempt, and not with delight in your failure, but as a fellow sinner upheld by grace, desiring your restoration before God.”
