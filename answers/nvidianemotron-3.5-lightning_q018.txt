### Why Bitterness and Forgiveness Stand in Direct Contrast

The passage follows a classic New Testament **"put off / put on"** pattern (cf. Eph 4:22–24). Verse 31 issues negative imperatives; verse 32 issues positive counterparts, grounded in the believer's identity in Christ. The contrast isn't merely semantic—it's covenantal and relational. 

- **Community integrity:** Ephesians is written to a church struggling with unity (Eph 4:1–6). Bitterness, wrath, and slander corrode the "one another" fabric of the body. Forgiveness, kindness, and tenderheartedness rebuild it. The direct opposition forces the reader to see that these aren't just personal preferences but marks of whether Christ's new humanity is truly governing a community.
- **Grace-motivated ethics:** The motivation clause—"even as God for Christ's sake hath forgiven you"—anchors the command in what God has already done. The contrast isn't "feeling bad vs. feeling good"; it's "living apart from Christ's forgiveness vs. living in it."

### What Bitterness Is in This Text

The Greek word **πικρία (pikria)** means "bitterness, harshness, or root-of-resentment." In the New Testament it often carries a metaphorical, almost horticultural weight (cf. Heb 12:15: "a root of bitterness springing up"). Here it is listed among relational toxins: wrath, anger, clamor, slander, and malice. 

The text treats bitterness as **all of the following, but in this order of priority**:

1. **A settled habit/root** — The verb "be put away" implies it's not a passing mood but a entrenched posture that must be actively uprooted.
2. **A chosen/oriented posture** — It is something the believer is commanded to "let go of," indicating volition. It's not excused as "just how I am."
3. **A feeling that spreads** — Because it's listed in a **"one another"** context, the text assumes bitterness is contagious within a community. A bitter heart infects speech, alliances, and the broader witness of the church.
4. **Less than a raw emotion** — While it may begin as hurt or anger, the biblical category of bitterness is the *refusal to release* a grievance, allowing it to ferment into a root that defiles many.

### What Forgiveness Looks Like Here

**ἀποφεύγοντας / χαριζόμενος (forgiving)** is likewise multidimensional:

- **A grace-enabled choice** — It is commanded, not merely prompted by feeling. The ground of the command is the already-received forgiveness of God in Christ.
- **Relational restoration** — "Tenderhearted" (πραΰς, praus) and "kind" (χρηστός, chrestos) describe the *manner* of the forgiveness—not a cheap pardon, but a Christlike, gentle restoration of the broken other.
- **Contagious by design** — The "one another" language makes forgiveness the spreading agent. Just as bitterness defiles, forgiveness refines the body.

### Why the Text Pairs Them So Sharply

The opposition highlights that **Christian identity is visibly enacted in how we handle relational friction.** If bitterness is the default "old self" operating in community, forgiveness is the default "new self" operating in it. The verse doesn't ask, "Do you feel bitter?" It asks, "Will you put it away and replace it with the forgiveness you've been given?"

In short: the text treats bitterness as a **contagious, rooted habit** that must be consciously uprooted, and forgiveness as a **grace-motivated, chosen posture** that restores and spreads the life of Christ through the church. The direct contrast is a spiritual diagnostic: whichever is ruling your community reveals whether you're walking in the old Adam or the new Christ.
