Amos doesn't ask for balance because he isn't describing worship that is good but incomplete. He is describing worship that has become offensive. The verbs show it: "I hate," "I despise," "I will not smell," "I will not accept," "I will not hear." These are the terms of rejection, not of adjustment. God doesn't say "do these things, but also do justice." He says the worship itself is unacceptable as it stands.

**Why the rejection is so sharp**

1. **Context defines the worship.** Throughout Amos, Israel is prosperous and religiously active while the poor are sold "for a pair of shoes" (2:6), the needy are trampled (5:11), and courts are corrupted (5:12). In that setting the feasts, sacrifices, and songs aren't neutral acts that happen to lack a companion virtue. They are performed by people actively exploiting others, and they function as cover. The worship is tainted by the life it accompanies.

2. **It misunderstands who God is.** Offerings treat God as someone who can be managed or paid off. If enough sacrifice is brought, the thinking goes, God will tolerate or bless whatever else is going on. Amos shows that God is not a patron whose favor can be bought but a judge who is concerned with how people treat each other. Worship that assumes otherwise is, at bottom, a form of idolatry even when it uses the right names and rituals.

3. **Balance would concede the fraud.** If the answer were "a little less ritual, a little more justice," the two would be interchangeable goods on a scale. Amos's point is that justice is not an optional supplement. It is what the worship was supposed to express. Without it, worship isn't diminished; it is falsified.

**The kind of fraud exposed**

- **Self-deception.** The worshipers seem sincere in their religiosity. They look forward to the "day of the LORD" (5:18), confident it means vindication. The fraud is partly that they have persuaded themselves that religious devotion settles their standing with God.
- **Moral laundering.** Piety is used to offset or legitimize injustice, producing a sense of being right with God while wronging people made in his image.
- **Aesthetic substitution.** "The noise of thy songs" and "the melody of thy viols" suggest that worship has become performance and pleasure. The people enjoy the experience of devotion without submitting to the one being worshiped. Notice God calls the music "noise," as if the beauty has become merely sound.
- **Covenant betrayal.** Sacrifice and feast belonged to a covenant that also demanded justice for the widow, orphan, and poor. Keeping the ceremonial half while breaking the ethical half is not partial obedience but a repudiation of the covenant's meaning.

**The positive demand**

Verse 24 offers an image, not a rule: justice should "run down as waters" and righteousness be "a mighty stream." The Hebrew suggests a perennial wadi, not a seasonal trickle. Justice is to be constant, abundant, and unstoppable, the normal flow of life and not an occasional gesture. This contrasts with ritual, which is scheduled and contained.

**An important qualification**

Amos is not abolishing worship or teaching that ritual is inherently worthless. The same tradition commanded these feasts and sacrifices. The critique is aimed at worship severed from righteousness. The implied claim is that worship and justice belong together, and that when they are separated, God prefers the justice and refuses the worship, because the worship without it is a lie told in his presence.
