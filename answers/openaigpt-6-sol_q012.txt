It is both, but its main point is **the danger of misplaced confidence**. In the surrounding verses, Jeremiah contrasts trusting in human strength with trusting in the Lord. Verse 9 explains why even our own motives cannot be the final authority: the heart can deceive *itself*. Verse 10 answers “who can know it?”—the Lord searches the heart and judges not only hidden intentions but “ways” and the “fruit” of actions.

That is a sober view of human nature, not a claim that every apparent good motive is secretly evil or that self-knowledge is impossible. It calls for humility about what we think we know.

So someone can speak of motives **honestly but provisionally**: “I want to help, though I may also want approval.” They can compare their professed intentions with their actions and their effects, welcome correction, and avoid claiming certainty about another person’s heart. The passage gives neither permission for cynical suspicion nor reason to stop examining ourselves; it says that our self-examination needs a judge beyond ourselves.
