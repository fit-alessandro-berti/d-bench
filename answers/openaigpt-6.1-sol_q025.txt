The danger is **not visibility itself, but the craving to be seen**. The decisive phrase is “**to be seen of them**”: Jesus warns against doing good *for the purpose of attracting recognition*.

That distinction matters because earlier he says, “Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father” (Matthew 5:16). Goodness can be public. The question is **where the attention is meant to lead**—toward God and the good being done, or toward the goodness of the person doing it.

Public goodness becomes morally dangerous when:

- **The recipient becomes a prop.** Their need serves the giver’s reputation rather than being the central concern.
- **Praise becomes the real reward.** In the following verse, Jesus says such people “have their reward”: they receive the applause they sought.
- **Appearance displaces integrity.** Being regarded as generous can become more important than actually loving one’s neighbor.

Doing good secretly, then, is not simply a rule against witnesses. It is a discipline that loosens our dependence on approval: *Would I still do this if nobody knew?*

The verse does not require us never to feel pleased when someone notices our kindness. It challenges us to ask whether recognition is an incidental consequence—or the purpose governing the act. **The moral issue is not merely who sees the deed, but whose approval the doer is living for.**
