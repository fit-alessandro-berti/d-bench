The shock of Amos 5:21–24 is intentional. It is not a mild call to “balance” liturgy with charity, as if Israel’s problem were simply too many festivals and too little social concern. The point is far more serious: **Israel’s worship had become a lie.** What God rejects is not worship as such, but worship that had been severed from the obedience, justice, and covenant faithfulness that alone make worship real.

### Why not just ask for “better balance”?

A call for balance would assume that Israel’s sacrifices, songs, and festivals were basically acceptable and merely needed to be supplemented with a little more justice. Amos says the opposite: as these people were then worshiping, God *hated* it.

1. **The ritual was not neutral—it was polluted.**  
   The offerings named in verses 22–23 (“burnt offerings,” “grain offerings,” “peace offerings,” songs, and lyres) were not pagan inventions. They were the very acts God had commanded. But sacrifices in Israel were never mechanical; they belonged to a covenant relationship. When the worshipers were oppressing the poor, taking bribes, and perverting justice in the courts (Amos 5:7, 10–13), their hands were “full of blood.” An offering brought by such hands was not slightly defective—it was abominable.

2. **They were using worship as a substitute for repentance, not merely neglecting mercy.**  
   The people were not accidentally forgetting justice; they were actively violating it and then coming to the sanctuary to reassure themselves that everything was fine. Worship had become a way to manage God—or, worse, to bribe Him—while continuing to exploit others. A thief who donates a portion of stolen money to the temple is not “imbalanced”; he is committing a further offense. Israel was fencing stolen goods through the altar.

3. **Notice the repetitive “your.”**  
   God says: “*your* feast days,” “*your* solemn assemblies,” “*your* burnt offerings,” “*your* meat offerings,” “*your* peace offerings,” “*thy* songs,” “*thy* viols.” These are no longer the LORD’s festivals; they have become Israel’s self-directed religious projects. The worship was about Israel’s national ego and economic security, not about the God who defends the poor and demands righteousness.

### The moral and spiritual fraud exposed

The fraud is both **moral** and **spiritual**.

- **Moral fraud:** Israel’s economic and legal life was built on exploitation. They “turn aside the poor in the gate,” take bribes, and trample the needy (5:11–12). Their wealth—and therefore their expensive sacrifices—was funded by oppression. To offer peace offerings while making war on the poor was to celebrate a “peace” that did not exist. The peace offering (*shelamim*) was meant to signify wholeness and fellowship with God and neighbor, but there was no wholeness to celebrate.

- **Spiritual fraud:** They treated God as if He could be manipulated by religious noise. The sacrifice, the song, and the lyre were treated as automatic appeasements—as though a busy festival calendar could blind God to injustice. This turned worship into a form of false security. They were saying, in effect, “We have the rituals, so God is on our side,” while hating the very justice that reveals God’s character.

In short, the fraud was **counterfeit covenant faithfulness**: using the forms of worship to cover a life of covenant-breaking.

### What God actually wants

Amos 5:24 is not a call to add justice as a third element alongside worship. It is a description of what true covenant life looks like:

> “Let judgment [*mishpat*] run down as waters, and righteousness [*tsedaqah*] as a mighty stream.”

*Mishpat* is justice in the gates—fair courts, honest business, protection for the vulnerable. *Tsedaqah* is right covenant relationship. God wants these not as occasional religious bursts, but as a **constant, ever-flowing river** (the Hebrew pictures a perennial stream, not a seasonal wadi). Their religion was flashy but intermittent; God wants a community whose daily life is saturated with justice.

### Why the rejection is so sharp

The harsh language is medicinal. A gentle request for “balance” would let Israel keep its self-deception: “Keep the festivals, sing the songs, and maybe also be a bit fairer.” But God refuses to let worship be used as a cover for oppression. He will not “smell” their sacrifices or hear their music, because to accept them would be to endorse the lie.

Yet the door is not closed. Just before this passage, Amos says:

> “Seek good, and not evil, that ye may live… Hate the evil, and love the good, and establish judgment in the gate” (5:14–15).

If justice begins to flow like a river, then the songs, offerings, and assemblies can become what they were always meant to be: truthful responses to the God who “turns justice to wormwood” when His people cast righteousness down, but who delights in a people who do justice, love mercy, and walk humbly with Him (Micah 6:8).

So the passage exposes **worship as a fraudulent substitute for obedience**. It rejects the notion that God can be honored with the lips while the poor are crushed by the hands. True worship is not a transaction to buy God off; it is a life in which justice and righteousness roll on like an ever-flowing stream.
