Repentance in Isaiah 55:7 is not only a change of behavior. It is a turning of the whole person—path and mind together—back to God, who answers that return with mercy rather than with a demand that the turn already be complete.

**“Way” and “thoughts” name two sides of the same life.**  
In the verse, the wicked person is told to forsake his *way* (the path he walks: habits, dealings, the pattern of conduct others can see), and the unrighteous person is told to forsake his *thoughts* (plans, schemes, the inner counsel that designs and justifies that path). Hebrew prophetic speech often pairs outer course and inner design this way. A “way” is not a single act; it is the road one keeps choosing. “Thoughts” are not fleeting moods; they are the calculations, desires, and self-justifying stories that keep the road open. Forsaking one without the other leaves the life half-turned: conduct cleaned up while the imagination still plots the old course, or pious intentions that never alter what one actually does.

**What that implies about conduct and imagination.**  
Outward conduct is not optional. The call is to leave the wicked way, not merely to feel sorry about it. At the same time, the text will not let repentance stop at visible reform. The unrighteous *thoughts* must be abandoned because they are where the way is conceived and excused. Imagination here is moral, not decorative: it is the workshop of plans that either serve God or rival him. Verses 8–9 sharpen the point. Human thoughts and ways are not merely imperfect; they stand over against God’s thoughts and ways. Returning, then, means letting one’s path and one’s inner counsel be judged and redirected by a wisdom higher than one’s own.

**What that implies about mercy.**  
The structure of the saying keeps mercy from becoming a reward for a finished self-improvement project. The imperative is “forsake… and return”; the promise is “he will have mercy… he will abundantly pardon.” Mercy meets the one who turns, not the one who has already become righteous by his own reform. The abundance of the pardon (“abundantly”) matches the depth of what must be forsaken: not only deeds, but the thoughts that generated them. Urgency frames the whole appeal (v. 6): the Lord may be sought and is near *now*. The window is gracious, not indefinite—but within it, the offer is not a thin forgiveness of surface faults. It is pardon wide enough for both the road walked and the mind that chose it, provided the person actually returns.

So the pairing is diagnostic and hopeful at once. Partial repentance—behavior without a changed mind, or inward resolve without a changed way—does not yet count as the return the verse describes. And yet the God who is sought does not answer that full turning with scarcity. He answers it with mercy and abundant pardon.
