**The verse does not teach that moral judgments never matter.** It warns against a specific stance: hypocritical, unmerciful, or self-exalting condemnation that treats others as finally beyond hope while ignoring one's own faults. Scripture repeatedly requires discernment, righteous evaluation, and moral distinctions.

### Context and the actual claim
Luke 6:37 sits in the Sermon on the Plain (parallel to Matthew 7:1–5). Immediately afterward Jesus talks about the speck and the log: remove the beam from your own eye *so that* you can see clearly to help your brother. The prohibition is not “never notice right and wrong.” It is “do not play God, do not measure others by a standard you refuse to apply to yourself, and do not withhold the mercy you yourself need.” The same chapter and the wider Gospels show Jesus making sharp moral judgments (against hypocrisy, greed, unbelief, exploitation of the vulnerable). Paul, John, and the Torah likewise command believers to test spirits, expose works of darkness, and judge *inside* the community (1 Corinthians 5–6; 1 John 4:1; John 7:24: “judge with right judgment”).

If “judge not” meant “moral categories are irrelevant,” the rest of the Bible would be incoherent. Commandments, prophets, wisdom literature, and the apostolic letters all presuppose that some actions, motives, and teachings are better or worse, true or false, life-giving or destructive.

### Discernment versus the appetite to condemn
- **Discernment** is the capacity to distinguish: truth from error, wisdom from folly, justice from oppression, repentance from hardness. It is necessary for any coherent ethics, law, parenting, friendship, or self-examination. It can be exercised with humility (“I too am capable of this”), with evidence, and with an aim toward restoration or protection rather than destruction. It remains open to correction and does not claim omniscience about another person’s heart or final destiny.
- **The appetite to condemn** is the desire to place oneself above others, to savor their failure, to issue a final verdict, or to use moral language as a weapon for status or revenge. It often ignores one’s own comparable (or greater) faults, refuses mercy, and treats people as irredeemable. That posture is what the verse targets: “condemn not, and you will not be condemned; forgive, and you will be forgiven.” It is the opposite of the humility required by the same teaching.

One can therefore say “this action is wrong and harmful” (discernment + moral judgment) without saying “this person is trash and I am the righteous one who gets to write them off” (appetite to condemn). The first is compatible with love of neighbor and with leaving ultimate judgment to God; the second is not.

Moral judgments matter because actions have real consequences, because communities need shared standards, and because truth-telling is part of loving people rather than enabling harm. The verse simply forbids turning those judgments into a self-righteous, unforgiving identity. The difference is not whether we evaluate, but *how* we evaluate and what we do with the evaluation.
