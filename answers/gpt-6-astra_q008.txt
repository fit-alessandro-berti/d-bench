The verse treats love of the visible brother or sister as a test because **love for God cannot be separated from the kind of love God brings into a person’s life**. In the surrounding passage, John says that love comes from God, that God has shown it by sending his Son, and that those loved by God ought to love one another (1 John 4:7–11). Loving others is not a competing obligation; it is a concrete expression of loving God.

The contrast between “seen” and “not seen” exposes the difference between a claim and its lived reality. It is easy to profess devotion to the invisible God while keeping that devotion insulated from practical demands. The visible person interrupts that insulation: here is someone whose needs, faults, and claims on us are real. First John makes this especially concrete elsewhere: how can God’s love abide in someone who sees a brother in need and refuses to help (3:17)?

The self-deception, then, is **mistaking religious feeling, language, or identity for love of God while withholding love from actual people**. Someone may sincerely enjoy worship, cherish the idea of God, or think of themselves as devout, yet preserve contempt and indifference toward others. John calls the resulting claim a lie because their conduct contradicts what they profess—not necessarily because every such person is consciously pretending.

“Brother” here refers especially to a fellow believer, making the contradiction particularly sharp: one claims to love God while hating someone who belongs to God’s family.

This does not mean that love requires constant warm feelings, agreement, or the absence of conflict. It means that a settled refusal of another’s good cannot be reconciled with love for the God whose character is love.

In short: **the unseen God must not become an imagined refuge from the demands of loving the person we can see.**
