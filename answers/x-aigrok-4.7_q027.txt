The parable names the verdict before either man speaks. It is told “unto certain which trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and despised others” (Luke 18:9). What condemns the Pharisee is that whole stance, not a mistaken rule about fasting or tithing.

**Not false doctrine.** Nothing in the scene says twice-weekly fasting or tithing is an error. Those practices can be real. The failure is what he does with them: he offers them to God as proof that he already stands in the right, and as the reason he is not “as other men are… or even as this publican.” Virtue has become a credential and a contrast. The prayer is “with himself”—turned inward, a self-assessment addressed upward. He asks for nothing. He confesses nothing. God is thanked for a comparison the Pharisee has already settled.

**Pride, comparison, contempt, and display are one act here, not rival options.** Pride is the self-trust of verse 9. Comparison is the method (“I am not as…”). Contempt is the social form of that trust: the other man in the temple is not a neighbor but evidence for the case. Self-display is how the virtues function in the prayer—not as obedience received, but as a résumé that separates him from extortioners, the unjust, adulterers, and this tax collector. Luke does not invite us to pick one defect and excuse the rest. The exaltation of the self and the despising of the other are the same movement.

**The tax collector is treated as truer because he stands where the Pharisee will not: as someone who needs mercy, not as someone who can supply righteousness.** He stays far off, will not lift his eyes, strikes his breast, and says, “God be merciful to me a sinner.” The prayer is short because the claim is short. He does not bargain, compare, or list repairs. In Luke’s wording he goes home “justified”—accepted in God’s judgment—rather than the other. The gestures matter only as the shape of that plea; they are not a better technique for appearing humble. What is spiritually true is the alignment of the man with his actual position before God: sinner, dependent, without a case.

The closing line of the parable makes the reversal explicit: the one who exalts himself is brought low; the one who humbles himself is lifted up. Justification, in this story, is not the prize for the cleaner record. It is the verdict given to the one who stops trusting his record and asks for mercy.
