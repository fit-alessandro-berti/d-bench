Genesis 4:6-7 is the first biblical statement of human moral accountability. God does not excuse Cain’s anger as an involuntary outburst. He interrogates the affect, *Why art thou wroth? and why is thy countenance fallen?*, and then gives a conditional moral law: *If thou doest well, shalt thou not be accepted? and if thou doest not well, sin lieth at the door.*

Moral agency is therefore presented as deliberative and self-governing, not reflexive. Acceptance before God is not a matter of status, primogeniture or the kind of offering Cain brought, it is a matter of *doing well*. The person is conscious of his inner state, can be addressed about it, and can choose between the well-doing that is accepted and the ill-doing that opens the door to sin.

Evil is not presented as a blind impulse or an internal habitus. It is personified and animalized. The Hebrew is *ḥattā't rāṣṣāh ba-petach*, literally sin is crouching at the entrance. The verb *rāṣṣāh* is used of an animal crouching to spring. Sin is at the threshold, a living, desiring creature. *We’elekha teshuqato we’attah timshal bo* is usually rendered ‘and unto thee shall be his desire, and thou shalt rule over him’. His desire is toward you; sin wants Cain, it is attracted to him, but it is not stronger than him.

That is the point of the second clause. Sin has a desire, but the desire is Cain’s to master. The same word for ‘rule’ *mashal* is the word for dominion given to humanity over creation in Gen 1:26,28. Sin is made subordinate to the moral agent. Cain is not the passive victim of a compulsion that must be discharged, nor is evil an abstract principle in him. It is an externalized, tempting power that crouches at the door waiting to be let in, and the human is the gatekeeper who may rule over it or yield to it.

The responsibility placed on the person is therefore active self-mastery and choice. God does not say sin will master you, but that you can master it. The moral failure will be Cain’s if he lets the crouching creature in. That is why the text immediately follows with the murder of Abel: Cain did not rule over sin, he allowed sin’s desire toward him to become his own desire, and he acted.

In short: agency is free, self-aware volition accountable before God; evil is a personified, beast-like tempter with its own desire toward man, but a tempter that is by divine design subject to man’s rule. The responsibility is to choose to do well and to exercise dominion over the crouching sin rather than be possessed by it.
