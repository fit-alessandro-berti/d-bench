Luke’s saying is **not a denial that moral judgments matter**. It is a warning against a particular posture toward other people: making condemnation our usual way of relating to them, or assuming the right to pronounce the final verdict on their worth and destiny.

The difference is between **discernment** and **the appetite to condemn**.

## 1. The problem is not the ability to recognize wrongdoing

The surrounding context matters. Luke 6:27–36 commands love even toward enemies. Then, after “Judge not, and ye shall not be judged,” Jesus speaks about someone correcting a fellow disciple—and first asks whether the corrector’s own eye is clear (6:41–42). His concern is correction without self-righteousness, not the absence of correction.

The Greek terms for “judge” and “condemn” overlap: “to judge” can itself mean to condemn. So we should not build the distinction on vocabulary alone. **The central distinction is moral and spiritual, not merely verbal.**

Luke is not saying, “Nothing is truly right or wrong, so do not trouble yourself making moral assessments.” That would make love and responsibility practically impossible. We must be able to distinguish justice from injustice, honesty from deception, care from manipulation.

What Jesus refuses is our taking God’s place as the final judge.

## 2. Discernment sees; condemnation settles the account

There is a profound difference between recognizing a fault and *wanting someone destroyed by it*.

| Discernment | The appetite to condemn |
|---|---|
| Seeks to understand what is true and what will serve people’s good. | Seeks reassurance, superiority, leverage, or the satisfaction of being proved right. |
| Distinguishes a person’s worth from a particular failure. | Tries to turn a particular failure into a total verdict on the person. |
| Acknowledges the judge’s own need for mercy. | Uses other people’s faults to establish comparative innocence. |
| Leaves room for confession, repentance, and restoration. | Wants the verdict settled before repentance has a chance to occur. |
| Can produce clear criticism, protection, and correction. | Feeds on contempt, public humiliation, retaliation, or self-exoneration. |

**Discernment is the disciplined exercise of seeing clearly without assuming infinite moral authority.** It can say, “This behavior is harmful, and I will not participate in it.” It can confront, warn, protect a vulnerable person, or refuse to trust someone who has demonstrated untrustworthiness.

**The appetite to condemn is different:** the fault has become more important than the person. The person is no longer someone whose story might be repaired; they are an object of contempt, a threat to our identity, or a prop in our self-justification.

This appetite often disguises itself as zeal for truth. But its characteristic question is not, “What is the truthful and constructive response?” It is, “How can I make the other pay, and make myself feel innocent?” It may also enable strictness that bears no fruit: someone can be “right” while taking no responsibility, showing no humility, and refusing reconciliation.

Strong feelings are not themselves proof that condemnation has taken over. A better question is whether our judgment **seeks the truth and the other person’s good, or feeds our pride at their expense**.

## 3. Moral judgments still matter

To say that moral judgments never matter would undermine the very love Luke commands. If we cannot recognize wrongdoing, we cannot resist it, protect those harmed by it, or help someone turn from it.

Scripture elsewhere assumes discernment rather than forbidding it. Paul instructs believers to correct and restore someone caught in wrongdoing, but specifies gentleness and spiritual maturity (Galatians 6:1–2). Jesus himself corrects error without treating the person as beyond restoration.

The gospel does not make us morally indifferent. It makes our judgments **humble, truthful, and answerable to God’s own judgment of us**. We may say, “This is wrong,” while acknowledging:

- Our own failures do not disappear when we identify another’s.
- Our understanding may be incomplete.
- The person who has offended us is not our final judge.
- Correction is not complete unless it aims at repentance and restoration.

There is a profound irony in refusing to forgive: we acknowledge our need for mercy while imagining that we have no need for others’ mercy. Luke’s warning exposes that contradiction. **The person who wants everyone else condemned may not realize how much he needs forgiveness himself.**

## 4. Forgiveness is not approval, passivity, or unlimited trust

Luke does not say, “Do not forgive because wrongdoing does not matter.” Rather, he commands, “Forgive, and you will be forgiven.”

Forgiveness declines vengeance; it refuses to keep another person permanently in debt to us as an instrument of control. It does **not** require pretending that wrongdoing did not occur, treating the offender as trustworthy before repentance, or remaining where doing so is reckless. It does not abandon justice, legitimate consequences, or the protection of vulnerable people.

A community can confront wrongdoing, seek restitution, and protect someone who has been harmed without embracing hatred toward the offender. Forgiveness concerns the spirit of the response, not the disappearance of discernment or boundaries.

## A practical test

When you judge another person, ask:

1. **Do I want the truth clarified and a good path forward—or do I want leverage and self-vindication?**
2. **Can I name the wrongdoing without declaring the person irredeemable?**
3. **Am I willing to receive correction and mercy myself?**
4. **What will this judgment produce: humility, protection, clarity, and repair—or contempt, division, and retaliation?**

Luke’s command is therefore not “nothing matters,” but **“do not let condemnation take the place of discernment.”** Final judgment matters—but it belongs to Christ. Our task is to see truthfully, love faithfully, and correct without self-righteousness, leaving the final verdict to the mercy of God.
