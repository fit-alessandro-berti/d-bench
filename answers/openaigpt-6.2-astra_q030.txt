Paul uses so many negations because love is tested precisely when we have a reason to envy, boast, flare up, or assume the worst. In Corinth, even spiritual gifts had become occasions for rivalry. To say that love “envieth not” and “vaunteth not itself” addresses both sides of that rivalry: love can welcome another person’s gift without feeling diminished, and exercise its own gift without making anyone else feel small.

The strength proposed here is **the freedom to seek another’s good without needing to prove one’s own importance**. That takes more than restraint. Patience gives someone time to change; kindness asks what one can do for them in the meantime. And because love “rejoiceth in the truth,” hoping the best of someone does not mean ignoring evidence of harm or accepting mistreatment. It means facing what is true without taking pleasure in another’s failure.

On this account, the measure of strength is not whether one can prevail over another person, but whether one can remain truthful and kind when pride or resentment would make that difficult.
