Paul defines victory over evil not as making your enemy suffer, but as refusing to let their wrongdoing determine how you act. If your enemy is hungry, you feed them. The good you do is concrete, and it doesn’t depend on whether they apologize or change.

The “coals of fire” are the difficult image. They may suggest the sting of shame that leads to repentance, or point to judgment left in God’s hands. Either way, the preceding verse says not to take revenge. If you feed your enemy *in order to hurt them*, you’ve turned kindness into a tool of revenge and missed Paul’s point.

The command is **genuinely strong**. It takes self-control to do good when retaliation feels justified. That doesn’t mean pretending the wrong didn’t happen, tolerating abuse, or abandoning justice. It means that even when you must resist someone’s wrongdoing, you don’t have to become like them to prevail.
