The power praised in Philippians 2:5–8 is not the power of domination, control, or self-assertion. It is **kenotic power** — power expressed through self-emptying, self-giving love. The Greek word behind “made himself of no reputation” is *ekenosen*, literally “he emptied himself.” Christ, being in very nature God, did not treat equality with God as something to cling to for advantage. Instead, he poured himself out: into human flesh, into servanthood, into obedience, and finally into the shameful death of a cross.

So this is power **that does not need to grasp, protect, or prove itself**. It is the power of one who is so secure in who he is that he can become small. It is not weakness pretending to be strength; it is strength choosing to serve. It is the power of love that wants the beloved to be free, which is why it refuses to coerce, overwhelm, manipulate, or dominate.

### Why does this humility joined to greatness feel unsettling?

Open domination is, in a strange way, easier to process. If someone is powerful and dominating, the world makes sense: power is being used as we expect. But when greatness humbles itself, our categories break.

- **It upsets our assumptions about power.** We assume power means rising, taking, controlling. A powerful person who voluntarily stoops reveals that humility is not weakness but a deeper form of strength — and that is confronting.
- **It exposes our own grasping.** When God does not need to cling to status, our desperate clinging to reputation, comfort, and control looks small and foolish.
- **It is hard to evade.** A dominating power can be resisted, resented, or violently opposed. But a humble power that serves and suffers for you disarms you. It asks for a response of love, not just submission.
- **It reveals God’s judgment differently.** A God who dominates overwhelms us. A God who humbles Himself to the cross judges our pride simply by being what we are not — and that judgment is harder to face because it is loving rather than coercive.

Humility joined to greatness feels unsettling because it is holy. It does not fit our power games. It calls us not merely to admire it, but to have the same mind in ourselves — to let go, to serve, to love even at our own expense. That is the power of the cross: power that defeats death not by crushing it, but by passing through it and rising beyond it.
