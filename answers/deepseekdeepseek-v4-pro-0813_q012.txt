The passage is better read as **realistic about self-deception** than as merely pessimistic about human nature. It is not saying that every person is as evil as possible or that every motive is always corrupt. It is saying that the human heart is so twisted and self-justifying that **no one can fully know their own motives** — and that therefore the heart is not a safe final witness.

In Hebrew, “heart” means the whole inner life: thoughts, will, desires, intentions. The phrase translated “desperately wicked” can also mean “desperately sick” or “incurably ill” (as in some modern translations). So Jeremiah’s point is not “everyone is monstrous,” but “the heart is diseased beyond self-repair, and it cannot accurately diagnose itself.”

The turn in verse 10 is crucial:  
> “I the Lord search the heart, I try the reins…”

The question “who can know it?” is answered by God, not by human introspection. So the passage is not ultimately hopeless. It lowers our trust in self-knowledge and directs that trust toward God, who alone knows the heart fully and judges justly.

Practically, then, how should someone talk about motives?

- **Speak humbly and provisionally.**  
  Say, “I think my intention was…” or “I wanted to honor God in this,” rather than “I know my heart is pure.”  
  Sincerity is real, but it is not proof of purity.

- **Ask God to search you.**  
  Psalm 139:23–24 is the right prayer:  
  > “Search me, O God, and know my heart: try me, and know my thoughts: and see if there be any wicked way in me.”

- **Let Scripture and wise counsel expose blind spots.**  
  Because the heart is self-deceiving, we need outside witnesses: God’s word, the Spirit, and honest Christian community.

- **Evaluate actions and fruit, not just claimed motives.**  
  Jeremiah 17:10 says God gives to each “according to his ways, and according to the fruit of his doings.”  
  So observable fruit matters, even when hidden motives are mixed.

- **Do not claim to read others’ hearts.**  
  First Corinthians 4:4–5 says the Lord will “bring to light the hidden things of darkness” and “disclose the purposes of the heart.” Final judgment belongs to God, not us.

So the passage is realistic: it treats the heart as an unreliable witness, but it does not leave us in despair. It calls us to stop treating inner certainty as final truth and to stand before God, who alone knows us fully.
