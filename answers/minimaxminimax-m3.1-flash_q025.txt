**The problem is not visibility; it is the motive.** Public visibility is morally neutral. It is the occasion of a temptation, and the temptation is to make being seen the *aim* of the good deed.

### The text's own distinction

Jesus does not forbid giving alms where others can see them. He forbids doing them *with a view to being observed*: the Greek phrase in verse 1 means "in order to be seen by them" (the same construction appears in verse 5 of fasting). "Before men" describes the setting; "to be seen of them" describes the intention.

So the question Jesus raises is not, *Who is watching?* but, *Why am I doing this—whose approval am I after?*

### Why the craving corrupts good

Good deeds naturally draw praise. That makes the desire for praise seductive, because it feels like a legitimate reward for a good act. But when praise becomes the goal, something subtle reverses: the good deed stops being a gift to the needy and becomes a performance for the gallery. The recipient becomes a prop in my self-portrait.

The reward sayings expose this. In verses 2 and 5, Jesus warns that the ostentatious giver or worshiper "has their reward"—they have been paid in the currency they actually sought. Human applause is not *bad* in itself, but it cannot serve as the ground or goal of a good work's worthiness before God. When it does, the work's aim has been misplaced, and the Father's reward is forfeited—not because the act was public, but because its end was self-glorification rather than God-glorification.

### Visibility itself is legitimate

Elsewhere Jesus wants good to be visible: "Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father" (5:16). Public almsgiving may also be necessary for justice, witness, or practical coordination. Concealment is not automatically virtue, and Jesus nowhere promises that a hidden act is a rewarded act. The secretness command in verses 3–4 is a diagnostic of the heart, not a magic condition for divine payment: the point is doing it "before your Father who is in secret," with Him as the audience and the end.

### The heart of the warning

The real danger is not the crowd's eyes but what your heart does with their applause. Public goodness is dangerous precisely because it feeds a craving to be seen *as* righteous—self-congratulation dressed as obedience. And because that craving is woven into human nature, even sincere people can slip into it.

The remedy is not a life of hidden good works but a life of good works whose final reference is God: done for His glory, for the neighbor's good, and free of the need to be admired for them. The question to ask is not only, "Who will see this?" but, "Who am I doing this for—and who gets the glory?"
