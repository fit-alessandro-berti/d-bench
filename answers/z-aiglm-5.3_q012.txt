## Pessimistic or realistic? Mostly a false fork — but in an instructive way

The two readings aren't competing so much as sequential: the passage is pessimistic about *unaided self-knowledge*, and that pessimism is generated precisely by its realism about self-deception. What it is not is a general despair about human nature — and the Hebrew resists that reading.

Two translation notes matter here. "Deceitful" is *aqov* — from the root for "heel," the same root as *Ya'aqov*/Jacob, "the supplanter." The heart is crooked, heel-grabbing, an inside job. And "desperately wicked" is better rendered "desperately sick" or "incurable" (*anush*) — the word Jeremiah uses elsewhere for an incurable *wound* (30:12), in a passage where God then declares He will heal it (30:17). So the claim is not "people are as evil as possible" but "the core of the person is bent in a way it cannot diagnose or cure from within." That's a medical metaphor, and a diagnosis presupposes a physician. Verse 10 supplies the examiner: the rhetorical question "who can know it?" is answered within two lines — no human, not even the owner; only the Searcher.

Context confirms this. The verses just before (17:5–8) contrast the one who trusts in flesh with the one who trusts in the Lord, and the book later promises a rewritten heart (31:33). The incurability of 17:9 is penultimate — it functions as the premise of the cure, not the conclusion of the argument. Verse 9 without verse 10 is just despair; verse 10 reframes the unknowable heart as *known*, which is either terror or relief depending on one's standing before the knower.

## Talking about motives when the witness is compromised

The verse doesn't forbid motive-talk; it disciplines it. Some corollaries the tradition itself draws:

- **Demote self-report from verdict to data.** "I did it because..." is testimony from an interested party. Treat it as one fallible datum, not a ruling. Hence the odd genres scripture develops for motive-speech — confession (honest statement of motives to Someone who can't be fooled) and petition: "Search me, O God, and know my heart" (Ps 139:23). Both move the audit from self to Other.
- **Follow verse 10's own evidentiary logic: ways and fruit.** Motives are better inferred from patterns — what you do repeatedly, under pressure, when it costs, when anonymous — than from introspective snapshots. "By their fruits" (Matt 7:16); "out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaks" (12:34).
- **Assume mixed motives by default.** The heart rarely lies wholesale; it blends. "Pure vs. hypocritical" is the wrong binary; the realistic default is alloy.
- **Read the leaks.** A spin doctor still leaves fingerprints — disproportionate anger, defensiveness, the gap between stated values and one's calendar and wallet. Affect often reports more honestly than the narrator does.
- **Recruit outside readers.** "The purposes of a heart are deep waters, but a person of understanding draws them out" (Prov 20:5). Others occupy angles on us we cannot. Trusted friends, confession, spiritual direction exist partly because self-deception is invisible at exactly the point where it operates.
- **Run the asymmetry: severity toward your own motives, charity toward others'.** Paul applies this verse's logic explicitly: "Do not pronounce judgment before the time... he will disclose the purposes of the heart" (1 Cor 4:5). Name acts, ask questions, defer verdicts on interiors.

Paul even models the right posture toward self-audit: "I do not even judge myself... it is the Lord who judges me" (1 Cor 4:3–4). His conscience is clear, and he notes that this doesn't even acquit him. That's not evasion — it's the relief of not being one's own final court.

It's worth adding that the epistemic half of the verse has aged remarkably well even outside theology. Confabulation studies, choice-blindness experiments, and self-serving bias research all show people confidently reporting reasons that demonstrably weren't causal. What the verse adds is theological: the *direction* of the deceit (self-justifying, not merely noisy) and the *remedy* (an external examiner, not better introspection).

So: pessimistic about self-sufficient self-knowledge — thoroughly. About human beings as such — no. It's a diagnosis with a named physician, in a book that closes the diagnosis with "I will put my law within them, and write it on their hearts."
