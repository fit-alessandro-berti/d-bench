The power praised here is **greatness that does not need to secure itself by dominating**.

Christ’s divine status is the starting point, not the reward for his humility. The striking movement is downward: from “the form of God” to “the form of a servant,” and finally to a degrading death. His greatness makes that descent meaningful. This is not someone without power making a virtue of necessity; it is someone whose power does not prevent him from serving.

The phrase “thought it not robbery to be equal with God” is rendered in many modern translations as not treating equality with God as something to exploit. On that reading, the passage praises **power freed from the need to turn every advantage into personal gain**. Christ’s humility expresses his greatness rather than merely concealing it.

That can feel more unsettling than open domination for several reasons:

- **Domination is legible.** We know its grammar: threat, obedience, resistance. Humble greatness disrupts those expectations. Someone who does not need to intimidate us cannot easily be understood—or managed—through the usual contests of status.
- **It exposes our dependence on recognition.** Beside someone who can relinquish honor without losing themselves, our own need to be admired, vindicated, or placed above others becomes painfully visible.
- **It makes a claim without coercing.** A tyrant can compel compliance, but cannot thereby deserve love. Self-giving greatness may awaken an obligation that feels deeper precisely because it is not enforced.
- **It unsettles our picture of greatness itself.** If the highest can stoop, then service is not necessarily inferiority, and vulnerability is not necessarily defeat.

Paul’s opening—“Let this mind be in you”—makes the passage more than an object of admiration. It asks readers to inhabit that pattern themselves.

But this should not be confused with praising imposed humiliation or telling the vulnerable to accept abuse. **Christ humbles himself; others do not acquire a right to humiliate him.**

The unsettling possibility is that the deepest power may belong not to the one who can make others suffer, but to the one who can surrender advantage without surrendering love or purpose.
