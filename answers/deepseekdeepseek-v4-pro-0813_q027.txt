The short answer is: **the parable condemns self-righteousness**, and the other things you list—pride, comparison, contempt, self-display—are all expressions of that root problem. False doctrine is not the main target, though an implied works-righteousness is present.

Luke gives the point explicitly in the introduction:

> “He spake this parable unto certain which trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and despised others.”  
> — Luke 18:9

So the Pharisee is not condemned simply for being a Pharisee, nor for fasting and tithing. Those practices could have been genuine acts of obedience. What condemns him is that he treats his own religious performance as the basis of his standing before God, and he uses another worshiper as a foil to prove his own superiority.

### What condemns the Pharisee?

**1. Pride / self-trust**  
He is described as one who “trusted in himself that he was righteous.” His prayer is outwardly addressed to God, but it is really self-congratulation. He asks for nothing, confesses nothing, and shows no sense of need.

**2. Comparison**  
He defines his righteousness by contrast with others:

> “God, I thank thee, that I am not as other men are, extortioners, unjust, adulterers, or even as this publican.”  
> — Luke 18:11

His righteousness is not before God alone; it is measured against other people. That comparison is a classic mark of self-righteousness.

**3. Contempt**  
The introduction says he “despised others.” His prayer singles out the tax collector with disdain. His virtue becomes a weapon against his neighbor rather than a response to God.

**4. Virtue as self-display**  
He recites his religious resume:

> “I fast twice in the week, I give tithes of all that I possess.”  
> — Luke 18:12

Again, these are not bad in themselves. But here they are used as self-justification. The prayer is dominated by “I”—what he does, what he is not, what he gives. It is less prayer than performance before God and others.

So the exact condemnation is not merely “false doctrine” in the sense of an intellectual error. It is a false posture of the heart: **self-righteousness expressed as pride, comparison, contempt, and self-display.**

### Why is the tax collector’s posture spiritually truer?

The tax collector has none of the Pharisee’s self-confidence. He stands afar off, will not even lift his eyes to heaven, beats his breast, and says only:

> “God be merciful to me a sinner.”  
> — Luke 18:13

His posture is truer because it corresponds to the reality of the human condition before God. He makes no claims, no comparisons, and no appeal to his own merits. He acknowledges his sin and throws himself entirely on God’s mercy.

Jesus’ verdict is striking:

> “I tell you, this man went down to his house justified rather than the other.”  
> — Luke 18:14

The tax collector is not justified because he was morally better than the Pharisee. He is justified because he took the only posture that can receive justification: **humble, empty-handed trust in God’s mercy**.

That is the point of the parable’s closing principle:

> “Every one that exalteth himself shall be abased; and he that humbleth himself shall be exalted.”  
> — Luke 18:14

In other words: pride, comparison, contempt, and self-display close a person off from grace; humility opens the soul to receive it.
