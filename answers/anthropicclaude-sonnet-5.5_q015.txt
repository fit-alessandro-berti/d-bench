## Why the image is so sharp

**1. Absurd exaggeration.** A *mote* is a speck of sawdust or chaff, and a *beam* is a roof timber. The picture of a man with a plank jutting from his face, earnestly offering to tweezer a speck from someone else's eye, is almost slapstick. Jesus uses comic hyperbole to make the hypocrite's self-deception look ridiculous rather than merely wrong.

**2. Same organ, same kind of fault.** Both obstructions are in the eye, the organ of perception. The issue is not just that the man has a bigger fault, but that his fault ruins his ability to judge faults. The beam distorts his vision, blocks his view, and makes him clumsy and dangerous at the delicate task he has taken on.

**3. The mismatch in attention.** The first question ("why beholdest thou... but considerest not") exposes a failure of proportion. We see others' small faults vividly and our own large ones not at all. The image also hints at projection: we notice most readily in others the very thing we are blind to in ourselves.

**4. The trap of the rhetorical questions.** Jesus asks rather than accuses, so the listener must supply the answer and convict himself. The ironic "behold" in verse 4 mocks the hypocrite's pose of careful scrutiny.

**5. "Hypocrite."** The Greek *hypokritēs* means a stage actor. The man plays the part of a concerned helper, but the performance conceals a self-exalting motive. Offering to help while ignoring one's own need makes the "help" a way of claiming superiority.

## How it criticizes hypocrisy without forbidding correction

**The last sentence is a command to correct.** "First cast out the beam... and *then* shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote." The goal is not that the speck stay in the brother's eye. Jesus assumes it should come out, and the passage ends by restoring the task of correction, now with a precondition.

**The problem is sequence and posture, not the act.** The condemned behavior is correcting while exempting oneself. The remedy is self-examination first, which yields clear sight, humility, and the gentleness needed for work as delicate as eye surgery.

**"Brother" keeps the context relational.** The word suggests correction as care within a community, not as a verdict from above. Compare Matthew 18:15 (go and tell your brother his fault) and Galatians 6:1 (restore the erring "in a spirit of gentleness, considering yourself").

**The wider context requires discernment.** Verse 1 ("Judge not") is followed within a few verses by "Give not that which is holy unto the dogs" (7:6) and "Beware of false prophets... ye shall know them by their fruits" (7:15–20). Jesus clearly expects his followers to make moral evaluations. What he forbids is a self-exempting, condemning kind of judgment, the kind that invites being judged by the same measure (7:2).

## The upshot

The image works because it turns the hypocrite's own eye against him. It doesn't say "never help your brother see." It says you cannot see well enough to help until you have dealt honestly with yourself. Correction survives, but only as something done with humility, self-knowledge, and compassion.
