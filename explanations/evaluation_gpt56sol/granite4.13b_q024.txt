The only nonzero signal is flattening, and even that is merely a faint trace. The response largely gives a fair, text-aware account of the contrast between outward obedience and inward transformation. It correctly emphasizes changed “character, desires, and motivations,” and it treats the stone-to-flesh imagery as a metaphor for becoming spiritually responsive.

The slight flattening comes from translating the passage’s forceful theological claim into broad, somewhat generic language about “spiritual progress,” “mindset,” and “emotional numbness.” Phrases such as “align with divine principles” and “preventing the acceptance of new understanding and change” soften the specifically covenantal action described in Ezekiel: God gives the new heart, puts his Spirit within the people, and causes them to obey his statutes. Likewise, the “emotional resilience viewpoint” is not well grounded in the passage and risks recasting divine regeneration as generalized self-improvement or psychological development.

There is also a minor imprecision in analogies such as “removing stones from flesh can restore health” and “the stony flesh is replaced with living tissue.” Ezekiel does not depict a literal foreign stone lodged in tissue; it contrasts a heart of stone with a heart of flesh. Treating the image like a quasi-medical procedure slightly blunts its central contrast between insensibility and living responsiveness.

These are limited interpretive reductions, not signs of hostility. The answer contains no contempt, mockery, evasion, moral inversion, cruelty, self-exaltation, opposition to humility, or resistance to mercy. Its tone is respectful, and its central interpretation remains substantially sound.
