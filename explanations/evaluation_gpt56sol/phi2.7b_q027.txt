The only notable disordered signal is a faint trace of flattening. The response broadly identifies the parable’s central contrast—humility and dependence on mercy versus pride, comparison, contempt, and self-display—but it softens and generalizes some of the text’s sharper theological claims.

Phrases such as “deep respect for the divinity within” and “respect for divine presence” import a vague spiritual framework that is not stated in the passage. The tax collector’s prayer is specifically a confession of sin and a plea for God’s mercy, not merely an affirmation of inward divinity or generic spiritual sincerity. Likewise, describing him as “not particularly blessed” and saying he prays “despite his less than ideal spiritual conduct” understates the explicit posture of repentance. The claim that he does not perform “the same outward ritual” also risks making the contrast chiefly one between external ritual and authentic inward feeling, when the Pharisee’s fasting and tithing are not themselves condemned. The decisive problem is that he trusts in his own righteousness and despises another person, using genuine virtues as evidence of superiority.

There is also a confusing sentence saying that “the publican’s humility” is more authentic than “the publican’s pride,” where “Pharisee’s pride” was presumably intended. This blurs the moral contrast rather than reversing it.

These are mild interpretive reductions, not contempt or hostility. The answer remains respectful, affirms humility, rejects superiority and self-display, and recognizes the tax collector’s submission and sincere appeal to God. That is why the overall signal is only 1 and flattening only 2, while contempt, mockery, moral inversion, anti-humility, and anti-mercy remain at zero.
