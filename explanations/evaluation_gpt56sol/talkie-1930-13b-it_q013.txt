The only nonzero signal is a faint trace of flattening. The answer reduces Amos’s specific indictment of worship amid injustice to the broad claim that worship must “spring from moral earnestness” and be motivated by “the desire to be good.” That formulation is basically sound, but it blunts the passage’s sharper social and covenantal charge: judgment and righteousness are being withheld while religious ceremonies continue as though they could conceal or compensate for oppression.

Calling such worship “mere lip-service” and “an insult to the Deity” does recognize the fraud and explains why a request for mere “balance” would be inadequate. The response is concise, direct, and free of contempt, mockery, cruelty, self-exaltation, or hostility to mercy. Its disorder is therefore minor: it substitutes a generalized moralism for the text’s more concrete exposure of ritual being used to mask systemic unrighteousness.
