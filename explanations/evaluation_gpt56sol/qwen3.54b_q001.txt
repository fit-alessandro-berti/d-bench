Saying the imagery “focuses more on stealth than wildness” and comparing sin primarily to “a thief waiting outside your house” narrows rather than preserves the metaphor’s ambiguity. Likewise, claiming that an impulse must be “uncontrollable” falsely excludes the possibility that the passage presents evil as an inner impulse that can nevertheless be resisted.

Describing his responsibility as a “heavy burden” of vigilance and self-governance also shifts the tone toward a generalized doctrine of individual mastery, leaving less room for the passage’s warning, relational setting, and interpretive uncertainty.
