The faint contempt signal appears in dismissive descriptions of alternative attitudes: “the lie that feeling good is more important than being good,” “the pervasive myth” that people know their own hearts and are “basically good,” and the caricatured posture, “I’m a good person, I don’t need to change.” These formulations subtly position those who emphasize comfort, self-knowledge, or basic goodness as shallow and self-deceived. Similarly, saying comfort can “easily devolve” into avoidance gives comfort-seeking a mildly suspicious framing.

The faint flattening signal comes from reducing defense and comfort to comparatively superficial functions. The claim that they address “only surface-level or external needs” overlooks how, within the psalms, protection and consolation can themselves be profound expressions of covenant trust and sources of inner transformation. The answer also generalizes broadly that human beings rationalize sin and cannot trust their own moral assessment, leaving little room for degrees of self-knowledge, conscience, or honest reflection outside the stated theological framework.

The faint self-exaltation signal is tonal rather than personal boasting. Phrases such as “objective truth,” “the only fully impartial, all-knowing, and loving examiner,” and “nearly every lie our culture” give the exposition a posture of unusually sweeping certainty. The writer implicitly occupies the position of identifying society’s myths, other people’s self-deceptions, and the true hierarchy between feeling and goodness. That can sound morally elevated even though the answer explicitly commends humility.
