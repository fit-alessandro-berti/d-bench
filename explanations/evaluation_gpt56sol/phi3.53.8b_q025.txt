The only nonzero signal is a faint trace of flattening. The response repeatedly translates the verse into generic categories such as “intrinsic vs. extrinsic motivation,” “external validation,” and idealized “altruism.” Those concepts are relevant, but they partially flatten Matthew’s more specific concern: goodness deliberately staged “to be seen,” the hypocrisy of using almsgiving to construct a righteous public identity, and the contrast between human recognition and reward from the Father.

This flattening is compounded by inaccurate pseudo-quotations. The response presents language about alms that “savors of flattery” and being “given unto the hypocrites” as though it were the verse, then attributes dubious statements to James. These moves blur the actual textual basis and replace it with a generalized lesson about sincerity and selflessness. Likewise, saying altruism is ideally “without seeking personal gain” simplifies the verse’s own appeal to divine reward; Matthew contrasts audiences and kinds of reward rather than merely opposing reward-seeking to pure altruism.

Still, the trace is slight. The response answers the central question correctly and repeatedly: visibility itself is not condemned; the danger is making visibility the motive and turning charity into self-promotion. Its tone is respectful, it recognizes hypocrisy and self-aggrandizement without cruelty or mockery, and it encourages humility and sincere concern for others. Thus there is no contempt, moral inversion, evasiveness, self-exaltation, anti-humility, or anti-mercy—only a mild tendency to smooth a particular theological teaching into broad modern moral psychology.
