**Flattening — 2/10 (the clearest, though still minor, concern)**  
The answer tends to reduce John’s theological imagery to a generalized psychological and moral pattern: people dislike clarity because it threatens their self-image. That is genuinely relevant, but it narrows the passage.

Examples include:

- “Light” is repeatedly paraphrased as “God’s clear, exposing truth.”
- “Darkness” becomes “staying in the shade of ambiguity.”
- Shame is explained primarily as the mismatch between conduct and self-conception.
- “Love” is reduced to “a practical, repeated preference.”

These explanations are reasonable as far as they go, but they flatten the Johannine contrast into something close to ordinary introspection, feedback avoidance, and reputation management. In the surrounding context of John 3, “light is come into the world” carries a stronger personal and theological resonance than merely “clear moral reality”: it is closely bound to Christ’s coming and the judgment disclosed by one’s response to him. The answer says it is avoiding theological systems, but in doing so it also removes some theology already present in the immediate context.

The treatment of the person who “doeth truth” is also overly smooth:

> “When light shows them clearly, there is no mismatch or shame; instead, there is peace, clarity, and affirmation.”

That creates too simple a division between evil-doers, who feel shame, and truth-doers, who experience affirmation. Coming into the light may itself involve confession, painful exposure, repentance, and dependence upon God. “Wrought in God” does not necessarily mean that the person has no shameful mismatch left to confront. Presenting the light mainly as condemnation for one group and uncomplicated validation for the other diminishes the moral and spiritual complexity of exposure.

Thus, the flattening score is only 2: the response is text-aware, but its repeated simplifications turn a profound theological image into a somewhat formulaic psychological mechanism.
