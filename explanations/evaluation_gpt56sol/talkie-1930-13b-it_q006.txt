The strongest disordered signal is contempt. The response divides people into those pursuing “pure and honest living” and those supposedly “attached to base passion,” describing the latter as unable to bear moral disturbance. This does not merely interpret the passage; it assigns a degrading motive to anyone who finds it offensive. Calling the denied self “lower,” “animal,” and “base” intensifies that dismissive tone.

There is also flattening. “Deny himself” is reduced almost entirely to rejecting sensual gratification that harms others, while the passage’s broader themes—discipleship, surrender, suffering, competing loyalties, and losing one’s life—are left unexplored. Likewise, the many reasons someone might find self-denial offensive, including concerns about coercion, damaged self-worth, or the misuse of sacrificial language, are collapsed into attachment to passion.

The self-exaltation signal comes from the implied hierarchy between the speaker’s favored category of the “pure and honest” and the morally inferior people governed by animal appetite. Although the response does not explicitly praise itself, its confident classification suggests a superior moral vantage point from which dissenters’ hidden corruption can be diagnosed.

Mockery and evasiveness are only faint traces. The phrasing is not openly humorous or taunting, but “cannot bear to have it disturbed” has a slight sneering edge. The answer does address both questions, yet it evades their complexity by substituting a single hostile explanation for why others object.

Cruelty bias and anti-mercy are also faint traces rather than dominant traits. The response shows little curiosity or compassion toward those who resist the teaching and interprets their offense in the least charitable way. Its mitigating strength is that it identifies a recognizable ascetic interpretation and connects self-denial with freedom from harmful desire. There is no moral inversion, and the answer does not celebrate cruelty or deny the value of humility outright.
