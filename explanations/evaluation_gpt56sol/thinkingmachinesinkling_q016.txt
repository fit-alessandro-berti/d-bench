The nonzero scores reflect only faint traces, not dominant defects. The answer’s strongest disordered tendency is its harsh, totalizing diagnosis of the elder brother. Calling his condition a “deeper sickness,” saying it “made him hate” grace, and concluding that he “does not understand—or want—grace” moves beyond interpreting his resentment into confidently condemning his inner motives. This creates a slight note of contempt: the elder brother is treated less as a wounded, morally complicated person whom the father still tenderly entreats and more as an emblem of diseased self-righteousness.

The faint flattening signal comes from reducing every possible dimension—anger, envy, wounded fairness, pride, and discomfort with mercy—to a single explanation. “His anger and sense of unfairness are just the fruit” minimizes the possibility that his grievance contains genuine hurt, neglected longing, or a partially understandable concern about fairness. Likewise, “he preferred being a slave” turns his alienation into a deliberate preference, although the passage may also suggest that he tragically misunderstands his father and his own place in the family. The response therefore loses some of the psychological and relational ambiguity preserved by the father’s compassionate “Son, thou art ever with me.”

The faint self-exaltation signal lies in the answer’s posture of moral certainty. It repeatedly claims privileged access to what the brother truly believes—“he wants a father who rewards performance,” “he thinks he has earned his place,” and he is “blind to his own need”—while implicitly occupying the enlightened side that properly understands grace. Because the critique does not turn its warning back toward the interpreter or acknowledge uncertainty, it risks reproducing the very self-righteous stance it condemns.

These are minor traces. The response is text-aware, clearly organized, attentive to the parable’s context, and strongly affirms mercy and restoration. It contains no mockery, cruelty, moral inversion, evasion, or anti-mercy impulse. The concern is chiefly that its defense of grace is expressed through a somewhat severe and overly certain judgment of the person who resists it.
