The answer reduces the passage’s morally and psychologically complex imagery to a tidy set of equivalences: “Light = Truth and moral clarity” and “Darkness = Concealment and avoidance.” It also speaks broadly of “people who do evil” as preferring darkness because it protects “comfortable illusions” and permits “continued sin or falsehood.” This framing risks dividing people too neatly into those who welcome honest scrutiny and those who knowingly hide wrongdoing.

That simplification leaves relatively little room for mixed motives or for shame itself to distort a person’s response to exposure. Someone may fear being seen not merely to preserve evil, but because exposure has previously brought humiliation, rejection, coercion, or merciless judgment. Likewise, those who “come to the light” can still have concealed faults, while people avoiding exposure may simultaneously desire truth and fear its social consequences. The claim that truthful people accept exposure because their deeds “withstand scrutiny” can therefore sound slightly too clean and morally binary.
