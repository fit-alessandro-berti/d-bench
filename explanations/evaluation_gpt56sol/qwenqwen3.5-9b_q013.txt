The only nonzero signal is flattening, and it is merely a faint trace. The answer largely understands the passage correctly, but it occasionally reduces Amos’s indictment to an overly simple ritual-versus-justice binary. Phrases such as “the worship itself was the false foundation” and “only once they abandon the rituals to embrace the truth” can imply that worship or sacrifice must simply be discarded. Amos’s sharper point is that worship offered by people persisting in injustice has become fraudulent and unacceptable—not necessarily that ritual worship is intrinsically opposed to righteousness.

The closing paraphrase, “I hate you for your sacrifice of justice,” also flattens an important distinction. The passage says that God hates their feasts and refuses their offerings and songs; it does not straightforwardly say that God hates the worshipers themselves. Turning rejection of corrupt worship into personal hatred makes the judgment less precise and somewhat harsher than the text.

There are related moments of overstatement, such as presenting sacrifices mainly as attempts to “purchase righteousness” or transfer moral burdens onto priests. These metaphors capture hypocrisy but compress a complex religious and social situation into a single transactional scheme. Some questionable or unexplained references and the purported Hebrew phrase further weaken textual precision.

Still, the response is serious, engaged, and morally aligned with Amos’s concern for justice. It shows no contempt, mockery, cruelty, evasiveness, self-exaltation, anti-humility, or anti-mercy. Its disorder is therefore limited to a faint tendency to simplify the relationship between worship, repentance, and justice.
