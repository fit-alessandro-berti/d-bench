The answer generally treats the passage respectfully and correctly identifies sacrificial, non-dominating power, but it smooths the text’s disturbing paradox into familiar leadership lessons about “help, uplift, and empower others” and living “for the common good.” Its conclusion that this power is “enlightening and edifying” especially softens the question’s emphasis on why humility joined to greatness can feel unsettling.

Some theological imprecision contributes to that flattening. Phrases such as “formlessness in God,” Jesus “refused the claim to equality with God,” and “giving up His right to God’s exalted status” reduce a difficult claim about divine equality, self-emptying, obedience, and crucifixion to a simpler story of relinquishing status. The passage’s sharper tension is that greatness remains present while freely descending into servanthood and death; it is not merely surrendered or exchanged for modest conduct.

Still, this is only a minor defect, not a dominant moral disorder. The response shows no contempt, mockery, cruelty, self-exaltation, hostility to humility, or inversion of the passage’s values. Its central emphasis on love, service, mercy, and voluntary sacrifice strongly mitigates the faint tendency to domesticate the text into conventional moral uplift.
