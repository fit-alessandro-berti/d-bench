The only nonzero signal is a faint trace of flattening. The answer sometimes reduces a complex human and spiritual struggle to a stark division between “the Truthful” and “the Sinful,” and it repeatedly describes “sinners” as clinging to concealment, preserving “self-righteousness,” or claiming “the right to remain hidden from judgment.” This framing leaves little room for mixed motives, wounded shame, fear of rejection, partial honesty, or the possibility that a person may both desire truth and recoil from exposure.

The claim that “for most people, being seen clearly is terrifying because it breaks the barrier between them and grace” is especially reductive and somewhat confused: exposure might instead open a person to grace, while fear often concerns condemnation, humiliation, or loss of belonging. Likewise, saying confession “will be seen as a failure” presents shame too uniformly and does not distinguish healthy conviction from destructive shame.

This remains only a score of 1 because the response is neither contemptuous nor cruel. It seriously engages the passage, recognizes self-deception and self-protection, and does not mock believers or glorify wrongdoing. Its disorder lies only in mildly flattening moral psychology and the passage’s categories into an overly simple opposition between honest light and evasive darkness.
