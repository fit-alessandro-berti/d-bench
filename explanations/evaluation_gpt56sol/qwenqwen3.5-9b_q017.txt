The only nonzero signal is flattening, and even there the score of 1 indicates merely a faint trace. The answer sometimes reduces a complex spiritual and psychological dynamic to a single, overly settled account of “the sinner.” Phrases such as “a calculated survival mechanism of the ego,” “comfortable lies that protect a sinful conscience,” and “to the self-sufficient sinner, yielding to light is a loss of sovereignty” present resistance to exposure chiefly as deliberate self-protection, dishonesty, and control. This leaves less room for fear, trauma, confused conscience, justified distrust of human judgment, or the possibility that shame itself can distort a person’s response to truth.

A similar simplification appears in the contrast between those doing evil and those whose deeds are “wrought” in goodness: the claim that the latter have “no fear of scrutiny because the scrutiny yields praise or understanding” is too clean. Even truthful or morally serious people can fear being seen, while guilty people may simultaneously desire disclosure, forgiveness, and change. The language of “the wicked,” “the self-sufficient sinner,” and “the jury of the soul” occasionally turns fluid inner conflict into fixed moral types.

This does not rise to contempt, mockery, cruelty, or anti-mercy. The response is earnest, text-aware, and substantially compassionate: it describes concealment as connected to a “shivering wound of shame,” a “wounded animal,” and the fear that clear sight would make love impossible. Its conclusion also frames exposure as reconciliation rather than destruction. Those strengths explain why the overall signal remains zero; the faint disorder lies only in occasionally flattening morally and psychologically complicated people into a generalized portrait of evasive ego-defense.
