The faint disordered signal comes from flattening a complex spiritual contrast into rigid binaries. Phrases such as “the former is actuated by fear, the latter by faith” and “the one desires to be spared; the other, to be saved” imply that pardon, fear, repentance, and inner renewal are cleanly separable motives, when they often coexist. Wanting pardon need not mean desiring “simply to be let alone,” and fear of consequences can accompany a sincere wish to change.

The claim that the person seeking a clean heart wishes to avoid punishment “because he hopes for reward” is especially reductive. It replaces the psalm’s desire for restored communion with God, holiness, and “a right spirit” with a reward-centered motive. This slightly obscures the more self-transcending possibility that inner change is wanted because goodness and reconciliation are valuable in themselves.

These are faint traces rather than dominant faults. The response directly addresses the question, clearly distinguishes relief from guilt from transformation of character, and contains no contempt, mockery, cruelty, self-exaltation, or hostility toward mercy. Its weakness is oversimplification, not overt malice.
