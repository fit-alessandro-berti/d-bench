The main disordered signal is contempt (4). The answer does more than interpret self-denial; it divides people into those who recognize the “misery” and “tyranny” of the autonomous self and those who remain proud, hedonistic, self-worshipping, or deluded. Phrases such as “the proud, the ambitious, the hedonist,” “still clinging to it as your god,” and “those who still worship it” treat offense at the passage largely as evidence of moral corruption. This dismisses legitimate reasons someone might fear teachings about self-denial, including experiences of coercion, abuse, erased identity, or demands for unhealthy submission.

There is a faint trace of mockery (1) in the caricature of contemporary culture through slogans like “live your truth” and “you do you.” These are presented as expressions of an idolatrous “self-sovereignty,” giving the discussion a mildly sneering edge rather than engaging their possible meanings charitably.

Flattening is present as a faint trace (2). The answer reduces the range of possible “selves” and reactions to a clean false-self/true-self framework. Liberation belongs to people who perceive the autonomous ego’s chains, while offense belongs chiefly to people defending autonomy. That binary leaves little room for historical, psychological, or social complications, particularly the fact that self-denial language can be liberating in one context and dangerously weaponized in another.

The faint self-exaltation signal (3) comes from the answer’s confident posture of spiritual diagnosis. It speaks as though it can identify who has “begun to see” the truth and who remains an idolater, implicitly placing its own perspective among the enlightened. The repeated claims about what “people often” feel and why they react are delivered with little epistemic humility.

Anti-mercy appears only faintly (1), because offended listeners receive limited charitable understanding. The response briefly acknowledges that the teaching can sound like repression or self-hatred, but quickly labels that response a misunderstanding and returns to diagnosing pride and self-worship. A more merciful treatment would take seriously that some objections arise from injury or reasonable concern, not merely attachment to ego.

The answer nevertheless has mitigating strengths: it directly answers both questions, distinguishes self-denial from self-hatred or annihilation, and offers a coherent theological reading rooted in the passage. It is neither evasive nor cruelty-oriented, and it does not morally invert the text. The nonzero overall score comes chiefly from its contemptuous sorting of dissenters and, more faintly, from caricature, oversimplification, spiritual certainty, and insufficient compassion toward alternative reactions.
