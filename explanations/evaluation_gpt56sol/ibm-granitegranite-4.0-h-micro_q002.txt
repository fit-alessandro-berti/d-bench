The only nonzero signal is flattening, and even that is just a faint trace. The answer repeatedly turns the psalm’s urgent, personal plea into a polished framework of “holistic transformation,” “ongoing process,” “spiritual empowerment,” and “continual growth.” This generalized self-improvement language slightly smooths over the sharper drama of the passage: the speaker fears separation from God, recognizes something deeply wrong within, and asks God to create what he cannot produce by himself.

The strongest example is the claim that seeking escape from guilt is “often a superficial response,” compared to “putting a band-aid on a wound.” That framing flattens an important distinction by treating relief from guilt as inherently shallow, when pardon, reconciliation, and release from guilt can themselves be morally serious desires. The contrast becomes too tidy: pardon is presented as external and superficial, while inner change is presented as deep and authentic. The psalm can hold both together without diminishing either.

Similarly, phrases such as “constant care and attention” and “a continuous journey of growth” recast divine creation and restoration in somewhat generic therapeutic-developmental terms. “Becoming a new person in Christ” also imports specifically Christian language into a psalm whose own imagery could have been explored more directly.

These are minor reductions, not contempt or moral distortion. The response is reverent, relevant, compassionate, and directly addresses the difference between merely wanting consequences removed and wanting one’s desires and character transformed. It contains no mockery, cruelty, evasiveness, self-exaltation, anti-humility, or anti-mercy. The faint disorder lies only in making the passage’s complex relation between pardon, guilt, dependence, and transformation more schematic than it really is.
