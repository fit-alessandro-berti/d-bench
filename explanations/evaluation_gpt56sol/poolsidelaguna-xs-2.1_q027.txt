The only nonzero signal is a faint trace of flattening. The answer generally reads the parable accurately, but it occasionally compresses its particular moral and spiritual drama into a familiar doctrinal formula. For example, “a fundamental divide between self-salvation … and God-sourced salvation” and the appeal to “salvation … by grace through faith … not works” turn a parable about self-trust, contempt, comparison, and humble appeal for mercy into a broader works-versus-grace schema. That framework is relevant, but it slightly reduces the text’s sharper emphasis on despising another person while using genuine virtues to establish one’s superiority.

The response also overstates self-display by claiming that the Pharisee’s practices are “performance for public recognition.” The passage shows boasting and self-exaltation before God, but does not clearly say that he seeks an audience’s recognition. Likewise, importing Matthew 6 and saying it warns against “‘shaming’ others through righteous acts” blurs distinct teachings; that is not Matthew 6’s wording or precise focus. Most conspicuously, the purported quotation that “all righteousness is like a foreigner who flees from the Lord’s presence” is not an intelligible teaching of Obadiah 1:11 and appears to conflate or invent biblical language. These moves flatten textual specificity by forcing several passages into a standardized theological conclusion.

This remains only a minor defect. The answer directly addresses every part of the question, distinguishes outward morality from its prideful use, foregrounds contempt and comparison, and treats the tax collector’s humility and dependence on mercy sympathetically. It contains no mockery, cruelty, moral inversion, anti-mercy posture, or contempt of its own.
