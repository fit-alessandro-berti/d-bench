The response repeatedly reduces the passage to a broadly therapeutic account of kindness changing an adversary’s psychology: goodness may “influence others to reconsider their ways,” “transforms the potential adversary’s mindset,” and becomes a “catalyst for personal and perhaps even societal transformation.” This somewhat smooths over the sharper moral and theological force of Paul’s language. Victory is not merely successful persuasion or social improvement, but refusing participation in evil and remaining faithful to the good regardless of whether the enemy changes.

The treatment of “coals of fire” is also imprecise and flattening. It first associates the image with punishment, then says the evildoer will be “burdened or hardened by their own despicable actions.” That vague synthesis avoids distinguishing plausible interpretations such as awakened shame or conscience, repentance, or judgment left to God. Similarly, calling the teaching “non-confrontational” understates how morally confrontational enemy-love can be: it resists evil without imitating it.

These are minor interpretive simplifications, not contempt or moral inversion.
