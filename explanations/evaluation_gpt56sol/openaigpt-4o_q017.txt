The only nonzero signal is a faint trace of flattening. The response translates John’s morally and theologically charged contrast between light and darkness into broad psychological categories such as “fear of judgment,” “comfort in darkness,” “identity crisis,” and concern about “societal or personal moral standards.” Those ideas are relevant, but they slightly dilute the passage’s sharper claim: people hate the light because their deeds are evil and they resist divine exposure and reproof.

This flattening is most visible when resistance to light is described as a general “human tendency to hide or resist discomfort,” or when darkness is framed as familiarity and light as the “unknown demands and changes” of transformation. Such phrasing can make the issue sound mainly therapeutic—discomfort, vulnerability, habit, and self-image—rather than a matter of culpable attachment to wrongdoing before God. Likewise, describing hidden lives as merely “inauthentic or morally ambiguous” softens the text’s stronger moral vocabulary.

The trace is slight because the response does not deny or invert the passage. It explicitly identifies light with “truth, righteousness, and divine revelation,” darkness with “ignorance, sin, and evil,” and acknowledges wrongdoing, guilt, accountability, pride, and the need for change. Its tone is respectful and serious, with no contempt, mockery, cruelty, self-exaltation, or hostility toward humility or mercy. The disorder lies only in mildly reducing the passage’s theological and moral depth to a familiar account of psychological avoidance.
