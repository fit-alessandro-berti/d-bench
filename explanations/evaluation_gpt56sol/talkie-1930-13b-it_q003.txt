The strongest problem is the answer’s moral inversion and flattening. Its claim that justice “prompts to invade the rights of another” reverses the ordinary meaning of justice: justice protects and renders what is due to others rather than violating their rights. Likewise, describing mercy merely as prompting one “to spare” those rights makes mercy sound like restraint from injustice, instead of compassionate generosity beyond strict desert.

This also flattens the verse’s three virtues into an inaccurate opposition between justice and mercy, followed by a narrow account of humility as modest self-estimation. It misses how they correct one another: justice prevents mercy from becoming indulgence or favoritism; mercy prevents justice from becoming cold or punitive; humility restrains self-righteous confidence in applying either. The response therefore fails to explain why their simultaneous demands are difficult.
