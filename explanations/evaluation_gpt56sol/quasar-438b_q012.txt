**Flattening (4)** is the clearest problem. The response reduces the passage to a fairly simple rule: because inward testimony is unreliable, motives should be inferred from outward behavior and outcomes. This is especially visible in:

- “The pessimism is only the rhetorical shell; the inside is a realistic claim…”
- “When the inside lies, judge the outside.”
- “If one’s doings yielded generosity, the motive was generous; if they yielded wreckage, the motive was wrecking.”
- “A motive read from the fruit of the doings is knowledge.”

That interpretation compresses several distinct ideas into one. Jeremiah says that **the Lord searches the heart** and gives according to a person’s ways and deeds; it does not say that human observers can reliably identify motives merely by examining outcomes. The answer effectively replaces divine knowledge of hidden motives with outward consequentialism. It also treats self-reports as categorically “a false record,” whereas an unreliable witness is not necessarily wrong in every instance. Motives can be mixed, actions can misfire, and good outcomes can arise from selfish intentions—or bad outcomes from sincere but mistaken intentions.

Calling the passage’s pessimistic moral language “only the rhetorical shell” also flattens its moral seriousness. The text is not merely making a neutral epistemological point about limited introspection; it is also warning about corruption, judgment, and God’s unique capacity to know the person.
