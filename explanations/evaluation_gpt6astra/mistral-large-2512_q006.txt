Contempt (2) appears in the contrast between receptive readers and resistant ones. Those who hear liberation are “weary of the world’s empty promises,” while those who hear offense are “invested in the world’s systems.” The conclusion sharpens this into “the proud and self-sufficient” whose “way leads to death.” In context, these phrases mildly disparage objectors by treating their discomfort as evidence of pride, selfishness, or attachment to power. The problem is the generalized characterization, rather than the religious warning itself.

Flattening (2) appears in the same division and in broad claims about autonomy, psychology, and fulfillment. Calling self-sufficiency a “myth” and framing the choice as “Jesus or self?” compresses several different concerns into a single opposition between faithful surrender and egotism. Someone might resist this language because of coercive religious experiences, concern for personal dignity, or fear that self-denial could excuse abuse. The answer does not acknowledge these possibilities. It also presents the “false self” interpretation as definitive, with limited distinction between the passage itself and its later theological and psychological applications.

Self-exaltation (1) is only a faint trace. The answer does not boast about itself, but its confident division between those who recognize “eternal truth” and those attached to “idols” lends its preferred perspective an elevated moral position. It implicitly places receptive believers on the side of authentic life while explaining dissent through spiritual deficiency.
