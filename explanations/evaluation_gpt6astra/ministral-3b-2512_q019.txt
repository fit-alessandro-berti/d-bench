The central contradiction is blessing God while cursing people made in God’s likeness: professed reverence for God conflicts with mistreatment of those who bear his image. The answer recognizes this but shifts toward a generic opposition between “divine potential” and “sinful tendencies.” Its claim that “blessing and cursing cannot coexist in the same heart” also blurs the distinction between what actually happens and what morally ought to happen. James exposes their coexistence and condemns it; he does not deny its possibility.

The flattening also appears in confident diagnoses of motives. “If we curse others, we are revealing that we hate them ... even if we don’t mean to” collapses differences between settled hatred, momentary anger, habitual language, and intention. Meanwhile, “when we bless, we may reveal self-righteousness or hypocrisy” introduces suspicion of praise without evidence that this explains the particular contradiction. Although “may” qualifies that claim, the overall framing makes speech seem like an uncomplicated disclosure of someone’s “true nature.”
