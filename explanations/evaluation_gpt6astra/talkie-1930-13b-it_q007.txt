The main issue is flattening (4): the answer reduces a morally complex distinction to a rigid opposition between intellect and emotion. “Discernment neither praises nor dispraises” makes discernment sound like neutral observation, obscuring its ability to recognize wrongdoing, assign responsibility, and guide protective action without becoming an appetite to condemn. Likewise, “the former has to do with truth, the latter with feeling” overlooks that moral feeling can be truthful and compassionate, while cold reasoning can serve punitive ends. The claim that condemnation “can be exercised towards others only” also excludes self-condemnation without justification.

The opening assertion, “it forbids them,” compounds this flattening by treating the verse as a blanket prohibition on moral judgments. It does not adequately explain how rejecting condemnation can coexist with judgments that matter. The passage’s emphasis on forgiveness offers a way to explore that distinction, but the answer leaves it undeveloped.
