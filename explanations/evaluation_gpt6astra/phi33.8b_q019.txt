Flattening (4) appears in the replacement of James’s specific contradiction with general observations about speech. The contradiction is that people bless God while cursing human beings made in God’s likeness: their treatment of those who bear that likeness contradicts their professed reverence for God. The answer instead centers on speech as a “double-edged tool,” our tendency to “turn towards negativity,” and choosing “what divides us rather than unites.” These formulations retain a broad lesson about kindness but weaken the connection between honoring God and honoring people.

The claim that bearing God’s image means “we bear some of His imperfections” further muddles that connection. The problem is not simply theological unorthodoxy: the answer introduces a claim unsupported by the passage and inconsistent with its own descriptions of God as “perfect and wise” and “always just.” Saying that we “often choose to reflect His ‘curse’ rather than His ‘blessing’” also shifts attention from the contradiction James exposes toward a speculative account of imitating divine powers. Similarly, the unsupported assertion about Greek words having “connotations of producing outcomes by divine intention” gives an appearance of textual precision without clarifying the actual argument.
