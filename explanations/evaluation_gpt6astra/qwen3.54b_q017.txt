It is the way its explanation occasionally reduces the complex relationship between wrongdoing, shame, and exposure to a rigid division between deceptive people and transparent people.

The clearest example is: “They may feel they are good, but the light reveals the actual reality: that they are not.” This moves from exposing wrongful deeds to declaring the person bad. Similarly, “the crushing reality of moral defeat” frames exposure as a total defeat of the self, leaving little room for shame that is disproportionate, fear of humiliation, mixed motives, or the possibility that acknowledging wrongdoing begins a process of repair.

The opposite side is simplified too. The answer says that for someone whose deeds are “wrought in God,” “the fear of exposure does not exist because nothing is hidden,” and that exposure is “validation.” This makes truthfulness sound like complete moral integration and fearlessness. A person can sincerely seek truth while still feeling shame, fearing others’ judgment, or needing correction. Coming to the light need not mean having only affirming things revealed.

The formulas “Evil = False” and “Good = Transparency,” along with “good deeds need no light,” further compress the passage into categorical equations. They blur distinctions between truth, visibility, moral goodness, and the willingness to have one’s actions examined. This is the limited disordered tendency captured by the nonzero score: simplifying moral vulnerability into concealment versus vindication.

The answer nevertheless engages the question directly, recognizes self-deception, and presents being known as requiring courage.
