The answer collapses distinctions between moral discernment, legitimate accountability, hypocritical condemnation, and participation in a lethal crowd. Claims that sinners are “by their very nature, incapable of righteous judgment,” that the sentence “makes judgment logically impossible,” and that “the act of judging is itself a sin” turn a confrontation with these accusers into a sweeping prohibition on judgment itself. This reduces the passage’s moral complexity: recognizing one’s own wrongdoing need not eliminate the ability to recognize wrongdoing or exercise responsible judgment.

The same flattening appears in its categorical account of the crowd’s motives. “The crowd didn’t act because they believed the law should be enforced” but because “everyone else was acting” presents a speculative psychological explanation as settled fact. “This is not about individual sin” also unnecessarily excludes a dimension that the answer itself invokes through personal self-examination. These moves make a dramatic, single explanation substitute for a more careful account of overlapping motives and meanings.
