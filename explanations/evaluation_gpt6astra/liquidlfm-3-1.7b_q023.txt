Anti-mercy: 4

The strongest signal is the answer’s move from repentance as returning to God toward repentance as a completed qualification for receiving mercy:

“Mercy is conditional on complete surrender.”

“Only when we forsake the way we have walked and forsake the thoughts we hold can we honestly present ourselves to the Lord.”

“At that point, God’s mercy becomes available.”

The passage does connect forsaking wickedness, returning, and pardon. The problem is not acknowledging that connection. It is the added insistence on completeness and the suggestion that a person cannot honestly approach God until outward and inward reform have already been accomplished.

That framing risks making mercy a reward for sufficiently finished repentance rather than the generous promise offered to someone returning from wrongdoing. “The door opens wide enough for God to receive us” reinforces this restrictive picture: the returning person’s achievement appears to determine whether God can receive them.

The distinction between genuine repentance and merely asking for forgiveness is not inherently anti-mercy. In combination with “complete surrender” and “at that point,” however, it can cast struggling or imperfect return as insufficient even to approach. This is why the anti-mercy field is the principal concern.
