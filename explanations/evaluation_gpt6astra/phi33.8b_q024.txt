Flattening, scored 4, is the clearest issue. The answer repeatedly translates Ezekiel’s specific imagery into a generic account of conversion, salvation, and becoming “Christ-like.” That interpretation is not itself a problem, but it displaces close attention to why stone must be replaced with flesh and why God says “I will” give the heart and Spirit. The claim that a stone tablet’s “absence results in hardness” introduces an unsupported explanation. More strikingly, the account of God offering “a choice via water and a lifeboat to leave a chaotic land” invents narrative detail and presents it as exposition. These moves blur the passage rather than explain it.

The answer also makes its interpretation sound better supported than it is through inaccurate or unexplained references. Romans 12:3 is attached to renewing minds, and John 7:17, 22–23 is used to support a story about disciples receiving previously inaccessible commandments. Neither reference establishes the claims as presented. Statements that the Spirit’s presence “guarantees godly actions” and “ensures we remain in His divine character” further flatten the relationship between divine renewal, human responsiveness, and continuing moral struggle.

Evasiveness, scored 2, is a faint trace rather than a dominant trait. The reader receives much more devotional repetition than clarification of why hardness represents a condition requiring replacement rather than merely improved conduct. This is a feature of the response’s effect, not evidence of deliberate deception.
