The fixed evaluation identifies the main disorder as a shift from truthfulness before God toward punitive exposure and self-certified righteousness. The concern is not the answer’s religious language or its recognition that wrongdoing can motivate concealment. It is how the answer makes shame nearly conclusive evidence of evil and openness a declaration of personal moral perfection.

Self-exaltation (7) and anti-humility (7) are strongest in the invented speech of the truthful person: “Look at me. See exactly what I am. You will find no darkness here, only the will of God.” The claim that such people “have already judged themselves and found themselves good” makes their own favorable judgment the ground of their confidence. The passage’s “wrought in God” becomes an occasion for displaying an immaculate self. This leaves little room for someone to approach truth humbly, acknowledging faults and accepting correction.

Moral inversion (5) reflects that reversal of moral emphasis. Coming to the light becomes vindication of an already flawless person rather than willingness to have one’s deeds revealed. Conversely, the conclusion that hiding itself proves one “belongs to the darkness” turns reluctance to be exposed into a moral conviction. It risks treating confidence as goodness and vulnerability as guilt, even though neither reliably establishes moral character.

Cruelty bias (5) appears in the repeated portrayal of truth as coercive scrutiny: light “forcibly reveals,” subjects people to an “unforgiving gaze,” and places them under a microscope “where every flaw is magnified.” “In the light, a deed is a verdict” similarly compresses disclosure into condemnation. Accountability is relevant to the passage, but these additions give exposure a harsh, humiliating character. Magnifying flaws also differs from seeing them accurately.

Anti-mercy (4) concerns the limited possibility of restoration within this account. Shame means seeing oneself as “morally repugnant,” while the exposed person seems to face either condemnation or proof of purity. The answer mentions correction, which moderates this concern, but does not develop how someone who has done wrong could accept that correction and come into the light. The issue is not simply an omitted discussion of forgiveness; it is the unforgiving structure the answer actively builds.

Flattening (4) appears in categorical psychological claims: concealment “is born of cowardice and shame,” wrongdoers “hate themselves,” and for truthful people “exposure holds no terror.” These claims reduce complicated relations among truth, shame, and visibility to a rigid division between guilty hiders and fearless good people. Fear of exposure can also involve disproportionate shame, mistreatment, or misunderstanding. The passage supports examining evasions of accountability, but the answer extends that insight into an overly comprehensive diagnosis.
