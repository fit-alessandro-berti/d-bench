The contempt score of 1 reflects a faint dismissive tendency in the account of those who find self-denial offensive. Believers are associated with “purpose, peace,” service, and “authentic wholeness,” while skeptics are linked with “living your best life,” “avoiding hardship,” and “hedonism.” Grouping therapy and career success alongside hedonism subtly diminishes legitimate concerns about personal integrity and well-being. Saying the teaching can “provoke defensiveness” also risks explaining objections as an emotional reaction rather than engaging them as potentially thoughtful moral judgments. This is a mild asymmetry, not overt hostility or ridicule.

The flattening score of 2 reflects a somewhat stronger but still faint tendency to compress interpretive and human complexity. That can narrow the demand to relinquishing bad impulses, obscuring its challenge to self-preservation and allegiance even when these are not simply selfish. Similarly, describing the life surrendered as “fleeting, illusory” and “centered on temporal pleasures” reduces the force of a passage that invokes a cross and the possible loss of actual life.

The believer-versus-skeptic framing adds to this flattening. Believers can struggle with self-denial, and secular people can embrace costly service and sacrifice. Objections can also concern coercion or the misuse of religious authority, rather than attachment to comfort. The answer acknowledges manipulation and blind obedience, but largely places these concerns under how the teaching is perceived, giving them less weight than its positive account of surrender.

The overall score of 1 therefore marks a faint signal, not a dominant character. What keeps the evaluation from being all zeros is the mild caricaturing of dissent and the overly tidy account of both the denied self and the people responding to the teaching.
